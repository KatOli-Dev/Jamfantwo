## The Moment Before Memory

The elven myth of the First Awakening is the most fundamental of the elven traditions, the story that the elves tell about the moment when they became aware of themselves and of the world around them. The myth is not a narrative of events in the manner of the human creation stories; it is a description of a state of consciousness, a memory of the first awareness that the elves carry within them and that they are able to recall through the disciplines of meditation and reflection. The First Awakening is the beginning of elven history, and it is the standard against which the elves measure all that has come after.

The myth is preserved in its fullest form in the Sylvan Court of the great forests of Aethelar, though every elven community maintains some version of the teaching. The elves of Sahrana, adapted to the warm climate of the northern coast, preserve a version that emphasises the heat and the light of the first morning. The elves of Morvind, who dwell in the sheltered valleys of the cold interior, preserve a version that emphasises the slow awakening through the long winter night. The variations among the elven peoples reflect the conditions of their homelands, but the core of the myth is the same wherever the elves tell it.

## Beneath the Heartwood

The Heartwood is the great tree that the elves believe stands at the centre of the world, and the First Awakening occurred beneath its branches. The Heartwood is not a tree in the ordinary sense; it is the tree that contains the pattern of all trees, the original form from which all the forests of the world derive their nature. The elves do not say that the Heartwood created the world or that the world grew from it; they say that the Heartwood was already present when the world began, and that it will remain when the world ends, and that it is the place of the first awareness.

The elves describe the Heartwood as vast beyond measure, its trunk so broad that a hundred elves standing with their arms outstretched could not encircle it, its branches extending so far that the outermost leaves catch the light of the sun at dawn and the light of the stars at midnight. The roots of the Heartwood extend into the deepest places of the world, and the leaves of the Heartwood brush against the realm of the sky, and the tree is the axis around which the world turns. The First Awakening occurred in the shadow of this tree, and the memory of that shadow is the memory that the elves cherish above all others.

## The First Awareness

The elves did not awaken gradually, the tradition teaches. They came to awareness in a single moment, a moment that the elven sages describe as the opening of an eye that had always been closed. The first elves were aware of themselves and of each other and of the Heartwood and of the world beyond the Heartwood's shadow simultaneously, and the awareness was complete and perfect and did not need to grow or develop. The elves knew in that first moment everything that they would later learn through experience, for the knowledge was present in the awareness itself and needed only to be unfolded over the course of a lifetime.

The first emotion that the elves experienced was wonder, the tradition says, and the first sound that they made was the word for wonder, which in the elven language is the same word as the word for the Heartwood. The elves say that wonder is the natural response of the awakened consciousness to the world, and that the loss of wonder is the first sign of the forgetting that comes with age and with the accumulation of experience. The elven disciplines of meditation and reflection are designed in part to recover the original wonder of the First Awakening, and the elves who master these disciplines are said to be able to recall the moment of awakening as clearly as if it had happened that morning.

## The World Into Which They Awoke

The world into which the elves awoke was not empty. The other peoples were already present, the plants and the animals and the beasts that walk and fly and swim, but none of them possessed the awareness that the elves possessed. The elves were the first to be conscious of themselves and of the world, and this consciousness set them apart from the other inhabitants of the world and gave them a responsibility that the other peoples did not share.

The elven tradition teaches that the other peoples were not inferior to the elves, for each kind of being has its own nature and its own perfection, and the creatures that lack conscious awareness are not incomplete in the way that a broken tool is incomplete. The other peoples were complete in themselves, the elves believe, and the awakening of the elves did not raise them above the other peoples but gave them a duty to care for the world that the other peoples could not perform. The elves were the first watchers, the first keepers of the world, and the First Awakening was the beginning of that duty.

## The Memory of the Awakening

The elves have carried the memory of the First Awakening through all the generations that have followed, and the memory is the foundation of elven identity. The elves do not forget as the other peoples forget; their memories are long and their traditions are preserved with a fidelity that the shorter-lived peoples find difficult to understand. The memory of the First Awakening is not a memory of events but a memory of a state of being, and the elves are able to recall that state through the disciplines of meditation that the elven sages have developed over the ages.

The discipline of the First Recall is the principal elven meditation, and it is taught to every elven child as part of the coming of age. The practitioner sits in silence and stillness and allows the mind to settle, layer by layer, until the accumulated experience of a lifetime no longer obscures the original awareness. The discipline can take years to master, and many elves never achieve the full recall, but even a partial success brings the practitioner closer to the state of the first awakening and renews the sense of wonder that the elves regard as the highest good.

## The Debate Among the Elven Scholars

The elven scholars are not agreed upon the interpretation of the First Awakening. The orthodox tradition holds that the awakening was a literal event that occurred at a specific time and place, beneath the Heartwood at the centre of the world, and that the memory of the awakening has been transmitted faithfully through the generations. The reformist tradition holds that the awakening is a metaphor for the emergence of consciousness, that it occurred gradually over a long period, and that the story of the Heartwood is a symbolic account of a process that cannot be described in literal terms.

A third tradition, preserved among the elves of the Sylvan Court, holds that the question of whether the awakening was literal or metaphorical is itself a misunderstanding of the nature of the myth. The awakening is not an event that happened in the past, the Sylvan Court teaches; it is a reality that continues in the present, and every elf who achieves the First Recall participates in the awakening anew. The past and the present are not separate in the elven understanding of time, and the First Awakening is as real now as it was when the first elves opened their eyes beneath the Heartwood.

## The Relation to the Other Creation Myths

The elven myth of the First Awakening does not conflict with the creation myths of the other peoples, for the elves do not claim that their awakening was the creation of the world. The world existed before the elves awoke, the tradition teaches, and the other peoples and the plants and the animals were already present. The elven awakening was the beginning of conscious awareness in the world, but it was not the beginning of the world itself, and the elves acknowledge that the other peoples may have their own beginnings that are equally valid from their own perspectives.

The elves regard the dwarven Stone Song with particular respect, for the dwarven myth describes the shaping of the world from the stone in terms that the elves recognise as true to their own understanding of the world's material nature. The elves do not accept the dwarven account as literally true, but they acknowledge that the Stone Song expresses a truth about the world that the elven myths do not capture. The relation between the elven and the dwarven traditions is a relation of complement rather than of conflict, and the scholars of the Sylvan Court and the stone-singers of the Stoneheim Holds have maintained a correspondence for many generations.
