## The Voice That Shaped the World

The myth of the Song That Raises Mountains is preserved in the traditions of the dwarven peoples and is closely related to the Stone Song, but it is a distinct myth that emphasises the active power of the song rather than the passive resonance that the Stone Song describes. The myth tells of a time when the world was flat and featureless, when the stone lay in a level plain that extended in every direction without variation, and of the song that raised the mountains and created the valleys and the caverns and the passages that the dwarves now inhabit.

The myth is preserved in the deep halls of the Karhold Realm and the Stoneheim Holds, and it is told by the stone-singers to the apprentices who are learning the craft of listening to the stone. The myth describes the first awakening of the song, the moment when the voice of the mountain began to sound and the world began to take the shape that it now possesses. The myth is not a story of a single event that happened in the distant past; it is a description of a process that continues in the present, and the stone-singers who shape the stone are continuing the work that the song began.

## The Flat World and the Silent Stone

The myth begins with a description of the world as it was before the song first sounded. The stone was present, the myth says, but it was without form and without feature, a level plain that extended in every direction without any variation of height or depth or texture. The stone was silent, for the song had not yet been awakened within it, and the silence was the silence of the potential that had not yet been realised.

The stone was not dead, the myth teaches, for the stone contains within itself the possibility of all the forms that it may later take. The stone was waiting, and the waiting was the condition of the world before the song. The silence was the necessary precondition for the song, and the flatness was the necessary precondition for the raising of the mountains.

The dwarves who hear this description recognise the state of the stone before the shaping, for the dwarves encounter this state in their own work. The block of stone that the carver takes from the quarry is the same as the world before the song: it contains the potential of all the forms that the carver may later shape, but it is silent and featureless until the carver begins to work. The myth teaches the dwarves that the work of the carver is a participation in the original shaping, and that the song that the carver hears within the stone is the same song that raised the mountains.

## The First Sounding

The first sounding of the song is described in the myth as a moment of great power and great beauty. The voice of the mountain began to sound, the myth says, and the sound was not a single note but a chord that contained within itself all the notes that would later be sounded. The chord resonated through the stone, and the stone began to change, and the flat plain began to rise and fall in response to the resonance.

The mountains rose where the sound was strongest, the myth says, and the valleys were formed where the sound was weaker, and the caverns and the passages were shaped by the patterns of the resonance that the song created. The song did not shape the stone by force; it shaped the stone by invitation, by the power of the resonance that awoke the potential that lay within the stone and that caused the stone to take the form that it had always contained within itself.

The myth describes the first sounding as the moment of origin not only for the mountains but for the dwarves themselves, for the dwarves were present in the stone when the song sounded, and the song awakened them as well. The dwarves are the part of the stone that became conscious, the myth teaches, and the awakening of the dwarves was the same moment as the raising of the mountains, and the two cannot be separated.

## The Singer and the Song

The myth does not identify a singer who produced the first sounding, for the dwarven tradition holds that the song is not the product of a singer who is separate from the song. The song is itself the singer, the dwarves say, and the mountains are the voice, and the distinction between the singer and the song and the stone is a distinction that the human mind makes but that does not correspond to the reality of the world.

The dwarven stone-singers who attempt to understand the nature of the first sounding say that the question of who sang is the wrong question, and that the correct question is what the song was. The song was the pattern of the world, the resonance that the stone was capable of producing, and the first sounding was the expression of this pattern in the material of the world. The song was not sung by any being; the song was the being, and the mountains and the dwarves and the caverns and the metals are the forms that the song took when it sounded in the stone.

## The Continuing Song

The myth of the Song That Raises Mountains does not describe a process that happened once and then ended, for the song continues to sound in the stone, and the mountains continue to rise and the valleys continue to deepen and the caverns continue to expand. The world is not a finished creation; it is a creation that continues, and the song that raises the mountains is the same song that the stone-singers hear in their work and that the dwarven carvers follow in their shaping of the stone.

The dwarves believe that the song is still raising mountains, and the evidence that they point to is the growth of the great halls that the dwarves carve from the stone. The halls are not static structures that the dwarves build and then leave; they are living spaces that continue to change and to grow, and the dwarves who work in the halls are participating in the continuing raising of the mountains.

## The Variations Among the Traditions

The myth of the Song That Raises Mountains is preserved in different forms among the dwarven peoples of the different continents. The dwarves of the Karhold Realm, who inhabit the oldest and the deepest of the dwarven halls, emphasise the aspect of the myth that describes the depths and the foundations of the mountains. The dwarves of the Stoneheim Holds, who inhabit the halls that are closest to the surface, emphasise the aspect of the myth that describes the raising of the peaks and the shaping of the visible world.

The dwarves of Sahrana, who are few in number and who have adapted their traditions to the desert, preserve a version of the myth that describes the raising of the sandstone mesas and the shaping of the canyons of the desert interior. The Sahrani dwarves say that the song sounds differently in the desert stone than it does in the northern mountains, and that the forms that the song raises in the desert are the forms that the desert requires.

## The Relation to the Centaur Sky Dance

The dwarven scholars who have studied the traditions of the other peoples have noted the similarity between the Song That Raises Mountains and the centaur Sky Dance, and they have suggested that the two myths may be describing the same reality from different perspectives. The dwarven myth describes the shaping of the world through sound and resonance, and the centaur myth describes the shaping of the world through movement and pattern, and the dwarves and the centaurs have acknowledged that the sound and the movement may be two aspects of the same process.

The dwarves and the centaurs do not claim that their myths are the same, but they recognise the complementarity of their traditions, and the scholars of the two peoples have exchanged their understandings of the myths for many generations. The exchange has enriched both traditions, and the dwarves who have studied the centaur Sky Dance report that they can hear the dance in the song, and the centaurs who have studied the dwarven Stone Song report that they can feel the song in the dance.
