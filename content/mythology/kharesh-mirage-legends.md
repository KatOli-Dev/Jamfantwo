## The Dancers of Light

The mirages of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert are among the most famous phenomena of the natural world, and the legends that surround them are among the richest traditions of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) peoples. The mirages are not merely optical illusions, the legends teach; they are manifestations of the desert's memory, visions of places and times that exist beyond the reach of ordinary perception. The mirages reveal what the desert wishes to reveal, the nomads of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) say, and the ability to read the mirages is a skill that the experienced desert travellers cultivate over a lifetime.

The legends of the mirages are preserved by the Kharesh Tribes and the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), and they are told by the caravan masters and the desert guides who have spent their lives traversing the sands. The legends distinguish between several different kinds of mirages, each with its own nature and its own meaning. The common mirages, which show pools of water and groves of palm trees that vanish as the traveller approaches, are regarded as the least significant of the phenomena, the desert's ordinary play of light and heat. The true mirages, the ones that the legends concern themselves with, are the visions that appear only to the worthy and that reveal truths that the desert otherwise conceals.

## The Mirror of the Past

The first and most common of the true mirages is the Mirror of the Past, a vision that shows the desert as it was in the ancient times, before the waters retreated and the sands advanced. The Mirror of the Past appears most often at dawn and at dusk, when the light is low and the heat of the day has not yet dissolved the clarity of the air. The vision shows a landscape of rivers and lakes and forests, of great cities and flourishing fields, of the world that existed before the Kharesh became a desert.

The Mirror of the Past is regarded as a gift by the desert peoples, a reminder that the harsh conditions of the present are not the only reality and that the desert was not always the place of hardship that it is now. The vision is bittersweet, the legend says, for it shows a world that can never be recovered, but it also carries the promise that the desert is capable of change and that the future may be different from the present.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have debated whether the Mirror of the Past mirages preserve a genuine memory of the ancient landscape or whether they are purely psychological phenomena, projections of the human mind that reflect the stories that the travellers have heard about the desert's past. The debate is unresolved, but the travellers who have seen the Mirror of the Past describe the vision in terms that are remarkably consistent, and the details of the landscape that they describe correspond to the evidence of the rock of the desert's ancient climate.

## The Veil of the Future

The second of the true mirages is the Veil of the Future, a vision that shows events that have not yet occurred and that warns the traveller of dangers that lie ahead. The Veil of the Future is the most dangerous of the mirages, the legends caution, for the vision can be misleading and the traveller who trusts the vision too readily may walk into the very danger that the vision was meant to warn against.

The Veil of the Future appears only to those who are in genuine need, the legends say, and it appears only when the need is urgent. The vision shows the traveller a path through the desert that leads to safety, or it shows the location of a hidden water source, or it reveals the approach of a sandstorm that the traveller cannot otherwise detect. The vision is always correct in its details, the legends say, but the interpretation of the vision is the responsibility of the traveller, and the traveller who misinterprets the vision may suffer the consequences.

The caravan masters of the Kharesh Tribes tell stories of travellers who saw the Veil of the Future and who ignored the vision, believing themselves to know the desert better than the vision did, and who perished as a result. The stories are told as cautionary tales, and the caravan masters teach their apprentices to trust the visions that the desert sends, even when the visions contradict the knowledge that the traveller has acquired through experience.

## The Gate of the Other

The third and rarest of the true mirages is the Gate of the Other, a vision that opens a doorway into a world that exists alongside the ordinary world but that is not ordinarily accessible to human perception. The Gate of the Other appears as a shimmering in the air, a distortion of the light that reveals a landscape that is not the landscape of the desert, a place of green fields and flowing water and cities built of stone that gleams with an inner light.

The Gate of the Other is the subject of the most elaborate of the mirage legends, and the stories that surround it are the most disputed among the scholars. Some of the legends describe the Other as the realm of the spirits, the place where the hidden folk dwell and where the souls of the ancestors reside. Other legends describe the Other as the world as it would have been if the desert had not claimed it, the potential that was lost when the waters retreated. Still other legends describe the Other as the true world, of which the desert is merely a shadow, and the Gate of the Other as the doorway through which the worthy may pass to a better existence.

The nomads of the Kharesh Tribes speak of the Gate of the Other with great caution, for the legends say that those who pass through the gate may not be able to return. The travellers who have seen the Gate of the Other describe a longing to enter it, a pull that is almost irresistible, and the legends caution that the longing is a test of the traveller's attachment to the world. The traveller who resists the longing and who turns away from the gate is rewarded with a clear path and a safe journey, while the traveller who yields to the longing may be lost forever.

## The Mirage Reader

The ability to read the mirages is a skill that the desert peoples cultivate with great care, and the mirage readers are among the most respected members of the Kharesh Tribes. The mirage reader is trained from childhood to distinguish between the ordinary mirages and the true mirages, and to interpret the visions that the true mirages present. The training is long and demanding, and the mirage reader who cannot read the visions accurately may lead the caravan to disaster.

The mirage reader's art is not a science in the manner that the scholars of the Cloisters understand science, and the mirage reader cannot explain how the visions are produced or why they appear to some travellers and not to others. The mirage reader works by intuition and experience, by the accumulated knowledge of generations of readers who have observed the mirages and who have passed their observations down through the families of readers. The art is a tradition rather than a technique, and the mirage reader who attempts to reduce the art to a set of rules will find that the mirages are not amenable to rules.

## The Meaning of the Mirage

The legends of the mirages are the desert's own mythology, the stories that the desert peoples have developed to make sense of the strange and beautiful phenomena that appear in the air above the sands. The mirages are not merely natural phenomena, the legends teach; they are the desert's way of communicating with the people who cross it, a language of light and heat that the travellers must learn to read if they are to survive the journey.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have studied the mirage legends with great interest, and they have documented the different types of mirages and the interpretations that the desert peoples have developed. The scholars acknowledge that the mirages are not supernatural phenomena, but they also acknowledge that the desert peoples' understanding of the mirages is a legitimate form of knowledge, a way of making sense of the world that is different from the scholarly approach but that is equally valid within its own terms.
