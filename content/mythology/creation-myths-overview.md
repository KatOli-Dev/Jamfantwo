## The Many Beginnings of the World

The peoples of the known world have never agreed upon a single account of how the world began. Each culture, each species, and each religious tradition preserves its own story of the first things, and these stories differ from one another in ways that reflect the conditions of the lands in which they arose and the temperaments of the peoples who tell them. The creation myths of the world range from the precise detail of the dwarven accounts to the luminous veils of the [Sunless Veils](/content/religion/sunless-veils) tradition, and the scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have devoted considerable effort to the collection and comparison of these narratives without ever attempting to reconcile them into a single coherent account, for the Cloisters hold that the diversity of the creation stories is itself a truth about the world that no single story could capture.

The creation myths are not merely stories about the past; they are the foundations upon which the cultures of the present rest. The way a people understands the origins of the world shapes the way that people understands its own place in the world, its duties toward the other peoples, and its relation to the divine or the natural order. The myths are therefore studied not only for their own sake but as keys to the understanding of the cultures that preserve them.

## The Dwarven Account: The Mountain's Song

The dwarven creation myth, known as the Stone Song, holds that the world was shaped from the primordial stone by the singing of the mountains. According to the tradition preserved in the deep halls of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm), there was in the beginning only the stone, infinite and silent, and the mountains sang within the stone, and the song formed the caverns and the passages and the veins of metal that run through the earth. The dwarves themselves were shaped from the living stone by the same song, and they were set in the deep places of the world to hear the song and to honour it by their crafting.

The Stone Song is not a story of a creator who stands apart from the creation. The mountains themselves are the singers, and the song is not a command uttered by a divine will but the natural resonance of the stone that has always been and will always be. The dwarves do not pray to the mountains in the manner that the human religions pray to their deities; they listen to the stone, and they shape it in accordance with the song that they hear within it. The dwarven stone-singers, the master carvers who are said to be able to hear the song more clearly than others, are the most revered members of dwarven society, and their work is regarded as a participation in the continuing creation of the world.

## The Elven Account: The Awakening Beneath the Heartwood

The elven creation myth tells of the first awakening beneath the Heartwood, the great tree that the elves believe stands at the centre of the world. According to the tradition preserved in the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and the deep forests of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), the elves were not created by any deity or shaped by any external power; they awoke, fully formed, beneath the branches of the Heartwood, and they knew themselves and each other from the first moment of awareness. The world into which they awoke was already old, and the other peoples and the beasts and the plants were already present, but the elves were the first of the peoples to possess the gift of conscious awareness, and they have carried the memory of that first awakening ever since.

The elven account does not explain the origin of the Heartwood or of the world itself; the elves do not possess a story that reaches further back than the awakening, and their scholars regard the question of what existed before the Heartwood as a question that cannot be answered and perhaps should not be asked. The awakening is the beginning of meaning for the elves, and they do not require a beginning for the world in order to understand their place within it.

## The Human Accounts: The Wandering Peoples

The human creation myths are more varied than those of any other species, for the human peoples have spread across every continent and adopted every manner of life, and their myths have changed with their circumstances. The oldest and most widespread of the human origin stories holds that the humans arrived from across the sea, that they were not native to the lands they now inhabit but came from a place that no longer exists, a homeland that was lost or destroyed or that simply faded from the world when the humans departed from it.

The account of [the Wandering Peoples](/content/mythology/human-wandering-peoples) is preserved in different forms among the human realms of Aethelar, [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). The northern human kingdoms of [Nordhaven](/content/location/settlement/city/nordhaven) and the [Karhold](/content/location/settlement/city/karhold) Realm tell of a great voyage across the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), led by the first navigators who followed the stars to a new land. The southern human cities of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) and the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) tell of a journey across the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), a migration that took generations and that left settlements scattered across the islands of the warm seas. The human peoples of Sahrana tell of a crossing of the great desert that was once a sea, a journey through the burning sands that claimed many lives before the survivors reached the fertile coast.

The common thread that runs through all these accounts is the sense of loss and the sense of arrival. The humans remember that they came from somewhere else, and they remember that the journey was hard, and they remember that the land they found was not the land they had left. The memory of the journey has shaped the human character, the scholars of the Houses of Memory say, and it accounts for the restlessness and the adaptability that distinguish the human peoples from the other species of the world.

## The Centaur Account: The Sky Dance

The centaur creation myth, known as the Sky Dance, holds that the world was formed by the movement of the constellations across the night sky. According to the tradition preserved among the centaur herds of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), the great constellations that wheel above the world are the first dancers, and their movement through the darkness traced the patterns that became the mountains and the rivers and the plains. The world is the imprint of the dance, and the dance continues still, and the centaurs honour the dance by their own movements across the plains.

The Sky Dance does not separate the creator from the creation in the manner of the human religions. The constellations are not gods who made the world and then withdrew from it; they are the world's continuing movement, and the centaurs participate in that movement by their own migrations across the plains. The great festivals of the centaur year are timed to the positions of the constellations, and the dances that the centaurs perform at these festivals are regarded as a continuation of the first dance that formed the world.

## The Halfling Accounts: The Hidden Folk

The halfling creation stories are less concerned with the origins of the world than with the origins of the hidden folk, the spirits and the small people that the halflings believe inhabit the fields and the forests and the streams. According to the tradition preserved among the halfling communities of the [Emberlands](/content/location/settlement/region/emberlands) and the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale), the world was created by the laughter of the first halfling, whose joy at the beauty of the empty world called forth the plants and the animals and the other peoples. The hidden folk were the first to respond to the laughter, and they have remained close to the halflings ever since, sharing the bounty of the fields and the secrets of the growing things.

The halfling accounts are the most cheerful of the creation myths, and they reflect the temperament of a people who have learned to find contentment in the small pleasures of the daily round. The halflings do not concern themselves with the grand questions of cosmic origins; they are content to know that the world is good, that the hidden folk are kind to those who treat them with respect, and that the harvest will come again in its season.

## The Sahrani Accounts: The Gifts of the Sun and the Sea

The creation myths of Sahrana are shaped by the harsh conditions of the continent, and they tell of a world that was created through sacrifice and struggle rather than through joy or song. The traditions of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) and the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) describe a world that was formed from the body of the first sun, which burned itself to nothing in order to provide light and warmth to the cold and empty world. The sun that now shines is the child of the first sun, weaker and smaller, and the day will come when it too will burn itself away, and the world will return to the cold and the dark from which it emerged.

The coastal peoples of Sahrana preserve a different account, one that centres upon [the Sea Mother's gift of salt](/content/mythology/sea-mothers-gift-of-salt). According to this tradition, the world was created when the Sea Mother wept into the empty basin of the world, and her tears became the salt seas, and the salt that she gave to the people of the coast was the first gift of life, the substance that preserves food and heals wounds and binds the communities of the shore together. The salt is the Sea Mother's continuing presence among her people, and the caravans that carry salt from the coast to the interior are regarded as carrying a sacred substance.

## The Shared Themes

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have identified several themes that recur across the creation myths of the known world. The first is the theme of movement: the dwarven song, the centaur dance, and the human journey all describe the world as the result of something moving through something else, as though creation were less an act of making than an act of passage. The second theme is the theme of loss: most of the myths describe a past that is irretrievably gone, a golden age or a homeland or a first sun that cannot be recovered. The third theme is the theme of participation: in most of the myths, the people are not merely the products of creation but participants in its continuation.

The creation myths of the world are not in conflict, the scholars of the Cloisters argue, because they are not about the same thing. The dwarven myth is about the nature of substance; the elven myth is about the nature of awareness; the human myths are about the nature of journey and change; the centaur myth is about the nature of pattern; the halfling myths are about the nature of joy. Each myth answers a different question, and the world is larger than any single question that could be asked about it.
