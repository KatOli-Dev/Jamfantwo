## The Walkers in the Snow

The Frostwall Clans of northern Morvind and the northern reaches of Aethelar preserve a rich tradition of stories about the spirits that walk the snow during the long winter months. The winter spirits are not gods in the manner of the deities of the southern religions; they are beings of the cold and the dark, the forces that the northern peoples must contend with during the season when the sun is low and the days are short and the world seems to hold its breath. The stories of the winter spirits are told during the winter itself, when the families gather around the hearth and the wind howls outside and the snow piles high against the walls.

The tradition of the winter spirits is shared among the Frostwall Clans, the Karhold Realm, and the northern settlements of the Nordhaven kingdom, and the stories vary from community to community even as they share common elements. The spirits are divided into categories according to their nature and their behaviour: the dangerous spirits that must be appeased or avoided, the mischievous spirits that play tricks on the unwary, and the benevolent spirits that help those who are lost in the snow.

## The Ice Walker

The most feared of the winter spirits is the Ice Walker, a figure that appears in the stories of every northern community and that is described in terms that are remarkably consistent across the different traditions. The Ice Walker is a tall and gaunt figure, the stories say, clad in white that blends with the snow, with eyes that are as pale as the winter sky and fingers that are as cold as the ice itself. The Ice Walker appears during the worst of the winter storms, when the wind is strongest and the snow is thickest and the cold is most intense.

The Ice Walker does not attack those it meets, the stories say, but the mere presence of the Ice Walker is a danger, for the cold that radiates from its body can freeze the blood in the veins of the living and the breath in the lungs of the unwary. The Ice Walker is the embodiment of the winter itself, the stories teach, and the only defence against it is the warmth of the hearth and the company of other living beings. Those who are caught alone in the snow when the Ice Walker appears are said to be lost, their bodies found in the spring with expressions of peace on their faces, for the Ice Walker takes its victims without violence and without struggle.

The Frostwall Clans maintain the custom of lighting a fire at the crossroads during the worst of the winter storms, a fire that is kept burning through the night as an offering to the Ice Walker and as a guide for those who might be lost in the snow. The custom is ancient, and the clans observe it faithfully, for they say that the Ice Walker respects the hearth and will not approach a place where the fire burns.

## The Frost Maidens

The Frost Maidens are among the more ambiguous of the winter spirits, beings of great beauty and great danger who appear in the stories of the northern settlements. The Frost Maidens are described as women of pale and luminous beauty, clad in garments of frost and ice, with hair that streams in the wind like the northern lights. The Frost Maidens appear on the nights when the aurora is brightest, the stories say, and they dance across the snow in patterns that mirror the movements of the lights above.

The Frost Maidens are not malevolent in the manner of the Ice Walker, but they are dangerous nonetheless, for their beauty can lure the unwary into the snow where they will perish from the cold. The stories tell of hunters and travellers who saw the Frost Maidens dancing and who followed them, captivated by their beauty, until they were lost in the wilderness and never seen again. The Frost Maidens do not intend to harm those who follow them, the stories say; they are simply indifferent to the fate of mortals, and the beauty of their dance is the only thing that matters to them.

The northern peoples regard the Frost Maidens with a mixture of awe and caution, and they teach their children to look away if they see the lights dancing in the sky and figures moving beneath them. The Frost Maidens are a reminder that beauty can be as dangerous as ugliness, and that the winter is a time when the usual rules of the world do not apply.

## The Hearth Wights

The Hearth Wights are the most benevolent of the winter spirits, and they are the figures that the northern peoples regard with the most affection. The Hearth Wights are small and shy beings, the stories say, who dwell in the spaces between the walls and beneath the floorboards of the northern houses. The Hearth Wights emerge during the winter nights, when the family is gathered around the fire, and they perform small services for the household: they mend the torn cloth, they sharpen the dull blades, they stack the firewood, and they chase away the mice and the other pests that seek shelter from the cold.

The Hearth Wights are easily offended, the stories caution, and they must be treated with respect if they are to remain in the house. The family must leave a small offering of food and drink at the hearth each night, and the offering must be fresh and of good quality, for the Hearth Wights will not accept stale bread or sour milk. The family must also refrain from speaking ill of the Hearth Wights or from attempting to see them, for the Wights are shy and will leave the house if they feel that they have been discovered.

The stories of the Hearth Wights are the most cheerful of the winter spirit tales, and they are told to the children as a way of teaching them the importance of hospitality and gratitude. The Hearth Wights are a reminder that even in the darkest and coldest of the winter nights, the household is not alone, and the spirits that share the house can be a source of comfort and help if they are treated with proper respect.

## The Winter King

The Winter King is the most powerful of the winter spirits, the ruler of the cold and the snow and the long darkness, and the stories of the Winter King are the most solemn of the Frostwall traditions. The Winter King is described as a figure of great age and great power, older than the mountains and the seas, and his domain extends from the northern ice to the edge of the settled lands. The Winter King is not evil in the manner that the southern religions understand evil; he is simply the embodiment of the winter, and the winter is a natural part of the world that must be endured and respected.

The Winter King is said to ride across the sky during the longest night of the year, leading the host of the winter spirits in a great hunt that marks the turning of the season. The hunt is a sign that the winter is at its deepest, the stories say, and that the return of the light is beginning. The northern peoples mark the longest night with great fires and feasts, and they stay awake through the night to welcome the return of the sun and to show the Winter King that they are not afraid.

## The Wisdom of the Winter Spirits

The traditions of the winter spirits are not merely stories that the northern peoples tell to pass the long winter nights. The traditions contain the accumulated wisdom of the Frostwall Clans and the other northern peoples, the knowledge of how to survive the winter and how to maintain the community through the season of hardship. The spirits are the forces that the northern peoples must contend with, and the stories are the means by which the knowledge of these forces is transmitted from generation to generation.

The scholars of the Houses of Memory who have collected the winter spirit traditions have observed that the stories serve a practical purpose in addition to their cultural and spiritual significance. The stories teach the dangers of the winter, the signs that a storm is coming, the places where the snow is deepest, and the methods of finding shelter and warmth. The stories are a form of practical knowledge that is encoded in narrative form, and the scholars acknowledge that the northern peoples could not have survived the winters without this knowledge.
