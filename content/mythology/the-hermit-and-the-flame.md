## The Solitary Keeper

The parable of the Hermit and the Flame is one of the central teaching stories of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame), and it is told in the hearth-halls throughout the lands where the faith is observed. The parable tells of a hermit who lived alone in the mountains and who kept a fire burning through the longest and the coldest winter that the world had ever known. The hermit's fire was the only light in the darkness, and the hermit's determination to keep the flame alive became the means by which the world was saved from the cold.

The parable is not a myth in the sense of the great creation stories; it is a teaching story, a narrative that the hearth-keepers use to instruct the faithful in the virtues that the Still Flame cultivates. The parable teaches the importance of the daily discipline of tending the fire, the duty of hospitality even when the resources are scarce, and the truth that the smallest flame, if it is kept alive, can become the fire that lights the world.

## The Hermit's Choice

The parable begins with the hermit, a man who had chosen to live apart from the communities of the lowlands and who had built his hut in a high valley where the winter came early and stayed late. The hermit was not a holy man in the manner that the temples understand holiness; he was simply a person who preferred solitude and who had found the discipline of the solitary life suited to his temperament. The hermit kept a fire on his hearth, as the Still Flame teaches, and the fire was his companion and his comfort and the sign of his participation in the one Flame that underlies all fires.

The winter that came upon the world was unlike any winter that had been known before, the parable says. The cold descended from the north and settled upon the land, and the snow fell without ceasing, and the sun was hidden behind the clouds for days and then weeks and then months. The communities of the lowlands suffered terribly, and many perished from the cold and the hunger that the winter brought. The hermit in his high valley suffered as well, for the snow was deeper than he had ever seen and the cold was more intense, but he had prepared for the winter as the hermits of the mountains learn to prepare, and he had stores of fuel and food that he had laid in during the summer.

## The Tending of the Fire

The hermit's discipline of tending the fire became the central act of his life during [the long winter](/content/history/the-long-winter), the parable says. The fire was not merely a source of warmth and light; it was the focus of the hermit's attention and the means by which he maintained his connection to the world beyond his hut. The hermit rose each morning before the dawn and kindled the fire, using the embers that he had banked the night before, and he spoke the words of the morning kindling that the Still Flame teaches. The fire burned through the day, and the hermit tended it, adding fuel as needed and adjusting the draught and the position of the logs, and in the evening he banked the fire for the night and spoke the words of the evening banking.

The discipline of the fire was the hermit's prayer, the parable teaches, and the fire itself was the hermit's scripture. The hermit did not need the hearth-hall or the company of the faithful to maintain the practice of the Still Flame, for the flame that burned on his hearth was the same flame that burned on every hearth, and the tending of the fire was itself the observance of the faith. The hermit's solitude was not a separation from the faith but a different manner of practising it, and the discipline of the solitary tending was as valid as the discipline of the communal gathering.

## The Coming of the Stranger

The turning point of the parable comes with the arrival of a stranger at the hermit's door, a traveller who had been lost in the snow and who had found the hermit's hut by the light of the fire that shone through the window. The stranger was near death from the cold, and the hermit took him in and set him by the fire and gave him warm food and drink and dried clothing. The stranger recovered over the course of several days, and the hermit and the stranger shared the fire and the food and the silence of the winter.

The stranger asked the hermit why he kept the fire burning when the world seemed to be dying from the cold, and the hermit answered that the fire was the flame of the world and that it must be kept alive regardless of the conditions. The stranger did not understand at first, for the stranger was from the lowlands where the faith of the Still Flame was not practised, and the stranger had never learned the discipline of tending the fire. The hermit did not attempt to explain the teaching of the Still Flame to the stranger; he simply continued to tend the fire, and the stranger watched, and the watching was the beginning of the stranger's understanding.

## The Passing of the Flame

The parable concludes with the hermit's death and the passing of the flame to the stranger. The hermit, knowing that his time was near, taught the stranger the discipline of the morning kindling and the evening banking, and he instructed the stranger to carry the flame down to the lowlands when the winter broke and to share it with the communities that had lost their fires. The stranger promised to do as the hermit asked, and when the hermit died, the stranger took the flame from the hermit's hearth and carried it down from the mountain.

The stranger found that the lowland communities had indeed lost their fires, for the long winter had exhausted their stores of fuel and their faith in the return of the warmth. The stranger built a fire in the centre of the first village that he came to, using the flame that the hermit had entrusted to him, and the people gathered around the fire and felt its warmth and remembered the hope that they had lost. The stranger taught the discipline of the morning kindling and the evening banking, and the fire spread from village to village, and the flame that the hermit had kept alive in the high valley became the fire that lit the world.

## The Meaning of the Parable

The parable of the Hermit and the Flame is understood by the faithful of the Still Flame to contain several layers of meaning. The most obvious layer is the teaching about the importance of the daily discipline: the hermit's faithfulness in tending the fire, even when there was no one to see it and no immediate need for it, was the foundation upon which the restoration of the world was built. The hermit did not know that the stranger would come, and he did not tend the fire for the sake of the stranger; he tended the fire because that was what the Still Flame required, and the stranger's coming was a consequence of the hermit's faithfulness rather than its purpose.

The second layer of meaning concerns the duty of hospitality. The hermit did not turn the stranger away, even though his stores of food and fuel were limited and the winter showed no sign of ending. The hermit shared what he had because the Still Flame teaches that none who come to a flame in need should be turned away, and the sharing was the means by which the flame was preserved.

The third layer of meaning concerns the nature of the flame itself. The hermit's fire was not his own; it was a share in the one Flame that underlies all fires, and the hermit was not the owner of the flame but its keeper. The flame could be carried from the hermit's hearth to the stranger's keeping without being diminished, and the fire that burned in the lowland villages was the same fire that had burned in the hermit's hut.

## The Parable in the Life of the Faith

The parable of the Hermit and the Flame is told in the hearth-halls of the Still Flame at the evening gatherings, and it is recited by the hearth-keepers as part of the instruction of the young. The parable is especially associated with the winter solstice, when the nights are longest and the fire is most precious, and the faithful gather at the hearth-halls to hear the story and to renew their commitment to the discipline of the flame.

The parable is also a favourite of the solitary practitioners of the Still Flame, the hermits and the wanderers who maintain the practice of the faith outside the life of the hearth-halls. The solitude of the hermit in the parable is a model for the solitude of the wanderer, and the parable teaches that the solitary keeper of the flame is as much a part of the faith as the gathered community.
