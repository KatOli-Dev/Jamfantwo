## The Reading of Signs

The folk omens and superstitions of the known world are not a unified system of belief but a vast collection of local traditions that vary from region to region and from community to community. The omens are the signs that the common people observe in the natural world and in the events of their daily lives, and the superstitions are the practices that the people follow to attract good fortune and to ward off bad. The omens and superstitions are not the doctrines of the great religions, but they are older than most of the religions and they persist alongside the formal faiths, a layer of belief that the people maintain regardless of the teachings of the temples and the academies.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have collected the folk omens and superstitions from every region of the known world, and they have produced extensive catalogues of the beliefs that the common people maintain. The catalogues reveal a remarkable diversity of traditions, but they also reveal common patterns that recur across the different regions and the different peoples. The omens are often concerned with the same events: births and deaths, marriages and journeys, the planting and the harvest, the weather and the seasons. The superstitions are often concerned with the same objects: salt and iron, fire and water, certain animals and plants and stones.

## Omens of the Household

The household is the primary setting for the folk omens, for the home is where the common people spend most of their lives and where the signs are most likely to be observed. The hearth is the focus of many omens, for the fire is the centre of the household's life: a fire that burns clear and bright is a sign of good fortune, while a fire that smokes or sputters or goes out unexpectedly is a sign of trouble to come. The sparks that leap from the fire are read as signs as well: a spark that leaps toward the door is a sign that a guest will arrive, while a spark that leaps toward the window is a sign that news will come from a distance.

The omens of the table are also widely observed. A loaf of bread that splits while baking is a sign of a quarrel in the household, while a loaf that rises high and evenly is a sign of harmony and prosperity. Spilled salt is an omen of misfortune, and the person who spills the salt must throw a pinch over the left shoulder to avert the bad luck. A knife that falls from the table is a sign that a visitor is coming, while a spoon that falls is a sign that a child will arrive. The order in which the objects on the table are arranged is also significant, and the careful housewife will take care to set the table in the proper manner.

## Omens of the Natural World

The natural world provides the richest source of omens, and the people of the different regions have developed detailed systems of interpreting the signs that the animals and the plants and the weather present. The behaviour of birds is among the most commonly observed signs: a raven that calls from the roof of a house is a sign of death, while a swallow that nests under the eaves is a sign of good fortune. An owl that calls during the day is a sign that the weather will change, while an owl that calls at night is a sign that a spirit is near.

The behaviour of domestic animals is also read for signs. A dog that howls without apparent cause is a sign that death is approaching, while a dog that barks at nothing is a sign that a spirit is passing. A cat that washes its face in a particular manner is a sign that rain is coming, while a cat that sneezes is a sign that good luck is near. The horses that stamp and snort are read for signs of approaching danger, and the cattle that lie down in the field are read for signs of the weather.

The plants and the trees are also sources of omens. The flowering of certain plants at unusual times is a sign that the seasons are out of balance, while the wilting of a plant that was healthy is a sign that illness will strike. The rowan tree is regarded as a protector against evil spirits, and a branch of rowan hung over the door is said to keep the household safe. The elder tree is regarded with caution, for it is believed to be inhabited by a spirit that may be angered by the cutting of the wood.

## Omens of the Body

The human body is the source of many omens, and the people observe the signs that the body presents with great attention. An itch on the right hand is a sign that money will be received, while an itch on the left hand is a sign that money will be spent. A ringing in the right ear is a sign that good news is coming, while a ringing in the left ear is a sign that bad news is on its way. A sneeze at the beginning of a journey is a sign that the journey will be safe, while a sneeze at the beginning of a meal is a sign that the food is wholesome.

The omens of birth are especially important, for the circumstances of a child's birth are believed to indicate the child's future. A child born during a storm is said to be strong-willed and passionate, while a child born in calm weather is said to be gentle and peaceful. A child born with a caul is said to be protected from drowning, and the caul is preserved and kept as a charm. The day of the week on which a child is born is also significant: children born on certain days are said to be lucky, while children born on other days are said to be unlucky.

## Superstitions of the Journey

The journey is a time of heightened vulnerability, the folk traditions teach, and the traveller must observe the proper precautions to ensure a safe return. The departure must be made with the right foot first, and the traveller must not look back after passing through the door. A journey that begins in the rain is said to be fortunate, while a journey that begins in fog is said to be unlucky. The traveller who meets a red-haired woman on the road must turn back and wait for a more favourable time, while the traveller who meets a white horse may continue with confidence.

The crossroads are regarded as places of special significance, for the crossroads are where the boundaries between the worlds are thin and where spirits and other beings may be encountered. The traveller who passes a crossroads must observe the proper rites: a coin left at the crossing is said to ensure a safe journey, while a stone placed at the crossing is said to mark the path for the return. The crossroads are also places where the dead may be encountered, and the traveller must not linger at the crossing after dark.

## The Persistence of the Old Ways

The folk omens and superstitions persist despite the teachings of the formal religions and the scepticism of the scholars. The people maintain the old ways because the old ways have proven themselves in the experience of the generations, the people say, and the formal religions and the scholarly scepticism have not provided a better way of understanding the signs that the world presents. The omens and the superstitions are the wisdom of the common people, preserved through the ages by the practice of the households and the communities, and the people who observe the omens and follow the superstitions do so not from ignorance but from a respect for the accumulated experience of their ancestors.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading who study the folk traditions have noted that the omens and superstitions are not irrational in the manner that the sceptics claim. The omens and superstitions are based on observations of the natural world and on patterns that the people have noticed over many generations, and the interpretations that the people place on the signs are often consistent with the patterns that the scholars have observed through more systematic methods. The folk traditions are a form of knowledge, the scholars acknowledge, and the dismissal of the folk traditions as mere superstition is itself a form of ignorance that the scholars must avoid.
