## The Small Folk and the Hidden Ones

The halfling harvest tales are among the most cheerful and the most cherished of the folklore traditions of the known world, and they are preserved with particular care in the halfling communities of the [Emberlands](/content/location/settlement/region/emberlands), the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale), and the river valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The tales tell of the hidden folk who inhabit the fields and the forests and the streams, the spirits of the harvest and the guardians of the growing things, and the relations between the halflings and these hidden ones. The tales are not myths of creation in the grand sense of the [dwarven Stone Song](/content/mythology/dwarven-stone-song) or [the centaur Sky Dance](/content/mythology/centaur-sky-dance); they are stories of the everyday world, of the work of the fields and the pleasures of the table and the small miracles that sustain the life of the community.

The halflings do not possess a single creation myth that explains the origins of the world in the manner of the other peoples. The halflings are content to inhabit a world that they did not make and that they do not need to explain, and their stories are concerned with the maintenance of the world rather than with its origin. The harvest tales are the closest that the halflings come to a mythology, and they are sufficient for the halfling understanding of the world and the halfling's place within it.

## The First Harvest

The oldest of the halfling harvest tales tells of the first harvest, the time when the hidden folk taught the halflings to plant and to tend and to gather the fruits of the earth. According to the tale, the first halflings lived on what they could gather from the wild places, and they were content with this life, but the hidden folk saw that the halflings could live more comfortably if they learned to cultivate the land, and the hidden folk took pity on them and shared the secrets of the seed and the soil.

The tale describes the first meeting between the halflings and the hidden folk, a meeting that occurred at the edge of a field where the wild grain grew. The hidden folk appeared to the halflings as figures of light and shadow, too quick and too subtle for the eye to follow, and they spoke in voices that sounded like the rustling of the leaves and the murmuring of the streams. The halflings were afraid at first, the tale says, but the hidden folk were patient and gentle, and they taught the halflings the songs that the seeds need to hear and the dances that the soil needs to feel and the words of thanks that must be spoken at the cutting of the grain.

The first harvest was a celebration that lasted for three days, the tale says, and the hidden folk joined the halflings at the table and shared in the bounty that they had helped to create. The hidden folk have remained close to the halflings ever since, and the harvest festivals are still the occasions when the two peoples come together to celebrate the turning of the seasons and the abundance of the earth.

## The King of the Grain

One of the most beloved of the halfling harvest tales is the story of the King of the Grain, a figure who appears in the fields at the time of the harvest and who must be treated with respect if the following year's crop is to be abundant. The King of the Grain is not a ruler in the human sense; he is the spirit of the grain itself, the life that dwells within the stalks and that must be honoured when the stalks are cut.

The tale tells of a farmer who neglected to honour the King of the Grain, who cut the harvest without speaking the words of thanks and who left no offering at the edge of the field. The King of the Grain was angered by this neglect, and he withdrew his presence from the farmer's fields, and the following year the crops failed and the farmer and his family went hungry. The farmer learned his lesson, the tale says, and he sought out the wise ones of the community who knew the proper rites, and he performed the ceremonies of apology and made the offerings that the King of the Grain required, and the spirit returned to the fields and the crops grew again.

The tale is taught to halfling children as a lesson in gratitude and respect, and the halflings maintain the custom of leaving a portion of the harvest in the fields as an offering to the King of the Grain. The offering is not a sacrifice in the manner of the temple religions; it is a gift of thanks, a recognition that the harvest is not the work of the farmer alone but the work of the hidden folk and the soil and the sun and the rain as well.

## The Spriggan and the Cellar

The halfling harvest tales include stories of tricksters and mischief-makers as well as stories of the benevolent hidden folk. The most famous of the trickster figures is the Spriggan, a being of the hedgerows and the thickets who delights in playing tricks on the unwary and who must be placated with offerings of milk and honey if the household is to enjoy peace and prosperity.

The tale of the Spriggan and the Cellar tells of a halfling family whose cellar was plagued by a Spriggan who soured the milk and nibbled the cheese and scattered the stores of root vegetables across the floor. The family tried every means of driving the Spriggan away, but the tricks only grew worse, until the grandmother of the family remembered the old custom of leaving a saucer of milk and a spoonful of honey at the cellar door. The family followed the grandmother's advice, and the Spriggan was appeased, and the cellar was peaceful from that time forward.

The tale is told as a reminder that the hidden folk are not always benevolent and that they must be treated with respect if the household is to prosper. The halflings maintain the custom of leaving offerings at the thresholds and the doorways and the corners of the fields, not from fear but from a recognition that the world is shared between the halflings and the hidden ones and that the hidden ones have their own needs and their own ways of making them known.

## The Festival of the First Grain

The halfling harvest tales are intimately connected to the festivals of the halfling year, and the most important of these festivals is the Festival of the First Grain, which marks the beginning of the harvest season. The festival is described in the tales as a time when the boundary between the halfling world and the world of the hidden folk grows thin, and the hidden folk walk openly among the halflings and share in the celebrations.

The tale of the First Grain Feast describes the preparations that the community must make for the festival, the cleaning of the houses and the decoration of the tables and the baking of the special bread that is shared with the hidden folk. The tale describes the appearance of the hidden folk at the feast, their forms more visible than at other times of the year, their voices clearer and more distinct, their presence a blessing upon the community that has remembered to honour them.

The tale concludes with the return of the hidden folk to their own realm at the end of the festival, and the halflings are left with the memory of the celebration and the promise that the hidden folk will return again when the next harvest comes. The Festival of the First Grain is the most important of the halfling observances, and it is the occasion when the community renews its bonds with the hidden folk and with the land that sustains them both.

## The Wisdom of the Harvest Tales

The halfling harvest tales are not merely stories for the entertainment of children; they contain the accumulated wisdom of the halfling people, the knowledge of the soil and the seasons and the hidden folk that has been preserved through the generations. The tales teach the halflings how to live in harmony with the land and with the beings who share it, and they preserve the customs and the rites that maintain the prosperity of the community.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading who have studied the halfling harvest tales have observed that the tales are remarkably consistent across the different halfling communities, despite the distances that separate them. The tales preserve a body of knowledge about the natural world that the halflings have accumulated over many centuries, and the scholars acknowledge that the halflings possess a practical understanding of the land and its cycles that exceeds the knowledge of the more scholarly peoples.

The halflings do not claim that their harvest tales are literally true in the manner that the historians claim the truth of their chronicles. The tales are true in the way that the seasons are true, the halflings say, and in the way that the grain grows and the hidden folk visit and the harvest comes again in its time. The truth of the tales is the truth of the world that the halflings inhabit, and it is a truth that the halflings have found to be sufficient for their needs.
