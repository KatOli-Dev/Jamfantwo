## The Ancient Enmity

The folktale of the Dragon and the Phoenix is among the most widely told stories in the known world, and versions of it are found among the peoples of every continent. The tale tells of the ancient enmity between the great dragon of the mountains and the phoenix of the skies, a conflict that lasted for ages and that threatened to consume the world, and of the reconciliation that ended the conflict and established the balance that the world now enjoys. The tale is a story of opposites that must be reconciled, of fire that burns and fire that renews, of the earth and the sky and the forces that hold the world together.

The oldest versions of the tale are preserved in the oral traditions of the eastern lands, where the dragon and the phoenix are regarded as the two great powers that govern the cycles of the world. The versions that the scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have collected from the different regions of the world differ in their details, but they all agree on the essential elements of the story: the dragon and the phoenix were enemies, their conflict threatened the world, and the reconciliation that ended the conflict was achieved through the intervention of a third party who saw the truth that the two combatants could not see.

## The Dragon's Domain

The dragon in the tale is a creature of the earth and the deep places, and its nature is the nature of the stone and the fire that burns within the mountain. The dragon is patient and enduring, the tale says, but its patience is the patience of the stone that endures until it can endure no longer and then breaks with a force that cannot be resisted. The dragon's fire is the fire of the forge and the volcano, the fire that destroys and transforms, and the dragon breathes this fire because the fire is its nature and it cannot do otherwise.

The dragon's enmity toward the phoenix arose from the dragon's nature, the tale teaches. The dragon is a creature of stability and permanence, and the phoenix is a creature of change and renewal, and the two natures are opposed in a way that cannot be resolved by any compromise that leaves both unchanged. The dragon saw the phoenix as a threat to the order of the world, a being of chaos and flux that would undo the work of ages, and the dragon determined to destroy the phoenix and restore the world to the stability that the dragon valued above all else.

## The Phoenix's Realm

The phoenix in the tale is a creature of the air and the light, and its nature is the nature of the sun and the fire that burns in the sky. The phoenix is swift and changeable, the tale says, and its changes are not the changes of chaos but the changes of renewal, the cycles of birth and death and rebirth that sustain the life of the world. The phoenix's fire is the fire of the sun and the dawn, the fire that gives life and warmth, and the phoenix renews itself by burning because the burning is the means of its renewal.

The phoenix's enmity toward the dragon arose from the phoenix's nature, the tale teaches. The phoenix is a creature of change and growth, and the dragon is a creature of permanence and resistance, and the two natures are opposed in a way that cannot be resolved by any compromise that leaves both unchanged. The phoenix saw the dragon as a threat to the vitality of the world, a being of stagnation and resistance that would arrest the growth of all living things, and the phoenix determined to overcome the dragon and free the world for the changes that the phoenix valued above all else.

## The Conflict and the Suffering

The conflict between the dragon and the phoenix lasted for ages, the tale says, and the world suffered terribly from their enmity. The dragon's fire scorched the land and the phoenix's flames burned the skies, and the peoples of the world lived in fear of the day when the two great powers would meet in a final battle that would consume everything. The seasons were disrupted, the harvests failed, the rivers ran dry, and the animals fled from the lands where the conflict raged.

The different versions of the tale describe the conflict in different terms, but they all agree that the suffering of the world was the catalyst for the reconciliation. The world could not endure the conflict indefinitely, and the peoples of the world cried out for relief, and the cry was heard by a being who was neither dragon nor phoenix and who could see what the two combatants could not see.

## The Third Being

The third being who intervened in the conflict is named differently in the different versions of the tale. In the eastern version, the third being is a wise woman who had lived through the ages and who had seen the rise and fall of many powers. In the northern version, the third being is a dwarf of the deep halls who had learned the secret of the stone and the fire. In the southern version, the third being is a desert spirit who had witnessed the cycles of the sun and the stars. In the halfling versions, the third being is a simple farmer who offered the dragon and the phoenix a share of the harvest and a place at the table.

The third being in every version sees what the dragon and the phoenix cannot see: that they are not opposites but complements, that each contains something of the other, that the fire of the dragon and the fire of the phoenix are the same fire manifested in different forms. The third being brings this understanding to the dragon and the phoenix, not through argument or force but through demonstration, by showing the dragon the renewal that the phoenix brings and showing the phoenix the stability that the dragon provides.

## The Reconciliation

The reconciliation of the dragon and the phoenix is the climax of the tale, and it is described in terms that vary among the versions but that all share a common structure. The dragon and the phoenix, having been brought to see their mutual dependence, agree to cease their conflict and to divide the world between them. The dragon will rule the earth and the deep places, the mountains and the caverns and the veins of fire that burn within the stone. The phoenix will rule the sky and the light, the sun and the dawn and the cycles of birth and death and renewal.

But the division is not a separation, the tale teaches, for the dragon's fire and the phoenix's fire are the same fire, and the earth and the sky are the same world, and the two powers remain connected by the balance that they have agreed to maintain. The dragon's fire heats the earth and the phoenix's fire lights the sky, and the two fires together sustain the world in a harmony that neither could achieve alone.

## The Balance and the Meaning

The tale of the Dragon and the Phoenix is not only a story about two mythical beings; it is a teaching about balance and reconciliation, about the resolution of opposites that the world requires for its continued existence. The tale is told in different ways by different peoples, but the meaning is the same wherever the tale is told: the world is held together by the balance of opposing forces, and the destruction of one force by the other would be the destruction of the world itself.

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have observed that the tale of the Dragon and the Phoenix is one of the few myths that is shared across the boundaries of species and continents, and they have speculated that the tale may preserve a memory of an actual historical event, a great conflict between the powers of the world that was resolved through the intervention of a mediator. The scholars acknowledge, however, that the evidence for this interpretation is circumstantial and that the tale may be purely symbolic, a story that expresses in narrative form the balance that the world requires.

The dragon and the phoenix continue to exist in the world, the tale concludes, and their conflict continues in a subdued form, the tension between stability and change that is the condition of life itself. The world is not a place of perfect harmony, and the balance that the dragon and the phoenix maintain is a balance of tension rather than a balance of peace. But the balance holds, and the world continues, and the tale reminds the peoples of the world that the forces that oppose one another are also the forces that sustain one another, and that the reconciliation of opposites is the work that the world requires of those who would live in it.
