## The Darkness That Fell Upon the World

The story of the sun that was stolen is one of the most widely told tales in the known world, and versions of it are found among the human communities of every continent and among many of the other species as well. The story tells of a time when the sun was taken from the sky, and the world was plunged into a darkness that threatened to extinguish all life, and the creatures of the world were forced to undertake a great quest to recover the light. The story is told in different ways by different peoples, but the core of the tale is the same wherever it is told: the sun was stolen, and it was recovered, and the recovery was made possible by the cooperation of the beings who had previously been enemies.

The oldest written version of the story is preserved in the archives of the Cloisters of Reading at Eldreth, where it appears in a manuscript that the scholars date to the early centuries of the human settlement of Aethelar. The manuscript is fragmentary, and the beginning and the end of the story are missing, but the central portion is intact, and it describes a darkness that fell upon the world when the sun was taken by a creature of the deep places who coveted the light and who wished to keep it for himself alone.

## The Thief in the Deep

The thief of the sun is named differently in the different versions of the story. In the northern version, told in the halls of the Karhold Realm and the Frostwall Clans, the thief is a great serpent that dwells in the caverns beneath the mountains, a creature of cold and darkness that hates the light and that stole the sun to extinguish it. In the eastern version, told in the city-states of the Auric Isles and the Thessaran League, the thief is a sorcerer-king who believed that he could rule the world if he controlled the light, and who imprisoned the sun in a tower of black stone. In the southern version, told among the Kharesh Tribes and the Vashari Pact, the thief is a djinn of the deep desert, a being of hunger and want who consumed the sun in its greed and who kept the light locked within its belly.

The common element in all the versions is the motivation of the thief. The thief acts not out of malice toward the peoples of the world but out of a desire that cannot be satisfied, a longing that the sun itself seems to represent. The serpent wants to extinguish the light because the light is pain to its cold eyes. The sorcerer-king wants to control the light because the light is power. The djinn wants to consume the light because the light is warmth and the djinn is cold. The thief in every version is a figure of excess, and the theft of the sun is the consequence of a desire that has grown beyond all measure.

## The Quest for the Light

The recovery of the sun required a quest that brought together the different peoples of the world in a cooperation that they had never achieved before and that they have rarely achieved since. The versions of the story differ in the details of the quest, but they all agree that the recovery required the participation of beings who were natural enemies and who had to set aside their enmity for the sake of the common good.

In the northern version, the quest is undertaken by a human hero, a dwarf, and an eagle, who together descend into the serpent's lair and confront the thief in its own domain. The human hero provides the courage and the ingenuity; the dwarf provides the knowledge of the deep places and the skill to navigate the caverns; the eagle provides the speed and the vision and the ability to carry the recovered sun back to the sky. The three must learn to trust each other and to work together, and the story describes in detail the process by which their initial suspicion and hostility give way to mutual respect and friendship.

In the eastern version, the quest is undertaken by a company of seven companions, each representing a different people: a human, an elf, a dwarf, a centaur, a gnome, a halfling, and a merfolk. The seven companions must overcome the sorcerer-king's defences, each contributing a skill or a knowledge that the others lack, and the story emphasises the theme of diversity as a source of strength. The eastern version is the longest and the most elaborate of the known versions, and it has been adapted into plays and pageants and narrative songs throughout the eastern lands.

In the southern version, the quest is undertaken by a single hero, a woman of the Kharesh Tribes who is guided by the spirits of the desert and who must pass through three trials before she can confront the djinn. The southern version emphasises the theme of inner transformation: the hero must overcome her own fears and desires before she can face the thief, and the recovery of the sun is as much an internal victory as an external one.

## The Reconciliation

The story of the stolen sun is remarkable among the myths of the world for the way it treats the figure of the thief. In many versions of the story, the thief is not destroyed when the sun is recovered; the thief is reconciled with the peoples of the world, and the reconciliation is the true climax of the tale. The serpent is bound in the deep places but not killed, and it is given the warmth of the earth's core to ease its cold. The sorcerer-king is stripped of his power but permitted to live, and he becomes a keeper of the knowledge that he once used for domination. The djinn is satisfied with a portion of the sun's warmth, and it becomes a guardian of the desert water sources.

The reconciliation is the element of the story that the scholars of the Houses of Memory find most significant. The sun was stolen because a desire had grown beyond measure, and the recovery of the sun did not eliminate the desire but redirected it toward a purpose that served the common good. The thief in every version is not a villain to be destroyed but a being who has lost its way and who must be guided back to balance, and the peoples of the world are tested by the theft and found worthy of the light.

## The Variations and the Meanings

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have identified more than forty distinct versions of the stolen sun story, ranging from the elaborate epics of the eastern tradition to the brief and stark accounts of the northern oral traditions. The variations among the versions are significant, and they reflect the different values and concerns of the cultures that preserve them.

The northern versions emphasise the themes of courage and endurance and the importance of cooperation across the boundaries of species. The eastern versions emphasise the themes of diversity and knowledge and the power of working together. The southern versions emphasise the themes of inner transformation and the satisfaction of desire. The halfling versions, which are among the most cheerful of the tales, emphasise the themes of hospitality and sharing: in the halfling versions, the sun was stolen because the thief was lonely and wanted the light for company, and the sun is recovered when the peoples of the world invite the thief to share in their celebrations.

The meaning of the story is not fixed, the scholars of the Cloisters argue, and the different versions offer different insights into the human condition and the nature of the world. The story of the stolen sun is a story about loss and recovery, about desire and satisfaction, about enmity and reconciliation, and it is told in as many ways as there are peoples who need to hear it.
