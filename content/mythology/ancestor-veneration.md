## The Reverence for the Departed

The practice of ancestor veneration is among the most ancient and the most widespread of the spiritual traditions of the known world, and it is found among the peoples of every continent and every species. The practice takes different forms in different cultures, but the underlying belief is the same: the dead do not cease to exist when they depart from the world of the living, and they continue to take an interest in the affairs of their descendants. The ancestors are honoured by the living, and the living seek the guidance and the protection of the ancestors, and the relationship between the living and the dead is maintained through the observances that the tradition prescribes.

The ancestor veneration practices are not religions in the sense that the great temple faiths are religions, for they do not involve the worship of deities or the adherence to a formal doctrine. The practices are a matter of family and community, of the obligations that the living owe to the dead and the respect that the dead are due. The practices are maintained alongside the formal religions, and the practitioners of the Still Flame or the Shield and Song or the other faiths also maintain the observances of the ancestors.

## The Houses of Memory

The most formal and the most extensive of the ancestor veneration traditions are the Houses of Memory, the establishments that the Order of the Shield and Song maintains for the keeping of the community's memory. The Houses of Memory are the places where the names of the dead are preserved and where the songs of the ancestors are sung, and the practitioners of the order maintain the discipline of the evening review as the means by which the memory of the departed is kept alive.

The Houses of Memory are open to all members of the community, and the names of the dead are added to the rolls that the singers maintain. The singers learn the names by heart, and the songs that they sing at the evening review include the names of those who have died since the previous review, so that the dead are not forgotten. The tradition holds that a name that is not spoken is a name that is lost, and the discipline of the review is the discipline of speaking the names so that the names will not be lost.

The Houses of Memory are the most visible institution of the ancestor veneration tradition, but they are not the only institution, and the practice of honouring the ancestors is maintained in the households of the common people as well as in the establishments of the order. The family maintains the memory of its own ancestors through the stories that are told and the names that are preserved, and the household is itself a house of memory in the smaller sense.

## The Practices of the Different Peoples

The ancestor veneration practices of the different peoples reflect the conditions and the values of the cultures that maintain them. The elves of the great forests maintain the most detailed genealogical records of any people, and the elven ancestors are remembered not only by name but by the deeds that they performed and the qualities that they possessed. The elven ancestors are believed to continue to inhabit the forests where they lived, and the elves who walk the forest paths may encounter the spirits of their ancestors and receive guidance from them.

The dwarves of the deep halls maintain the tradition of the Ancestral Chamber, the cavern where the remains of the honoured dead are placed and where the living come to pay their respects. The dwarven ancestors are believed to continue to work in the stone, and the dwarves who hear the song within the stone may be hearing the voices of the ancestors who continue to shape the mountain. The dwarven tradition teaches that the dead are not gone but have passed into the stone, and the mountain that the dwarves inhabit is the body of the ancestors.

The centaurs of the plains maintain the tradition of the Ancestral Herd, the great gathering of the spirits of the centaurs who have died and who continue to move across the plains in the form of the wind and the grass and the running water. The living centaurs honour the Ancestral Herd by maintaining the patterns of movement that the ancestors established, and the migrations of the centaurs are a continuation of the migrations of the ancestors.

The halflings of the fields and the gardens maintain the most intimate of the ancestor traditions, and the halfling ancestors are believed to inhabit the household alongside the living. The halflings set a place at the table for the ancestors on the feast days, and they leave offerings of food and drink at the threshold for the spirits who return to visit. The halfling tradition teaches that the ancestors are never far away, and that the living and the dead share the same house and the same table and the same life.

## The Kharesh Ancestor Traditions

The Kharesh Tribes of Sahrana maintain a tradition of ancestor veneration that is adapted to the conditions of the desert and the nomadic way of life. The ancestors are believed to inhabit the places where they died, and the travellers who pass through these places must observe the proper rites to ensure the goodwill of the spirits. The cairns that the nomads build at the sites of the deaths are the markers of the ancestral presence, and the travellers who pass the cairns add stones to them as a sign of respect.

The Kharesh tradition also includes the practice of the Ancestral Dream, the vision that the ancestor sends to the descendant in sleep to warn of danger or to guide the descendant toward a decision. The Ancestral Dream is regarded with great seriousness by the Kharesh peoples, and the dreamer who receives the vision must act upon it or risk the displeasure of the ancestor. The interpretation of the Ancestral Dream is the work of the dream-readers, the members of the tribe who have the gift of understanding the messages that the ancestors send.

## The Frostwall Ancestor Traditions

The Frostwall Clans of northern Morvind maintain a tradition of ancestor veneration that is oriented toward the winter and the cold. The ancestors are believed to inhabit the northern lights, the aurora that dances across the winter sky, and the spirits of the dead are visible in the movements of the lights. The Frostwall peoples watch the aurora for signs of the ancestors' presence, and they interpret the colours and the patterns of the lights as messages from the dead.

The Frostwall tradition includes the practice of the [Winter Telling](/content/culture/winter-telling), the period during the deepest winter when the families gather around the hearth and tell the stories of the ancestors. The Winter Telling begins on the longest night of the year and continues until the first signs of the spring, and the stories that are told during this period are the means by which the memory of the ancestors is preserved. The Winter Telling is the most important of the Frostwall cultural observances, and the children who hear the stories during the Winter Telling carry the memory of the ancestors into the future.

## The Debate Over the Nature of the Ancestors

The scholars of the Cloisters of Reading have debated whether the ancestors that the people honour are genuinely conscious beings who continue to exist after death or whether the ancestor veneration practices are symbolic expressions of the community's relationship to its own past. The debate is not one that the common people who practise the ancestor veneration engage in, for the people are concerned with the practical effects of the practices rather than with the theoretical questions about the nature of the afterlife.

The people who honour the ancestors do so because the practices have proven themselves in the experience of the generations. The ancestors who are honoured are believed to continue to protect and to guide their descendants, and the families who maintain the practices are believed to prosper as a result. The practices are a matter of faith, and the faith is its own justification.

## The Continuity of the Tradition

The ancestor veneration traditions of the known world have persisted through the rise and the fall of the great religions and the changes of the political order. The traditions are maintained by the families and the communities, and they do not depend on the support of the temples or the academies. The ancestor veneration is the most personal and the most intimate of the spiritual traditions, and it is the tradition that is most likely to survive the changes that the future will bring.
