## The Song That Sleeps in the Stone

The dwarven creation myth, known in the dwarven tongue as Khaz-Vorn or the Stone Song, is the foundation upon which the dwarven understanding of the world and their place within it is built. The myth is not a story in the ordinary sense, for the dwarves do not tell it as a narrative of events that happened in a distant past; they experience it as a continuing reality, a resonance that persists within the stone and that the dwarves are able to hear and to respond to through their craft. The Stone Song is the most ancient of the dwarven traditions, and it is preserved in its fullest form in the deep halls of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm), though every dwarven settlement maintains some version of the teaching.

According to the tradition that the dwarven stone-singers have handed down through countless generations, there was in the beginning neither light nor dark, neither heat nor cold, but only the stone. The stone was not dead matter as the surface peoples imagine it; it was alive with a potential that had not yet been realised, a stillness that contained within itself every form that would later emerge. The stone was the only substance, and it extended in every direction without limit, and it was silent with the silence that precedes the first sound.

## The First Resonance

The mountain sang, the stone-singers say, and the singing was the first event, the first change, the first movement in the infinite stillness. The mountain did not sing with a voice, for there was no air for the voice to travel through; it sang through the stone itself, a vibration that passed through the substance of the world and shaped it as it passed. The song was not a melody in the manner that the surface peoples understand melody; it was a pattern of resonance and dissonance, of pressure and release, of the fundamental notes that the stone responds to and that the dwarven carvers have learned to recognise.

The song formed the caverns and the passages and the great halls of the deep places, and it formed the veins of metal that run through the stone and the crystals that grow in the hidden chambers far beneath the surface. The song formed the mountains themselves, raising them from the stone and shaping their peaks and their slopes and their hidden interiors. The world that the surface peoples see was shaped by the same song that shaped the deep places, though the surface was shaped from a greater distance and the song grew fainter as it travelled upward through the stone.

## The Shaping of the Dwarves

The dwarves were shaped from the living stone by the same song that shaped the mountains and the caverns and the veins of metal. The stone-singers teach that the dwarves were not created by an act of will or by the intervention of any external power; they emerged from the stone as the song passed through it, as the crystals emerge from the rock when the conditions are right and the minerals are present. The dwarves are part of the stone, and the stone is part of the dwarves, and the song that shaped them continues to resonate within them.

The first dwarves were rough and unfinished, the stone-singers say, like uncarved blocks fresh from the quarry, and they learned to shape themselves by listening to the song and by working the stone in accordance with its resonance. The craft of the dwarven carver is not a skill that is taught in the manner that surface crafts are taught; it is a discipline of listening, of opening oneself to the song that persists within the stone and of allowing the hand to follow the resonance. The master carver is one who hears the song most clearly and who can shape the stone in accordance with its deepest patterns.

## The Stone-Singers and Their Lineage

The stone-singers are the keepers of the Stone Song, and they are the most revered members of dwarven society. The stone-singers are not a priesthood in the manner of the human religions; they do not conduct rites or offer prayers or intercede with any divine power on behalf of the community. The stone-singers are master carvers who have trained for decades to hear the song within the stone, and their authority derives from their skill and their knowledge and the depth of their listening.

The lineage of the stone-singers is traced through apprenticeship rather than through blood, and the training of a stone-singer begins in childhood and continues for a minimum of twenty years. The apprentice learns to work the stone with the hands and the tools, but the true training is the training of the ear and the sensibility, the cultivation of the ability to perceive the resonance that lies beneath the surface of the stone. The stone-singers say that the song is always present, but that most dwarves cannot hear it because they have not learned to listen, and the work of the apprentice is to learn to listen.

## The Song and the Craft

The Stone Song is the foundation of the dwarven craft, and the dwarven carvers and smiths and masons all work in accordance with its principles. The dwarven understanding of metalwork is not a science in the manner that the human smiths understand it; it is a discipline of resonance and harmony, of shaping the metal in accordance with the song that the metal itself contains. The dwarven smiths say that the metal has its own voice, and that the skilled smith is the one who can hear the voice and who can shape the metal without forcing it against its nature.

The dwarven craft is not limited to the working of stone and metal. The dwarven builders shape their halls according to the song, and the dwarven cities of the deep places are arranged in patterns that correspond to the resonance of the surrounding stone. The halls are not designed in the manner that the surface peoples design their buildings, with an eye to proportion and appearance; they are shaped to follow the natural lines of the stone, and the beauty of the dwarven halls is the beauty of the stone itself when it is allowed to express its own nature.

## The Variations Among the Dwarven Peoples

The Stone Song is preserved in its fullest form in the [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) Holds and the [Karhold](/content/location/settlement/city/karhold) Realm, but the dwarven peoples of the different continents maintain variations on the teaching that reflect the particular conditions of their homelands. The dwarves of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), who live in the deepest and most extensive of the dwarven halls, emphasise the aspect of the song that speaks of the depths and the darkness and the slow work of the stone over vast spans of time. The dwarves of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), whose halls are closer to the surface and who trade more extensively with the surface peoples, emphasise the aspect of the song that speaks of the relation between the deep places and the open air.

The dwarves of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) are few in number and their tradition of the Stone Song is fragmentary and adapted to the hot and dry conditions of their adopted homeland. The [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) dwarves tell of a song that passes through the sand as well as the stone, and they have developed a craft of working the desert glass that is unknown in the northern continents. The Sahrani dwarves are not regarded by the northern dwarves as possessing the full tradition of the Stone Song, but the northern dwarves acknowledge that the Sahrani dwarves have preserved something of value in their adaptation of the teaching to the conditions of the desert.

## The Relation to the Surface Faiths

The Stone Song is not a religion in the manner of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) or the [Shield and Song](/content/religion/shield-and-song), and the dwarves do not regard it as competing with the surface faiths for the allegiance of the peoples. The dwarves who live near the surface and who trade with the surface peoples often adopt some of the practices of the local religions while maintaining the Stone Song as their primary understanding of the world. The dwarven communities of the Stoneheim Holds maintain a cordial relation with the Still Flame and the [Unwritten Name](/content/religion/unwritten-name), and the dwarven carvers have contributed to the construction of hearth-halls and temples in the surface settlements.

The dwarven stone-singers regard the surface religions with a certain distance, for the surface faiths concern themselves with matters that the dwarves consider secondary. The Still Flame teaches of the warmth that underlies the world, and the Shield and Song teaches of the watch and the memory, and the dwarves acknowledge the truth of these teachings as far as they go. But the dwarves hold that the deepest truth is the truth of the stone and the song, and that the surface faiths are concerned with the conditions of the surface rather than with the fundamental nature of the world.

## The Continuing Song

The Stone Song does not belong to the past. The song that shaped the world in the beginning continues to resonate within the stone, and the dwarves continue to hear it and to respond to it through their craft. The dwarven carvers say that the song is not finished, that the world is still being shaped, and that the dwarves participate in the shaping by their work. The halls that the dwarves carve are not merely dwellings; they are contributions to the continuing creation, extensions of the song into new chambers and new passages and new forms.

The Stone Song is the oldest of the dwarven traditions, and it will be the last, for as long as the stone endures and as long as there are dwarves to hear the song within it, the Stone Song will continue. The dwarves do not speculate about what will happen when the song finally ceases, for they cannot imagine a world without the song, and they do not wish to imagine one.
