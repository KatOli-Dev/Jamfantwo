## Overview

The First-Grain Feast is the principal late-summer observance of the agricultural communities of central and southern [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), celebrated by the farmers and townsfolk of the river valleys and grainlands of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic), and the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) on the day the first sheaves of the new harvest are brought in from the fields. The feast is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone, though customs vary from one people to another. It celebrates the renewal of the earth and the labour that brought the harvest to ripeness, and its rituals acknowledge the bounty the year has provided.

## Origins and Meaning

The feast's origins are prehistoric, and different nations offer different accounts of its founding. Scholars of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) propose that it began as a religious observance of early agricultural peoples, when the first fruits of the harvest were presented at the temple in thanksgiving. Scholars of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) argue that it is older than temple observances, descending from a domestic ritual of prehistoric farmsteads in which the first sheaf was reserved for the household hearth. Scholars of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) suggest the feast was originally a pastoral observance adapted to agriculture when the plains peoples began cultivating the margins of the grasslands.

Regardless of its origins, the feast is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. It takes place on the day the elders of each community appoint for the commencement of the harvest, chosen with reference to the ripeness of the grain in the fields nearest the settlement. The first sheaf is cut by the community elder, and this cutting signals the beginning of the general harvest that follows in the subsequent days and weeks.

## The Cutting of the First Sheaf

The cutting of the first sheaf is performed by the community elder at a small plot set aside for the ceremonial harvest. This plot lies nearest the settlement, and its grain is allowed to ripen a few days earlier than the surrounding fields to ensure readiness on the appointed day. The household whose land the plot represents stands in a circle around the elder while the cutting is performed.

The elder cuts the first sheaf with the community's customary sickle, a tool of considerable antiquity preserved in the elder's household from one generation to the next. The elder speaks an invocation of the earth's fertility and a prayer for the bounty of the coming harvest as the sickle is laid to the grain. The first sheaf is then bound in the customary fashion and presented to the head of the household, who receives it with a gesture of thanks. The cutting of the first sheaf is observed with great solemnity and is one of the most important ceremonial events of the farming year.

## The Procession to the Granary

The community processes from the field to the granary where the harvest will be stored. The elder leads the procession carrying the sheaf upon a pole decorated with ribbons and flowers of the season. The community follows in an order determined by the seniority of households and customary precedence, accompanied by the music of pipes and drums.

The granary is the principal storehouse of the community. The elder carries the sheaf inside and lays it upon the threshing floor at the centre of the building. This laying is the formal moment of the procession, marked by words from the elder invoking protection of the grain through the months of storage and praying for renewal of the harvest in the year to come.

## The Blessing of the Loaves

The blessing of the loaves is conducted at the communal oven. The loaves are prepared from the grain of the first sheaf and shaped in forms customary to the community, most commonly the shape of a wheel or a sun that recalls the celestial body whose heat ripened the grain. The elder pronounces a blessing upon the loaves before the fire is kindled.

The bakers manage the oven and the timing of the bake. The loaves are watched throughout, and when the crust turns the colour of ripened wheat they are pronounced ready. The loaves are drawn from the oven, allowed to cool, and broken for the feast. The bread of the feast is reckoned to possess a particular virtue that ordinary bread does not, and it is shared among the households with the care the customs of the community require.

## The Feast and the Sharing

The feast is the principal social occasion of the harvest season, attended by the community and by visitors from neighbouring towns. The principal dish is the blessed bread, broken by the elder and distributed to the guests. The bread is accompanied by the first fruits of the harvest — early vegetables, herbs of the kitchen garden, and honey from the season's first extraction. The ale and mead are brewed from the previous year's grain, and the quality of the brewing is a matter of household pride.

The feast is also the occasion for customary gift-giving. Households exchange the produce of their farms and gardens in a formal ceremony conducted by the elder. The sharing of gifts measures the bond the community recognises among its members. The feast is conducted with strict observance of form from the opening invocation to the closing word of thanks.

## The Dance and the Contests

The dance and contests are held in the evening after the feast. The elder and the head of the household whose sheaf was first cut lead the dance, joined by the community in sequence determined by the customary order of households. Pipes and drums accompany the dancing, which continues until the small hours. The songs sung at the dance recount the events of the farming year and the deeds of the households that worked the land.

Contests include wrestling, foot races, and sheaf-throwing, judged by appointed elders. Prizes include a sheaf of new grain, a loaf of blessed bread, and a measure of the feast's ale. The contests are conducted with a friendly rivalry and provide an occasion for the display of the strength and skill that the farming life requires.

## The Distribution to the Stranger

The distribution to the stranger is the final observance of the feast, conducted at the gate of the settlement or at the customary place the community has established. The [Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/open-hand) doctrine requires that strangers and travellers be received at the feast and provided with a share of the food and merriment. Households take turns providing lodging for visitors, extending hospitality without regard to rank or nation.

The elder presents to each visitor a loaf of blessed bread and a measure of ale, addressing words of welcome and a wish for the visitor's welfare on the journey ahead. The distribution is observed with great strictness in every community that recognises the Open Hand, and visitors who have received the distribution speak well of the welcome they received, enhancing the reputation of the community.

## Variations among the Peoples

The [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) observes the feast at the margin of the grasslands, combining agricultural rites of the harvest with pastoral rites of the herd. The [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) includes the formal presentation of the first sheaf at the temple, reflecting the maritime character of the city and its adaptation of agricultural rites to urban conditions. Communities of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) observe the feast in the form established by the central lowlands, practiced in every farming settlement from the western foothills to the eastern lowlands.

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) do not observe the feast as the surface peoples do, for dwarven communities do not practice lowland agriculture and their calendar is ordered by the rhythm of the underground workings. The dwarves mark the harvest season with their own observances adapted to mountain life.

## Significance

The First-Grain Feast is the principal late-summer observance of the agricultural communities of central and southern [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The feast holds both civic and religious significance, recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed, and the cutting of the first sheaf is one of the customary acts of agricultural communities at the beginning of the harvest season. The feast endures as a celebration of the bond between the people and the earth, affirming that the grain which sustains households through the winter is the gift of the soil and of the labour the soil requires.
