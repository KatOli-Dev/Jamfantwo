## The Measurement and Mapping of a Curved World

Spherical Cartography is the discipline of representing the curved surface of the world upon flat charts and of determining position upon that surface through the measurement of celestial bodies. It is the principal intellectual tool of the navigators and geographers of the present age, and its development has been the work of the academies of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) and of the scholars who have corresponded with them across the three continents. The practitioners of the discipline are called spherical cartographers, and their charts and tables are the foundation upon which the maritime trade of the known world depends.

## The Curvature of the World

The central premise of Spherical Cartography is that the world is a sphere, or very nearly so, and that the surface upon which all peoples dwell is the outer surface of this sphere. The premise is not accepted by every tradition of learning; the temples of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/culture/sky-walkers-way) teach that the world is a flat disc beneath the dome of the sky, and the older traditions of the dwarven holds preserve accounts of a world that is a great stone suspended in the void. The spherical cartographers hold that the evidence for the curvature of the world is overwhelming and that the contrary traditions are the product of an incomplete understanding of the nature of the world.

The evidence for the curvature of the world is of several kinds, and the spherical cartographers have assembled it into a body of argument that they regard as conclusive. The first evidence is the behaviour of ships upon the open sea: a vessel departing from a harbour disappears over the horizon hull-first and mast-last, which is the pattern that would be expected if the surface of the water were curved. The second evidence is the changing elevation of the celestial pole as a traveller moves north or south: the pole rises as the traveller moves northward and sinks as the traveller moves southward, which is the pattern that would be expected if the traveller were moving across the surface of a sphere.

The third evidence is the shape of the shadow of the world upon the moon during the lunar eclipses that occur from time to time. The shadow is always curved, and the curvature is consistent with the shadow that a sphere would cast. The fourth evidence is the testimony of the navigators who have sailed far enough to the south to observe that the stars of the southern sky are different from the stars of the northern sky, and that the southern stars rise and set in patterns consistent with the rotation of a sphere. The spherical cartographers regard the accumulation of these evidences as sufficient to establish the curvature of the world beyond reasonable doubt.

## The Methods of Position-Finding

The practical work of the spherical cartographer is the determination of position upon the surface of the world and the representation of that position upon a chart. The determination of position is accomplished through the measurement of the elevation of the sun and the principal stars above the horizon and through the calculation of the latitude and longitude that correspond to these measurements.

Latitude is determined by measuring the elevation of the celestial pole above the northern horizon or, in the southern hemisphere, by measuring the elevation of the principal southern stars. The measurement is accomplished with an instrument called the astrolabe, a device of graduated circles and sighting arms that the cartographer holds before the eye and aligns with the celestial body. The astrolabe is the principal tool of the spherical cartographer, and the quality of the instrument determines the accuracy of the measurement.

Longitude is more difficult to determine than latitude, and the problem of longitude is the principal technical challenge that the spherical cartographers of the present age have not yet fully resolved. The difficulty arises from the rotation of the world, which causes the apparent position of the celestial bodies to change as the observer moves east or west. The determination of longitude requires the simultaneous measurement of the time at a reference point and the time at the observer's location, and the measurement of time at sea is a matter of considerable difficulty. The cartographers of the Amurran academy have developed methods of estimating longitude through the observation of the moons of distant planets and through the careful recording of the direction and distance of travel, but these methods are less precise than the determination of latitude, and the improvement of longitude measurement is a matter of ongoing research.

## The Charts and Their Projections

The representation of the curved surface of the world upon a flat chart is a problem of geometry that the spherical cartographers have addressed through the development of map projections. A projection is a systematic method of transferring the positions of points upon the sphere to positions upon a flat sheet, and each projection involves a compromise between the preservation of accurate shapes, accurate distances, and accurate directions. The choice of projection depends upon the purpose of the chart and the region it covers.

The most commonly used projection in the charts of the present age is the one developed by the cartographers of the Amurran academy, which preserves accurate directions at the cost of distorting shapes and distances in the higher latitudes. This projection is well suited to the needs of navigation, for the navigator who plots a course upon a chart using this projection can measure the bearing of the course directly from the chart and steer the vessel accordingly. The projection is less well suited to the representation of the relative sizes of landmasses, for the high-latitude regions appear larger than they are relative to the equatorial regions.

The charts produced by the spherical cartographers are among the most valued products of the academies. The charts of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) published under the direction of [Halen Amurra](/content/people/notable/halen-amurra) are the standard reference of the navigators of the great ports, and the charts of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) produced by the cartographers of [Iren Khalisar](/content/people/notable/iren-khalisar) are the most detailed and accurate charts of those waters in existence. The charts are updated as new observations become available, and the publication of a corrected edition of a principal chart is an event of considerable importance in the maritime world.

## The Relationship with Tide-Reading

Spherical Cartography and [Tide-Reading](/content/culture/tide-reading) are sister disciplines, and the practitioners of each rely upon the findings of the other. The spherical cartographer requires the tide-reader's tables to correct the position of coastal features for the state of the tide, and the tide-reader requires the spherical cartographer's charts to plot the locations of observation stations and to relate the observations from different stations to one another. The two disciplines are taught together in the academies, and the scholars who have contributed to one have often contributed to the other.

The treatises of Halen Amurra include contributions to both disciplines. His commentary upon Spherical Cartography proposes several refinements to the methods of measurement of position commonly employed by navigators of the great ports. The refinements are based upon his observations of the currents of the Auric Ocean and upon his understanding of the behaviour of the waters in relation to the rotation of the world. The integration of the two disciplines in his work is a measure of the unity of the natural philosophy that the academies of the present age are developing.

## The Frontiers of the Discipline

Spherical Cartography is a discipline whose frontiers are defined as much by what remains unknown as by what has been established. The cartographers of the present age have charted the coastlines of the three continents and the principal islands of the oceans with reasonable accuracy, but the interiors of the larger landmasses are known only through the reports of travellers whose accounts are often imprecise and sometimes contradictory. The cartographers are engaged in the collection and comparison of these reports, and the charts of the interiors are gradually being refined as new information becomes available.

The greatest unknown of Spherical Cartography is the extent of the world beyond the regions that are known to the inhabitants of the three continents. The cartographers have established that the world is a sphere, but they have not determined the circumference of the sphere with precision, and they do not know whether the three continents are the only substantial landmasses upon the surface of the world or whether other continents lie beyond the horizons that the navigators of the present age have reached. The questions are the subject of considerable speculation and of a small number of voyages undertaken specifically to resolve them, but the answers remain beyond the reach of the methods available to the present generation of scholars.

The future of Spherical Cartography lies in the extension of the network of observations to the less frequented regions of the world and in the refinement of the instruments and methods that the cartographers employ. The scholars of the present generation are confident that the remaining questions will yield to the patient accumulation of observations, and that the discipline will continue to advance the understanding of the shape and extent of the world that all peoples share.
