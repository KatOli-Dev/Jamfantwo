## The Power of the Name

The name is one of the most important elements of individual identity in every culture of the known world. The giving of the name is a ceremony of considerable significance, marking the acceptance of the child into the community and the beginning of the child's life as a member of the society. The customs that govern the choice and the use of names vary considerably from one people to another, reflecting the different beliefs and values of each culture, but the fundamental importance of the name is recognised everywhere.

The name carries the history of the family and the lineage, connecting the individual to the ancestors who have borne the name in the past and to the descendants who will bear it in the future. The name also carries the meaning that the parents have chosen for the child, a declaration of the hopes that the family holds for the child's future. The name is the key to the individual's identity, the word that the individual answers to throughout life and that the community uses to call the individual to account.

## The Naming Customs of the Vael Valley

The naming customs of the Vael river valley are governed by the traditions of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) and the customary law of the parish. The child is given the name at the naming ceremony, which is conducted at the temple when the child is approximately one month old. The ceremony is presided over by the priest, who blesses the child and speaks the name that the parents have chosen.

The Vaelic naming tradition uses the three-part name that is the standard form of the temperate lands. The first name is the given name, chosen by the parents from the traditional stock of Vaelic names or from the names of the ancestors. The second name is the father's name, indicating the lineage of the child. The third name is the place name, indicating the village or the region from which the family comes.

The given names of the Vael valley are drawn from the traditional pool of names that the community has used for generations. ## The Naming Customs of the Plains Peoples

The naming customs of the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) and the human herders of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) are adapted to the nomadic conditions of the grasslands. The child is given the name at the first gathering that follows the child's birth, when the tribe assembles for the naming ceremony and the elders pronounce the name that the parents have chosen.

The naming tradition of the plains uses the single name that is the standard form of the nomadic cultures. The name is chosen from the traditional stock of the tribe or from the names of the ancestors, and the name is supplemented by the epithets that the individual accumulates through life. The epithets describe the deeds or the characteristics of the individual, and they are used to distinguish one individual from another when the single name alone is not sufficient.

## The Naming Customs of the Dwarven Halls

The naming customs of the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) reflect the importance of the lineage and the craft in the dwarven culture. The child is given the name at the binding ceremony, when the child is formally accepted into the lineage of the father. The name is pronounced by the thane of the hall, who speaks the traditional words that mark the child's acceptance.

The dwarven naming tradition uses the two-part name that is the standard form of the dwarven people. The child is given the name at the naming ceremony, conducted at the temple of the city when the child is approximately forty days old. The ceremony is presided over by the priest, who speaks the name that the parents have chosen and records the name in the register of the city.

The Sahrani naming tradition uses the four-part name that is the standard form of the coast. The first part is the given name, chosen from the traditional stock of Sahrani names. The second part is the father's name, indicating the lineage of the child. The third part is the family name, indicating the trading house or the merchant family to which the child belongs.
