## Overview

The Deep Mourning is the principal funeral rite of the peoples of the maritime communities of the known world, observed by the families and the communities of those who have died at sea or whose lives have been bound to the water in a manner that the customary observances of the inland peoples do not adequately address. The rite is observed in every coastal community of the known world and upon every island of the seas that wash the shores of the three continents, though the customs that accompany it vary from one community to another and the chronicler notes that no two communities observe the rite in precisely the same manner. The rite is above all an acknowledgement of the bond between the dead and the sea, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the committal of the body or the memory of the dead to the waters that were the principal theatre of the life that is ended.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the rite are lost in the pre-historic period, and the chroniclers of the different maritime communities offer different accounts of its founding. The scholars of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles) have proposed that the rite was originally a rite of the fisherfolk of the open ocean, a recognition that the bodies of those who had died at sea could not always be recovered for the customary burial of the inland communities. The scholars of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) have proposed that the rite is older than the settled towns of the coast, and that it is descended from an ancient observance of the pre-historic peoples of the shore. Whatever its origins, the rite has acquired a meaning that is independent of the theories of its founding, and the rite is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The rite is typically observed upon the death of a mariner, a fisher, or a person whose life has been closely bound to the sea, though the rite is also observed upon the death of a person whose body cannot be recovered for the customary burial and upon the death of a person who has expressed a wish that the rite be observed upon their behalf. The rite may be conducted in conjunction with the funeral customs of the religion to which the deceased belonged, and the chronicler notes that the rite is sufficiently flexible to accommodate the requirements of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and the other religions that are observed in the coastal communities. The rite is also observed for persons who were not affiliated with any religion, and the rite has a civic as well as a religious character.

## The Preparation of the Body

The preparation of the body for the rite is the first of the customary observances, and the preparation is conducted by the family of the deceased under the supervision of the elder of the community who has been appointed for the purpose. The body is washed in salt water that has been drawn from the sea at the place where the deceased was last ashore, and the washing is accompanied by the recitation of the customary prayers and the customary invocations. The body is then dressed in the clothing that the deceased is known to have preferred, and the clothing is supplemented by the tokens of the deceased's calling, which may include the fisherman's knife, the sailor's cap, or the mariner's instrument.

The body is then placed upon a bier that has been constructed for the purpose, and the bier is draped with a cloth of a colour that is customary for the occasion. The colour of the cloth varies with the community and the religion, but the most common colours are the deep blue of the open ocean, the grey of the coastal waters, and the white of the foam upon the shore. The bier is then transported to the place of the committal, which may be a boat that has been prepared for the purpose, a pier that extends into the water, or a cliff that overlooks the sea. The transport of the bier is accompanied by the customary procession, and the members of the family and the community walk in silence behind the bier, with the elder at the head of the procession.

## The Committal at Sea

The committal at sea is the central rite of the Deep Mourning, and the committal is conducted upon a boat that has been prepared for the purpose. The boat is rowed to a place that is at a sufficient distance from the shore to satisfy the requirements of the customary observance, and the place is typically marked by a buoy or by some other feature that the community has established for the purpose. The body, in its bier, is placed upon a platform that has been constructed at the stern of the boat, and the platform is tilted to allow the bier to slide into the water. The sliding of the bier into the water is the formal moment of the committal, and the moment is marked by the recitation of the customary words and by the casting of the customary offerings into the water alongside the bier.

The offerings that are cast into the water typically include the flowers that the family has gathered for the purpose, the bread that has been broken and shared among the mourners, and the tokens of the deceased's calling that the family has chosen to surrender to the sea. The offerings are cast into the water by the eldest member of the family who is present, and the casting is accompanied by the words that the elder addresses to the deceased. The words are typically a brief acknowledgement of the deceased's life and a wish for the peace of the deceased in the waters that have been the principal element of the deceased's life. The boat then returns to the shore, and the members of the family are conducted back to the community by the elder.

## The Memorial and the Marking

The memorial that follows the committal is the second of the principal elements of the rite, and the memorial is conducted upon the day that follows the committal or at some later date that the family may choose. The memorial is a gathering of the family and the community at the place where the deceased was last ashore, and the gathering is an occasion for the sharing of the memories of the deceased and for the customary refreshment. The memorial is also the occasion for the marking of the place that has been established in honour of the deceased, and the marking may take the form of a stone that has been set upon the shore, a cairn that has been erected upon a cliff, or a post that has been driven into the beach.

The markings that are established in honour of the deceased are a familiar feature of the coastal communities of the known world, and the markings are maintained by the families of the deceased and by the community for as long as the markings may endure. The markings are not held to be sacred in the religious sense, but they are held in a customary reverence, and the disturbance of a marking is accounted a serious breach of the customary observance. The markings also serve a practical purpose, for the markings are the signs by which the community may remember the deceased and by which the visitors to the shore may learn of the lives that have been bound to the sea. The chronicler notes that the markings are also the occasion for the annual observances that the family may conduct in memory of the deceased, and the annual observances are an important element of the customs of the coastal communities.

## Variations among the Communities

The rite is observed in different ways among the different maritime communities of the known world, and the variations are a measure of the diversity of the customs of the communities that share the celebration. The communities of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles) observe the rite upon the open water of the ocean, and the committal is conducted at a considerable distance from the shore. The communities of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) observe the rite in the waters of the strait, and the committal is conducted at the place that the deceased's family has chosen for the purpose. The communities of the inland waters of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) observe the rite upon the rivers and the lakes of the interior, and the committal is conducted at a place upon the water that the family has chosen.

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) do not observe the rite, for the dwarven communities do not dwell upon the water, and the dwarven funeral customs are conducted upon the land. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the central woodlands observe a rite of their own that is adapted to the conditions of the forest, and the elven rite does not include the committal at sea that is the central element of the Deep Mourning. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) observe the rite in a manner that is adapted to the conditions of the desert, and the salawa rite includes the casting of the offerings upon the sand rather than upon the water.

## Significance

The Deep Mourning is the principal funeral rite of the maritime communities of the known world, and the rite is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The rite is an acknowledgement of the bond between the dead and the sea, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the committal of the body or the memory of the dead to the waters that were the principal theatre of the life that is ended. The chronicler notes that the rite has acquired a civic as well as a religious significance that is recognised by the authorities of the coastal communities in which it is observed, and the committal is one of the customary acts of the maritime communities at the death of a member. The rite endures as a celebration of the bond between the dead and the sea and as a reminder that the waters that the mariner has known in life are the waters to which the mariner is committed in death.
