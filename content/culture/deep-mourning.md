## Overview

The Deep Mourning is the principal funeral rite of the maritime communities of the known world, observed upon the death of those whose lives were bound to the water. Every coastal community and island observes the rite, though customs vary from one settlement to another. The rite acknowledges the bond between the dead and the sea, and its rituals direct the body or the memory of the deceased toward the waters that defined the life that has ended.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the rite are prehistoric, and different maritime communities offer divergent accounts of its founding. Scholars of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles) argue that it began among fisherfolk of the open ocean as a recognition that bodies lost at sea could not receive inland burial. Scholars of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) maintain that the rite predates settled coastal towns and descends from an observance of prehistoric shore peoples. Regardless of its origins, the Deep Mourning is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The rite is conducted for mariners, fishers, and others whose lives were closely bound to the sea. It is also observed when a body cannot be recovered for customary burial or when the deceased expressed a wish for the rite. The Deep Mourning may be combined with the funeral customs of the deceased's religion and accommodates the requirements of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and other faiths found in coastal communities. The rite is also performed for those without religious affiliation, giving it a civic as well as a religious character.

## The Preparation of the Body

The family of the deceased, supervised by a community elder, prepares the body. It is washed in salt water drawn from the sea at the place where the deceased was last ashore, accompanied by customary prayers and invocations. The body is dressed in the deceased's preferred clothing, supplemented by tokens of the deceased's calling — the fisherman's knife, the sailor's cap, or the mariner's instrument.

The body is placed upon a bier draped with cloth of a colour customary to the community and religion. The most common colours are deep blue for the open ocean, grey for coastal waters, and white for sea foam. The bier is transported in a silent procession to the place of committal: a boat prepared for the purpose, a pier extending into the water, or a cliff overlooking the sea. The elder leads the procession, and the family and community follow in silence.

## The Committal at Sea

The committal at sea is the central rite of the Deep Mourning. The boat is rowed to a sufficient distance from the shore, a place often marked by a buoy or other feature the community has established. The body in its bier is placed upon a platform at the stern of the boat, and the platform is tilted to allow the bier to slide into the water. This sliding marks the formal moment of committal, accompanied by customary words and the casting of offerings alongside the bier.

Offerings typically include flowers gathered by the family, bread broken and shared among the mourners, and tokens of the deceased's calling surrendered to the sea. The eldest family member present casts the offerings, speaking words that acknowledge the deceased's life and wish for peace in the waters that were the principal element of that life. In stratified communities, the elaboration of the offerings marks the deceased's station: a prosperous merchant's family may cast silver coins and imported spices, while a fisher's family offers only bread and wildflowers, and the harbourmaster's record notes the difference. The boat returns to shore, and the elder escorts the family back to the community.

## The Memorial and the Marking

The memorial is conducted on the day following the committal or at a later date chosen by the family. The family and community gather at the place where the deceased was last ashore to share memories and customary refreshment. The memorial is also the occasion for establishing a marking in honour of the deceased — a stone set upon the shore, a cairn erected upon a cliff, or a post driven into the beach.

These markings are a familiar feature of coastal communities. Families and the community maintain them for as long as they endure. Though not held as sacred in a religious sense, they are treated with customary reverence, and disturbing a marking is considered a serious breach. The markings serve the practical purpose of helping the community remember the dead and informing visitors of lives bound to the sea. Annual observances conducted by families at these markings form an important element of coastal custom.

## Variations among the Communities

Maritime communities observe the rite differently, reflecting the diversity of customs across the known world. In the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), the committal occurs on the open ocean at a considerable distance from the shore. Communities of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) hold the rite in the waters of the strait at a place chosen by the deceased's family. Communities of the inland waters of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) conduct the rite on rivers and lakes at a place the family designates.

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) do not observe the Deep Mourning, as dwarven communities do not dwell upon the water and their funeral customs are conducted on the land. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the central woodlands observe a rite adapted to the forest, which does not include committal at sea. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) observe a version adapted to the desert, casting offerings upon the sand rather than the water. Some coastal communities distinguish between the treatment of mariners who die at sea — whose bodies are committed directly to the water — and those who die ashore, whose bodies are first prepared in the manner described above before being conveyed to the sea. Among the island peoples of the southern latitudes, the bier is set adrift on a small raft pushed out from the shore and allowed to drift beyond sight before the raft itself is taken by the currents; this variant is known as the Drifting Committal.

## Significance

The Deep Mourning is the principal funeral rite of the maritime communities and is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The rite holds both civic and religious significance, recognised by the authorities of the coastal communities, and the committal is one of the customary acts of maritime peoples at the death of a member. The rite endures as an acknowledgement of the bond between the dead and the sea, affirming that the waters the mariner knew in life are the waters to which the mariner is committed in death.
