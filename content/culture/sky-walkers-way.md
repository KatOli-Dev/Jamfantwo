## The Ancient Polytheist Religion of the Grasslands

The Sky Walkers' Way is the oldest of the organised religions of the central grasslands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), a polytheist faith whose pantheon of sky gods, earth spirits, and ancestral figures has been worshipped by the peoples of the plains since the earliest period of recorded history. The religion takes its name from the belief that the principal gods walk upon the sky as the wind walks upon the grass, that the sky is a country of which the earth is a reflection, and that the order of the sky is the pattern by which the order of the earth must be measured. The great temples of the Sky Walkers' Way were first established upon the central grasslands during the Age of Founding, and the faith remains the principal religion of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) and of several of the smaller kingdoms of the interior.

## The Pantheon of the Sky

The pantheon of the Sky Walkers' Way is a company of gods and spirits whose number is not fixed, for the tradition holds that new gods may appear when the sky brings forth a new aspect of itself. The principal gods are those who have been recognised since the earliest period of the religion's history, and they are the objects of the public cult conducted at the temples of the grasslands. Each god is associated with a particular aspect of the sky and a particular function in the order of the world.

The chief of the sky gods is the Walker, the god who moves across the sky in the form of a great wind and whose passage is the wind that sweeps across the grasslands. The Walker is the god of movement, change, and the restless energy that drives the world forward. The Walker is not a creator god in the sense of having made the world; the world is held by the tradition to be eternal, and the gods are the aspects of the sky that have always been present. The Walker is the first among equals, the god whose activity is most visible and most constant.

The second of the principal gods is the Keeper, the god who holds the sky in its place and who maintains the order of the seasons. The Keeper is the god of stability, memory, and the enduring patterns that persist through the cycles of the years. The Keeper is the patron of the temples and of the priests who maintain the traditions of the faith. The Keeper is also the god of the chronicles, for the keeping of records is held to be a participation in the Keeper's own work of maintaining the order of the world.

The third of the principal gods is the Shaper, the god who gives form to the clouds and who shapes the patterns of the sky. The Shaper is the god of craft, art, and the imposition of order upon formless matter. The Shaper is the patron of the artisans and craftsmen, of the smiths and the weavers, and of all who take raw material and shape it into something new. The Shaper is also the god of the hunt, for the hunt is the shaping of the wild into the order of the community.

The lesser gods are the spirits of particular places and aspects of the sky, including the spirits of the four winds, the spirits of the dawn and dusk, and the spirits of the storms and calms. These are honoured in local observances that vary from one community to another, and the tradition does not prescribe a uniform cult for the lesser gods beyond the general obligation to acknowledge them when their presence is manifest.

## The Founding of the Temples

The great temples of the Sky Walkers' Way were established during the Age of Founding, the early period in which the principal polities of the present age were taking shape upon the central grasslands. The chroniclers of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) record the foundation of the temples as the central event of the age, the occasion upon which the peoples of the plains first gathered in large numbers for the observances of the faith and the building of the great structures that would house the cult.

The first temple was built at a place where the sky is said to be closest to the earth, a hill upon the open grassland where the horizon is visible in every direction and the dome of the sky appears to rest upon the rim of the world. The temple is a circular structure of stone and timber, open to the sky at the centre, where the altar of the Walker stands upon a raised platform of earth. The temple has been rebuilt several times over the centuries following damage from storms and fires, but the site has never been abandoned, and the temple is still used for the principal observances of the faith in the present age.

The second temple was built at the edge of the grasslands where the sky meets the forest, a place held to be the boundary between the country of the sky and the country of the trees. The temple is a structure of wood and thatch, blending into the forest so thoroughly that a traveller may pass within a few paces of it without seeing it. The temple is dedicated to the Keeper and is the principal repository of the chronicles of the faith. The third temple was built in the heart of the settled lands, a city temple that served the needs of the growing urban population of the plains and that became the model for the city temples of the later period.

## The Priests and the Observances

The priests of the Sky Walkers' Way are organised into colleges attached to the principal temples, each college governed by a senior priest who serves for the term customary to the community. The priests are responsible for the conduct of the public observances, the maintenance of the temples, and the preservation of the chronicles and traditions of the faith. The priesthood is open to any person who has undergone the training and been accepted by the college, and the selection of candidates is based upon aptitude and dedication rather than upon birth or wealth.

The principal observances of the faith are conducted at the temples upon days appointed by the priests according to the calendar of the sky. The most important observance is the Festival of the Opening, conducted at the spring equinox when the sky and the earth are held to be in balance and the world is renewed. The festival includes the offering of the first shoots of the season at the altar of the Walker, the reading of the chronicles of the year that has passed, and the blessing of the seed grain that will be planted in the weeks following the festival.

The second of the major observances is the Festival of the Closing, conducted at the autumn equinox when the balance tips toward the darkness and the world prepares for the winter. The festival includes the offering of the last sheaf of the harvest at the altar of the Keeper, the reading of the chronicles of the year that is ending, and the blessing of the stores that will sustain the community through the winter.

The minor observances are conducted at the solstices and at the times of the new and full moons, and they vary from one community to another. The tradition does not prescribe a uniform set of minor observances, and the priests of each temple are free to establish the calendar of observances that suits the needs of their community.

## The Relationship with the Still Flame

The relationship between the Sky Walkers' Way and the [Still Flame](/content/culture/still-flame), the monotheistic religion that has spread through the central and southern regions of Aethelar in the later period, has been marked by periods of conflict and periods of accommodation. The earliest encounters between the two religions were characterised by mutual suspicion. The priests of the Sky Walkers' Way regarded the monotheistic teaching as a threat to the social order that the polytheist cult sustained, and the missionaries of the Still Flame regarded the polytheist temples as obstacles to the spread of the true faith.

The conflicts between the adherents of the two religions were most intense in the early period of the Still Flame's expansion, when the missionaries of the monotheistic faith were active in the towns of the river valleys and the priests of the Sky Walkers' Way were attempting to maintain the authority of the traditional cult. The conflicts were conducted more often through argument and debate than through violence, though there are recorded instances of temples being attacked and priests being driven from their communities.

The reconciliation achieved between the two religions in the present age is a measure of the maturity both traditions have attained. The priests of the Sky Walkers' Way have accepted that the Still Flame is a genuine expression of the divine principle, though they maintain that the monotheistic understanding is incomplete and that the full truth requires the acknowledgment of the many aspects of the sky. The clergy of the Still Flame have accepted that the Sky Walkers' Way contains elements of truth, though they maintain that the truth is fully present only in the monotheistic revelation. The accommodation between the two religions is a practical arrangement rather than a theological synthesis, but it has allowed the adherents of both traditions to live together in peace and to co-operate in matters of common concern.

## The Way in the Present Age

The Sky Walkers' Way remains the principal religion of the Plains Confederation and of the smaller kingdoms of the interior that have not adopted the Still Flame. The temples continue to conduct the observances according to the traditions established in the Age of Founding, and the priests continue to maintain the chronicles and the calendar of the sky. The influence of the faith upon the culture and governance of the plains is considerable, and the temples are among the most respected institutions of the region.

The faith has also maintained a presence in the cities of the lowlands, where the adherents of the Sky Walkers' Way and the Still Flame have learned to coexist. The temples of the Way in the lowland cities are smaller and less wealthy than the temples of the grasslands, but they are maintained by communities of adherents who continue to observe the festivals and the minor observances according to the traditions of their ancestors. The enduring presence of the Sky Walkers' Way in the lowland cities is a testament to the resilience of the older traditions and to the capacity of the two religions to find a practical accommodation in the present age.
