## The Universal Duty

The duty of hospitality is recognised by every people of the known world as one of the most fundamental obligations of civilised life. The custom of hospitality requires that the traveller be welcomed into the household of the host, provided with food and shelter, and protected from harm for the duration of the visit. The obligation of the host to the guest is matched by the obligation of the guest to the host, which requires that the guest respect the household of the host and depart without causing harm or trouble.

The forms of hospitality vary from one culture to another, reflecting the different conditions and values of each people, but the underlying principle is the same everywhere: the stranger who comes to the door is to be treated as a member of the household for the duration of the visit. The violation of the obligations of hospitality is regarded everywhere as one of the most serious offences in the customary law, a breach of the trust that holds the community together.

## Hospitality in the Vael Valley

In the Vael river valley, the duty of hospitality is a sacred obligation imposed by the teachings of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame). The faithful are taught that the stranger who comes to the door may be a manifestation of the eternal flame, sent to test the generosity of the household. The household is therefore required to welcome the stranger as the household would welcome the flame itself.

The Vaelic custom of hospitality begins with the greeting at the door, where the host speaks the traditional words of welcome and offers the guest the customary cup of water or ale. The guest is invited into the central room of the household and offered a seat at the hearth, the place of honour in the Vaelic household. The host then offers the guest the meal that is the principal expression of hospitality, sharing the food of the household without regard to the quantity or the quality.

The guest is expected to accept the hospitality of the host with gratitude and to depart within the customary period of three days. The guest who remains beyond the third day is expected to contribute to the work of the household, assisting with the tasks that are appropriate to the guest's abilities. The guest who abuses the hospitality of the host, by stealing or by causing damage to the household or by failing to depart when requested, is subject to the penalties of the customary law.

## The Hospitality of the Plains

The hospitality customs of the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) and the human herders of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) are adapted to the conditions of the nomadic life, in which the traveller is a common figure and the provision of hospitality is a matter of survival. The traveller who comes to the camp is welcomed without question and provided with food and shelter for as long as the traveller requires.

The hospitality of the plains begins with the offering of milk or water, the traditional welcome of the nomadic peoples. The guest is invited to sit at the fire, and the host shares the food of the camp without regard to the guest's ability to repay the hospitality. The guest is expected to observe the customs of the camp, to respect the property of the host, and to depart when the guest's business is concluded.

The hospitality of the plains is also extended to the animals of the traveller. The horses and the camels of the guest are provided with water and pasturage, and the guest is expected to provide the same hospitality to the animals of the host when the host travels through the territory of the guest. The obligation of hospitality extends to the animals because the animals are essential to the survival of the traveller on the plains.

## The Dwarven Hospitality of the Hall

The hospitality customs of the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) are governed by the traditions of the hall, which require that the visitor to the mountain be welcomed with the proper ceremonies and provided with the hospitality that the dwarven tradition requires. The visitor to a dwarven hall is received at the gate by the keeper of the gate, who escorts the visitor to the hall of feasts and presents the visitor to the thane.

The dwarven hospitality includes the offering of food and ale, the provision of a chamber for the visitor's use, and the conduct of the visitor's business with the appropriate masters of the hall. The visitor is expected to observe the customs of the hall, to respect the authority of the thane, and to depart within the period that is specified by the dwarven tradition. The visitor who abuses the hospitality of the hall is barred from returning and may be reported to the other halls of the dwarven people.

## The Sahrani Hospitality of the Courtyard

The hospitality customs of the [Sahrani city-states](/content/culture/sahrani-city-life) reflect the mercantile culture of the coast, in which the traveller is a potential trading partner and the provision of hospitality is a matter of commercial prudence. The traveller who comes to a Sahrani household is welcomed into the courtyard and offered the traditional refreshment of coffee and dates.

The Sahrani custom of hospitality is governed by the rules of the salt, the traditional symbol of the bond between the host and the guest. The guest who has shared the salt of the host is bound by the obligation of gratitude, and the host who has offered the salt is bound by the obligation of protection. The bond of the salt is a sacred bond, recognised by the customary law of the coast, and the violation of the bond is punished by the exclusion of the violator from the community.

## The Hospitality of the Northern Coast

The hospitality customs of the northern coast are adapted to the conditions of the maritime life, in which the traveller is a common figure and the provision of hospitality is a matter of necessity. The traveller who comes to a northern settlement is welcomed at the harbour and escorted to the longhouse of the harbour master, who provides the traveller with food and shelter for the duration of the visit.

The northern custom of hospitality includes the offering of food and ale, the provision of a bed in the longhouse, and the assistance of the harbour master in the conduct of the traveller's business. The guest is expected to contribute to the work of the household, assisting with the tasks that are appropriate to the guest's abilities, and to depart when the traveller's business is concluded.

## The Significance of Hospitality

The customs of hospitality are among the most important of the traditions that the peoples of the known world maintain, reflecting the fundamental recognition that the stranger is entitled to the same consideration as the member of the community. The duty of hospitality is a universal obligation, recognised by every people and enforced by the customary law of every community. The hospitality that the host provides to the guest is the hospitality that the host hopes to receive when the host becomes the guest in another land. The exchange of hospitality is the foundation of the relationships that connect the peoples of the known world, a bond of trust and mutual obligation that makes the travel and the trade between the communities possible.
