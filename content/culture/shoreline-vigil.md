## Overview

The Shoreline Vigil is the principal late-autumn observance of the fishing and trading communities of the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast) of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed by the families and the crews of every settlement from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) during the week in which the autumn gales are expected to begin. The vigil is observed in every harbour and in every cliff village of the coast, though the customs that accompany it vary from one community to another, and the chronicler notes that no two communities observe the vigil in precisely the same manner. The vigil is above all an acknowledgement of the hazards of the season that is beginning, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the safety of the boats that must be hauled up before the storms and the welfare of the crews that will not put to sea again until the spring.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the vigil are lost in the pre-historic period of the coastal peoples, and the chroniclers of the [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) offer different accounts of its founding. The scholars of the eastern coast have proposed that the vigil was originally a practical observance of the fisherfolk, a marking of the moment when the boats must be drawn up from the water and the gear stowed for the winter if the loss of vessel and tackle is to be averted. The scholars of the western coast have proposed that the vigil is older than the settled villages, and that it is descended from a rite of the pre-historic peoples of the cliff caves who watched the storms from the high rocks and counted the safe returns of their kin. Whatever its origins, the vigil has acquired a meaning that is independent of the theories of its founding, and the vigil is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The vigil is reckoned to begin upon the day that the elders of each community have appointed for the commencement, and the day is typically chosen with reference to the observed behaviour of the weather and the migrations of the seabirds that the fishers take as their signs. The commencement is not the same day upon every coast, for the autumn gales reach the eastern communities several days before they reach the western, and the elders of each settlement set the date of the vigil with the judgement that the customs of their place require. The vigil is observed for a period of seven days, and the seventh day is the closing of the observance, marked by the customary feast and the customary ceremonies that the chronicler will describe below.

## The Drawing Up of the Boats

The drawing up of the boats is the first of the principal observances of the vigil, and the drawing up is conducted upon the first day by the crews of every fishing vessel that has worked the season's waters. The boats are hauled from the water upon the customary slips that the communities maintain for the purpose, and the hauling is conducted with the assistance of the households of the crews and the neighbours who have come to lend their strength. The crews sing the customary songs as they haul, and the songs are a measure of the relief that the season of danger is at last being brought to a close. The chronicler notes that the drawing up of the boats is observed with great strictness in every community of the coast, for the boats are the foundation of the livelihood of the people and the loss of a boat in the storms that are coming would be a calamity that no household could easily bear.

Once the boats are drawn up, the gear is stripped from the vessels and stowed in the customary storehouses that the communities maintain above the high-water mark. The nets are hung upon the racks to dry, the sails are folded and taken into the boathouses, and the oars and the masts are laid up in the places that the customs of the community have established. The masts of the larger vessels are unstepped and laid upon the ground beside the hulls, and the vessels are blocked up upon the timbers that will hold them off the ground through the winter. The work of the stowing is conducted by the crews themselves, and the labour is shared among the households according to the customary division that the community enforces.

## The Watch Upon the Cliffs

The watch upon the cliffs is the second of the principal observances of the vigil, and the watch is maintained by the young men and the young women of the community through the hours of darkness from the first day to the seventh. The watchers take their station upon the high rocks that command the approaches to the harbour, and they maintain the watch in pairs, each pair relieved after a turn of four hours by the pair that succeeds them. The watchers carry the customary lanterns that have been lit from the embers of the household fires, and the lanterns are hung upon the hooks that the communities have fixed in the rock for the purpose. The lanterns are visible from far out at sea, and the light of the coastal lanterns is the first sign that a vessel returning late from the fishing grounds sees as it approaches the land.

The watch is not merely a practical measure for the guidance of returning vessels, for the watch is also a vigil in the older sense of the word, a keeping of the night in contemplation of the year that is passing and the year that is to come. The watchers are expected to maintain a silence during their turns of duty, and the silence is observed with a strictness that the customs of the community enforce. The watchers may speak only to give the customary signals of the watch, and the signals are a small repertoire of calls and lamp-flashes that the elders have established for the purpose. The chronicler notes that the watch is regarded as a duty of considerable honour, and the young people who serve upon the cliffs are held to have discharged an obligation that the community recognises and remembers.

## The Memorial for the Lost

The memorial for the lost is the third of the principal observances of the vigil, and the memorial is conducted upon the third day of the observance at the customary place that each community maintains for the purpose. The customary place is typically a cairn that has been erected upon the headland that overlooks the harbour, and the cairn bears the names of the crews and the vessels that have been lost to the storms of the coast in the years that are past. The memorial is attended by the families of the lost and by the members of the community who wish to pay their respects, and the memorial is conducted by the elder who has been appointed for the purpose.

The memorial consists of the recitation of the names inscribed upon the cairn, and the recitation is accompanied by the casting of a sprig of evergreen into the waters of the harbour. The evergreen is gathered from the wind-shrub of the cliff tops, and the sprig is chosen by the family of one of the lost whose name is recited in the course of the ceremony. The casting of the sprig is the formal moment of the memorial, and the moment is marked by the customary words that the elder addresses to the assembly. The words are a brief acknowledgement of the lives that were lost and a wish for the peace of the lost in the waters that have been the principal theatre of their labours. The memorial is concluded by the singing of the customary lament that the coastal communities have maintained for as long as the customs can be traced.

## The Feast of the Last Catch

The feast of the last catch is the fourth of the principal observances of the vigil, and the feast is held upon the fifth day in the boathouse that the community has set aside for the purpose. The feast is prepared from the fish and the shellfish that the crews have taken in the final days before the drawing up of the boats, and the dishes of the feast are the dishes that the customs of the coast have established for the occasion. The principal dish is the salt cod that has been cured in the fashion of the coast, served with the root vegetables that the cliff gardens produce and the flat bread that the households have baked for the feast. The ale that is served at the feast is the last of the season's brewing, and the quality of the ale is a matter of household pride that is the subject of much friendly rivalry among the neighbours.

The feast is attended by the crews and their households, and the feast is an occasion for the customary gifts that the households exchange at the close of the fishing season. The gifts are typically of a practical nature, including the tools and the clothing that the recipient will require in the winter that is coming, and the gifts are accompanied by a word of blessing that the giver addresses to the recipient. The feast is also the occasion for the customary songs that the communities maintain, and the songs recount the events of the season that is ending and the deeds of the crews that worked the waters. The chronicler notes that the songs are a measure of the importance that the coast attaches to the season's labours, and the singing of the songs is one of the principal pleasures of the feast.

## The Lighting of the Beacons

The lighting of the beacons is the fifth of the principal observances of the vigil, and the beacons are lit upon the sixth day at the sunset that marks the beginning of the final night of the observance. The beacons are the fires that are kindled upon the headlands at the customary points that the communities have established, and the beacons are of considerable size, the wood for them having been gathered in the weeks that precede the vigil. The beacons are lit from the embers of the household fires that have been carefully preserved through the year for this purpose, and the failure to preserve the embers is accounted a serious breach of the customs.

The beacons are visible from one end of the coast to the other, and the lighting of one beacon is the signal for the lighting of the next, so that a chain of fire runs along the cliffs from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait. The chain is a striking sight, and the mariners who are still at sea upon the night of the beacons have reported that the line of fire seems to extend without break from one horizon to the other. The beacons are permitted to burn down through the night, and the ashes that remain in the morning are gathered by the households and scattered upon the fields and the cliff gardens as a blessing upon the coming year's planting. The chronicler notes that the chain of beacons is the most visible of the observances of the vigil, and the lighting is the moment at which the communities of the coast are most visibly united in a single observance.

## The Closing

The closing of the vigil is conducted upon the seventh day, and the closing is the occasion upon which the community returns to the ordinary business of the winter season. The closing is marked by the customary ceremony that the elder conducts at the cairn upon the headland, and the ceremony consists of the recitation of the customary prayers and the customary songs that the community maintains. The closing is also the occasion for the customary procession that the crews conduct around the harbour, the crews walking in silence behind the elder with the lanterns that have burned through the week. The procession concludes at the boathouse, where the elder pronounces the customary words that release the community from the obligations of the vigil and returns the community to the customary life of the winter.

The closing is also the occasion for the customary hospitality that the [Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/open-hand) requires, and the households of the community receive the strangers and the travellers who have come to the coast for the season's last voyage. The strangers are welcomed at the feast that follows the closing, and the feast is the occasion for the exchange of the news of the wider world that the strangers bring from the ports of the south and the east. The chronicler notes that the closing is a moment of mixed character, for the relief that the season of danger has at last formally commenced is tempered by the recognition that the boats will not be launched again until the spring, and the families of the crews must sustain themselves through the long winter by the resources that they have laid up against the season.

## Variations among the Communities

The vigil is observed in different ways among the different communities of the coast, and the variations are a measure of the diversity of the customs of the settlements that share the observance. The communities of the eastern coast, which lie nearer the strait, observe the vigil at an earlier date than the communities of the western coast, and the customs of the eastern communities are influenced by the maritime culture of the strait towns. The communities of the western headlands, which face the open waters of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), observe the vigil at a later date, and the customs of the western communities retain elements of the older rites of the cliff caves that the scholars have noted. The communities of the fjords of the central coast observe the vigil at a date that lies between the dates of the eastern and the western communities, and the customs of the fjord communities combine elements of both.

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain holds of the interior do not observe the vigil, for the dwarven communities do not dwell upon the coast and the customs of the dwarven year are ordered by the rhythm of the underground workings. The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the inland towns of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) observe their own customs at the onset of the winter, and the customs of the inland towns differ from the customs of the coast in the manner that the conditions of the two regions require. The chronicler notes that the vigil is a distinctively coastal observance, and the customs of the vigil are not shared with the communities of the interior, for the conditions of life upon the coast and the conditions of life in the interior are too different to admit of a single observance for both.

## Significance

The Shoreline Vigil is the principal late-autumn observance of the fishing and trading communities of the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast) of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and the vigil is observed by every settlement of the coast from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait. The vigil is an acknowledgement of the hazards of the season that is beginning, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the safety of the boats that must be hauled up before the storms and the welfare of the crews that will not put to sea again until the spring. The chronicler notes that the vigil has acquired a civic as well as a religious significance that is recognised by the authorities of the [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) in which it is observed, and the lighting of the beacons is one of the customary acts of the coastal communities at the onset of the storm season. The vigil endures as a celebration of the endurance of the coastal peoples and as a reminder that the sea that sustains them through the summer is the sea that endangers them through the winter.
