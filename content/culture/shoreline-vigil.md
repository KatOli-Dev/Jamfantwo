## Overview

The Shoreline Vigil is the principal late-autumn observance of the fishing and trading communities of the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast) of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed by the families and crews of every settlement from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) during the week in which the autumn gales are expected to begin. The vigil is observed in every harbour and cliff village of the coast, though customs vary from one community to another. It acknowledges the hazards of the coming season, and its rituals are directed toward the safety of the boats that must be hauled up before the storms and the welfare of the crews that will not put to sea again until the spring.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the vigil are prehistoric. Scholars of the eastern coast of [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) propose that it was originally a practical observance of the fisherfolk, marking the moment when the boats must be drawn up from the water and the gear stowed for the winter. Scholars of the western coast argue that the vigil is older than the settled villages, descending from a rite of the prehistoric peoples of the cliff caves who watched the storms from the high rocks and counted the safe returns of their kin. Regardless of its origins, the vigil is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The vigil begins upon the day the elders of each community appoint, chosen with reference to the observed behaviour of the weather and the migrations of the seabirds that the fishers take as their signs. The commencement varies along the coast, as the autumn gales reach the eastern communities several days before they reach the western. The vigil is observed for a period of seven days, and the seventh day is the closing, marked by customary feast and ceremonies.

## The Drawing Up of the Boats

The drawing up of the boats is the first principal observance, conducted upon the first day by the crews of every fishing vessel. The boats are hauled from the water upon the customary slips the communities maintain, with the assistance of the households and neighbours. The crews sing customary songs as they haul, a measure of the relief that the season of danger is at last being brought to a close. The drawing up of the boats is observed with great strictness, for the boats are the foundation of the livelihood of the people and their loss in the coming storms would be a calamity no household could easily bear.

Once the boats are drawn up, the gear is stripped and stowed in the customary storehouses maintained above the high-water mark. Nets are hung upon racks to dry, sails folded and taken into the boathouses, oars and masts laid up in the places the customs of the community have established. The masts of larger vessels are unstepped and laid beside the hulls, and the vessels are blocked up upon timbers to hold them off the ground through the winter. The labour of stowing is shared among the households according to the customary division the community enforces.

## The Watch Upon the Cliffs

The watch upon the cliffs is the second principal observance, maintained by the young men and women of the community through the hours of darkness from the first day to the seventh. The watchers take their station upon the high rocks commanding the approaches to the harbour, maintaining the watch in pairs, each pair relieved after a turn of four hours. The watchers carry customary lanterns lit from the embers of the household fires, hung upon hooks fixed in the rock. The lanterns are visible far out at sea, and their light is the first sign a vessel returning late from the fishing grounds sees as it approaches the land.

The watch is not merely a practical measure for guiding returning vessels. It is a vigil in the older sense — a keeping of the night in contemplation of the year that is passing and the year to come. The watchers are expected to maintain silence during their turns of duty, observed with strictness. They may speak only to give customary signals — a small repertoire of calls and lamp-flashes established by the elders. The watch is regarded as a duty of considerable honour, and the young people who serve upon the cliffs are held to have discharged an obligation the community recognises and remembers.

## The Memorial for the Lost

The memorial for the lost is the third principal observance, conducted upon the third day at the customary place each community maintains. The customary place is typically a cairn erected upon the headland overlooking the harbour, bearing the names of crews and vessels lost to the storms of the coast in years past. The memorial is attended by the families of the lost and by members of the community who wish to pay their respects, conducted by an appointed elder.

The memorial consists of the recitation of the names inscribed upon the cairn, accompanied by the casting of a sprig of evergreen into the waters of the harbour. The evergreen is gathered from the wind-shrub of the cliff tops, chosen by the family of one of the lost whose name is recited in the course of the ceremony. The casting of the sprig is the formal moment of the memorial, marked by customary words from the elder acknowledging the lives lost and wishing peace for the lost in the waters that were the principal theatre of their labours. The memorial is concluded by the singing of the customary lament the coastal communities have maintained as long as the customs can be traced.

## The Feast of the Last Catch

The feast of the last catch is the fourth principal observance, held upon the fifth day in the boathouse the community has set aside for the purpose. The feast is prepared from the fish and shellfish the crews took in the final days before the drawing up of the boats, prepared according to the dishes the customs of the coast have established. The principal dish is salt cod cured in the fashion of the coast, served with root vegetables from the cliff gardens and flat bread baked by the households. The ale is the last of the season's brewing, and its quality is a matter of household pride and friendly rivalry among neighbours.

The feast is attended by the crews and their households, and is the occasion for customary gifts exchanged at the close of the fishing season. The gifts are typically of a practical nature — tools and clothing the recipient will require in the winter — accompanied by a word of blessing from the giver. The feast is also the occasion for customary songs recounting the events of the season that is ending and the deeds of the crews that worked the waters.

## The Lighting of the Beacons

The lighting of the beacons is the fifth principal observance, the beacons lit upon the sixth day at the sunset marking the beginning of the final night of the observance. The beacons are fires kindled upon the headlands at customary points established by the communities, of considerable size, the wood gathered in the weeks preceding the vigil. The beacons are lit from the embers of the household fires preserved through the year, and the failure to preserve the embers is accounted a serious breach of customs.

The beacons are visible from one end of the coast to the other, and the lighting of one beacon signals the lighting of the next, creating a chain of fire running along the cliffs from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait. The chain is a striking sight; mariners still at sea upon the night of the beacons have reported that the line of fire seems to extend without break from one horizon to the other. The beacons burn through the night, and the ashes remaining in the morning are gathered by the households and scattered upon the fields and cliff gardens as a blessing upon the coming year's planting. The chain of beacons is the most visible observance of the vigil, the moment at which the communities of the coast are most visibly united in a single observance.

## The Closing

The closing of the vigil is conducted upon the seventh day, the occasion upon which the community returns to the ordinary business of the winter season. The closing is marked by a ceremony conducted by the elder at the cairn upon the headland, consisting of the recitation of customary prayers and songs. The closing is also the occasion for a customary procession of the crews around the harbour, walking in silence behind the elder with the lanterns that have burned through the week. The procession concludes at the boathouse, where the elder pronounces the words that release the community from the obligations of the vigil and returns them to the customary life of the winter.

The closing is also the occasion for the customary hospitality the [Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/open-hand) requires. Households receive the strangers and travellers who have come to the coast for the season's last voyage, welcoming them at the feast that follows the closing. The feast is the occasion for the exchange of news of the wider world brought by strangers from the ports of the south and east. The closing is a moment of mixed character: the relief that the season of danger has formally commenced is tempered by the recognition that the boats will not be launched again until the spring, and the families of the crews must sustain themselves through the long winter by the resources they have laid up.

## Variations among the Communities

Communities of the eastern coast, nearer the strait, observe the vigil at an earlier date than those of the western coast, and their customs are influenced by the maritime culture of the strait towns. Communities of the western headlands, facing the open waters of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), observe the vigil at a later date, retaining elements of the older rites of the cliff caves. Communities of the fjords of the central coast observe the vigil at a date between the eastern and western communities, combining elements of both.

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain holds of the interior do not observe the vigil, as they do not dwell upon the coast and their year is ordered by the rhythm of the underground workings. The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the inland towns of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) observe their own customs at the onset of winter, differing from the customs of the coast in the manner the conditions of the two regions require. The vigil is a distinctively coastal observance, not shared with the communities of the interior.

## Significance

The Shoreline Vigil is the principal late-autumn observance of the fishing and trading communities of the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast) of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed by every settlement from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait. The vigil acknowledges the hazards of the coming season, and its rituals are directed toward the safety of the boats and the welfare of the crews. The vigil holds both civic and religious significance, recognised by the authorities of [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven), and the lighting of the beacons is one of the customary acts of the coastal communities at the onset of the storm season. The vigil endures as a celebration of the endurance of the coastal peoples, affirming that the sea that sustains them through the summer is the sea that endangers them through the winter.
