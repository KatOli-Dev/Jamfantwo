## Overview

The Feast of the First Grain is the principal late-summer observance of the agricultural communities of the central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), celebrated by the farmers and the townsfolk of the river valleys and the grainlands of the [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael), the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic), and the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) upon the day that the first sheaves of the new harvest are brought in from the fields. The feast is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone, though the customs that accompany it vary from one people to another, and the chronicler notes that no two communities observe the feast in precisely the same manner. The feast is above all a celebration of the renewal of the earth and of the labour that has brought the harvest to ripeness, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the formal acknowledgement of the bounty that the year has provided.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the feast are lost in the pre-historic period, and the chroniclers of the different nations offer different accounts of its founding. The scholars of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) have proposed that the feast was originally a religious observance of the early agricultural peoples, a marking of the moment when the first fruits of the harvest were presented at the temple in thanksgiving for the year's yield. The scholars of the [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) have proposed that the feast is older than the temple observances, and that it is descended from a domestic ritual of the pre-historic farmsteads in which the first sheaf was reserved for the household hearth. The scholars of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) have proposed that the feast was originally a pastoral observance that was adapted to the agricultural life when the plains peoples began to cultivate the margins of the grasslands.

Whatever its origins, the feast has acquired a meaning that is independent of the theories of its founding, and the feast is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The feast is reckoned to take place upon the day that the elders of each community have appointed for the commencement of the harvest, and the day is typically chosen with reference to the ripeness of the grain in the fields that lie nearest the settlement. The first sheaf is cut by the elder of the community in a ceremony that the chronicler will describe below, and the cutting of the sheaf is the signal for the commencement of the general harvest that follows in the days and weeks after the feast.

## The Cutting of the First Sheaf

The cutting of the first sheaf is the first of the principal observances of the feast, and the cutting is performed by the elder of the community at the field that has been chosen for the purpose. The field is typically a small plot that lies nearest the settlement and that has been set aside for the ceremonial harvest, and the grain of the plot is allowed to ripen a few days earlier than the grain of the surrounding fields to ensure that it is ready for the cutting upon the appointed day. The elder is accompanied by the members of the household whose land the plot represents, and the household stands in a circle around the elder while the cutting is performed.

The elder cuts the first sheaf with the customary sickle that the community has maintained for the purpose, and the sickle is a tool of considerable antiquity that is preserved in the household of the elder from one generation to the next. The elder speaks the customary words as the sickle is laid to the grain, and the words are an invocation of the fertility of the earth and a prayer for the bounty of the harvest that is to follow. The first sheaf is then bound by the elder in the customary fashion and is presented to the head of the household, who receives it with the customary gesture of thanks. The chronicler notes that the cutting of the first sheaf is observed with great solemnity in every community of the agricultural regions, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the farming year.

## The Procession to the Granary

The procession to the granary is the second of the principal observances of the feast, and the procession is conducted by the members of the community from the field where the sheaf was cut to the granary where the harvest will be stored. The procession is led by the elder who cut the sheaf, and the elder carries the sheaf upon a pole that has been decorated with the ribbons and the flowers of the season. The members of the community follow the elder in the customary order, the order being determined by the seniority of the households and the customary precedence that the community enforces. The procession is accompanied by the music of the pipes and the drums, and the music continues until the granary is reached.

The granary is the principal storehouse of the community, and the granary is the building in which the harvest of the year will be kept until the grain is required for the bread of the households. The granary is opened for the occasion, and the elder carries the sheaf into the granary and lays it upon the threshing floor that occupies the centre of the building. The laying of the sheaf upon the floor is the formal moment of the procession, and the moment is marked by the customary words that the elder addresses to the assembly. The words are an invocation of the protection of the grain through the months of storage and a prayer for the renewal of the harvest in the year that is to come.

## The Blessing of the Loaves

The blessing of the loaves is the third of the principal observances of the feast, and the blessing is conducted at the communal oven where the bread of the feast is to be baked. The loaves are prepared from the grain of the first sheaf, and the loaves are shaped in the customary forms that the community has established for the occasion. The most common form is the shape of a wheel or a sun, and the shape of the loaf is a reminder of the celestial body whose heat has ripened the grain. The loaves are placed upon the stone of the oven, and the elder pronounces the customary blessing upon the loaves before the fire is kindled.

The baking of the loaves is conducted by the bakers of the community, and the bakers are skilled in the management of the oven and the timing of the bake. The loaves are watched throughout the baking, and the moment at which the crust turns the colour of ripened wheat is the moment at which the loaves are pronounced ready. The loaves are then drawn from the oven and laid upon the boards that have been set out for the purpose, and the loaves are allowed to cool before they are broken for the feast. The chronicler notes that the bread of the feast is reckoned to have a particular virtue that the bread of the ordinary baking does not possess, and the bread is shared among the households with a care that the customs of the community require.

## The Feast and the Sharing

The feast that follows the blessing of the loaves is the principal social occasion of the harvest season, and the feast is attended by the members of the community and by the visitors who have come to the settlement from the neighbouring towns. The principal dish of the feast is the bread that has been blessed in the ceremony, and the bread is broken by the elder and distributed to the guests by the servants of the community. The bread is accompanied by the first fruits of the harvest that the households have brought to the feast, including the early vegetables, the herbs of the kitchen garden, and the honey of the season's first extraction. The ale and the mead that are served at the feast are brewed from the grain of the previous year's harvest, and the quality of the brewing is a matter of household pride.

The feast is also the occasion for the customary gifts that the households exchange at the harvest, and the gifts are typically the produce of the farms and the gardens that the households have tended through the year. The gifts are presented in a formal ceremony that is conducted by the elder, and the ceremony is one of the most moving of the observances of the feast. The chronicler notes that the feast is a celebration of the generosity of the earth and of the labour of the households, and the sharing of the gifts is a measure of the bond that the community recognises among its members. The feast is conducted with a strictness of form that the customs of the community enforce, and the form is observed from the opening invocation to the closing word of thanks.

## The Dance and the Contests

The dance and the contests are the customary entertainments that accompany the feast, and the entertainments are conducted in the evening after the feast itself has concluded. The dance is led by the elder and the head of the household whose sheaf was the first to be cut, and the couple is joined in the dance by the members of the community in a sequence that is determined by the customary order of the households. The dance is accompanied by the music of the pipes and the drums, and the music continues until the small hours of the morning. The dance is also the occasion for the customary songs that the community maintains for the harvest, and the songs recount the events of the farming year and the deeds of the households that worked the land.

The contests are conducted in the customary forms that the agricultural communities have established, and the contests include the wrestling, the foot race, and the sheaf-throwing that are the customary demonstrations of the vigour of the community. The contests are judged by the elders of the community who have been appointed for the purpose, and the prizes that are awarded to the victors are the customary tokens of the harvest, including a sheaf of the new grain, a loaf of the blessed bread, and a measure of the ale of the feast. The chronicler notes that the contests are observed with a friendly rivalry that the customs of the community permit, and the contests are an occasion for the display of the strength and the skill that the farming life requires.

## The Distribution to the Stranger

The distribution to the stranger is the final of the principal observances of the feast, and the distribution is conducted at the gate of the settlement or at the customary place that the community has established for the purpose. The distribution is required by the [Doctrine of the Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/doctrine-of-the-open-hand), and the doctrine requires that the stranger and the traveller be received at the feast and provided with a share of the food and the merriment. The households of the community take it in turn to provide the lodging for the visitors who come to the feast, and the hospitality is extended without regard to the rank or the nation of the visitor.

The distribution is conducted by the elder of the community, and the elder presents to each visitor a loaf of the blessed bread and a measure of the ale of the feast. The elder addresses the customary words to the visitor as the gift is presented, and the words are a welcome to the settlement and a wish for the welfare of the visitor upon the journey that lies ahead. The chronicler notes that the distribution is one of the most distinctive of the observances of the feast, and the custom of the distribution is observed with great strictness in every community that recognises the Doctrine of the Open Hand. The visitors who have received the distribution are accustomed to speak well of the welcome they have received, and the reputation of the community is enhanced by the manner in which the distribution is conducted.

## Variations among the Peoples

The feast is observed in different ways among the different peoples of the known world, and the variations are a measure of the diversity of the customs of the agricultural communities that share the celebration. The [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) observes the feast at the margin of the grasslands where the cultivation has been established, and the customs of the plains combine the agricultural rites of the harvest with the pastoral rites of the herd. The [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) observes the feast with a ceremony that includes the formal presentation of the first sheaf at the temple, and the customs of the republic reflect the maritime character of the city that has adapted the agricultural rites to the conditions of the urban community.

The communities of the [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) observe the feast in the form that the central lowlands have established, and the customs of the kingdom are observed in every farming settlement from the western foothills to the eastern lowlands. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) do not observe the feast in the form that the surface peoples have established, for the dwarven communities do not practise the agriculture of the lowlands and the customs of the dwarven year are ordered by the rhythm of the underground workings. The dwarves mark the harvest season with their own observances, and the observances are adapted to the conditions of the mountain life.

## Significance

The Feast of the First Grain is the principal late-summer observance of the agricultural communities of the central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the feast is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The feast is a celebration of the renewal of the earth and of the labour that has brought the harvest to ripeness, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the formal acknowledgement of the bounty that the year has provided. The chronicler notes that the feast has acquired a civic as well as a religious significance that is recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed, and the cutting of the first sheaf is one of the customary acts of the agricultural communities at the beginning of the harvest season. The feast endures as a celebration of the bond between the people and the earth and as a reminder that the grain that sustains the households through the winter is the gift of the soil and of the labour that the soil requires.
