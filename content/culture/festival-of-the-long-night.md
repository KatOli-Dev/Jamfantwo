## Overview

The Festival of the Long Night is the principal midwinter celebration of the peoples of the central and northern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed on the shortest day of the year when the sun is at its lowest and the hours of daylight are at their fewest. The festival is observed in every nation of the temperate zone and in many of the communities of the northern coast, though the customs that accompany the celebration vary from one people to another and the chronicler notes that no two communities observe the festival in precisely the same manner. The festival is above all a celebration of the endurance of the light through the longest of the nights, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the renewal of the sun's strength and the return of the longer days.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the festival are lost in the mists of the pre-historic period, and the chroniclers of the different nations offer different accounts of its founding. The scholars of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) have proposed that the festival was originally an agricultural observance, a marking of the moment when the preparations for the spring planting must begin if the crops are to be ready for the harvest of the coming year. The scholars of the [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) have proposed that the festival is older than the agriculture of the lowlands, and that it is descended from a hunting ritual of the pre-historic peoples of the forest. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) observe a festival of their own upon the same day, and the dwarven festival is reckoned by the mountain chroniclers to be older than any of the human celebrations.

Whatever its origins, the festival has acquired a meaning that is independent of the theories of its founding. The festival is the celebration of the longest night and the first turning of the year toward the spring, and the rituals that accompany it are the rituals of hope and of endurance. The chronicler notes that the festival is observed by peoples of every religion and of none, and the festival has acquired a civic as well as a religious significance that is recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed. The proclamation of the festival is one of the customary acts of the rulers of the central kingdoms at the winter solstice, and the proclamation is accompanied by the lighting of the great fires that are the most visible of the festival's observances.

## The Lighting of the Fires

The central rite of the festival is the lighting of the fires that are kindled upon the hills and the high places of every community that observes the celebration. The fires are lit at the moment of the sunset upon the shortest day, and the lighting is the signal for the commencement of the feasting and the merry-making that accompany the festival. The fires are kindled from the embers of the previous year's hearth-fires, which have been carefully preserved through the year for this purpose, and the preservation of the embers is one of the customary duties of the head of every household. The failure to preserve the embers is accounted a serious breach of the festival's customs, and the household that has let its embers die is subject to a fine that is levied by the community.

The fires are of considerable size, and the wood that is consumed in them represents a substantial proportion of the timber that the community has gathered for the purpose. The gathering of the wood is itself a customary observance of the weeks that precede the festival, and the parties that go out to the forests and the hillsides for the wood are occasions of a communal activity that is in itself a part of the festival. The fires are permitted to burn down through the night, and the ashes that remain in the morning are distributed among the households of the community, to be scattered upon the fields and the gardens as a blessing upon the coming year's planting. The distribution of the ashes is one of the closing rites of the festival, and the rite is performed at the dawn of the day that follows the longest night.

## The Feast and the Giving of Gifts

The feast that accompanies the festival is the principal social occasion of the winter season, and the feast is prepared with a care that the household observes throughout the year in anticipation of the celebration. The principal dish of the feast is the roast meat of the season, and the meat is accompanied by the preserved fruits and the root vegetables that have been gathered in the autumn and stored for the winter. The bread of the feast is baked in the form of a wheel or a sun, and the shape of the loaf is a reminder of the celestial body whose return the festival celebrates. The spiced ale and the mead that are served at the feast are brewed in the weeks that precede the festival, and the quality of the brewing is a matter of household pride that is the subject of much friendly rivalry among the neighbours.

The giving of gifts is another of the customs that accompany the festival, and the gifts are exchanged among the members of the household and the members of the wider community at the feast. The gifts are typically of a practical nature, including the tools and the clothing that the recipient will require in the coming year, and the gifts are accompanied by a word of blessing that the giver addresses to the recipient. The giving of gifts is not confined to the members of the community, for the custom of the [Doctrine of the Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/doctrine-of-the-open-hand) requires that the stranger and the traveller be received at the feast and provided with a share of the food and the merriment, and the households of the community take it in turn to provide the lodging for the visitors who come to the celebration.

## The Vigil and the Watch

The vigil is the solemn counterpart of the merry-making that accompanies the festival, and the vigil is observed in the hours between the lighting of the fires and the dawn of the following day. The vigil is maintained by the elders and the councillors of the community, who gather at the principal fire and watch through the night in contemplation of the year that is ending and the year that is to come. The vigil is also the occasion for the reading of the chronicles of the community, and the deeds of the ancestors are recalled for the benefit of the young who are present. The chronicler notes that the vigil is a custom that is observed with greater strictness in some communities than in others, but the custom is sufficiently widespread to be reckoned among the principal observances of the festival.

The watch is maintained upon the walls of the towns and the boundaries of the communities, for the longest night is also the occasion upon which the spirits of the cold and the dark are believed to be most active. The watch is conducted by the young men of the community, who patrol the boundaries with torches and weapons, and the watch is renewed at intervals through the night. The watch is not a military operation in the strict sense, for the spirits that are feared are not the kind that may be turned aside by force of arms, but the watch is a symbol of the community's vigilance against the dangers of the season. The chronicler notes that the watch has acquired, in the more sober communities, a practical dimension, for the patrols are useful in the detection of the fires that may have escaped from the principal bonfires and that require the attention of the watchmen.

## Variations among the Peoples

The festival is observed in different ways among the different peoples of the known world, and the variations are a measure of the diversity of the customs of the nations that share the celebration. The [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) observes the festival upon the open grasslands, and the lighting of the fires is performed at the great camps where the people of the plains gather for the winter. The fires of the plains are the largest of any that are kindled upon the festival, and the light of the fires may be seen at a distance of many miles across the open country. The mounted warriors of the plains conduct a ceremonial ride upon the day of the festival, and the ride is reckoned among the most impressive of the displays of horsemanship in the known world.

The [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) observe a festival of their own upon the same day, though the Kharesh festival is more concerned with the spirits of the desert than with the sun of the temperate zones. The festival of the desert is a celebration of the endurance of the people through the cold nights of the desert winter, and the lighting of the fires is performed at the oases and the wells that are the gathering points of the tribes. The chronicler notes that the festival is also observed in the coastal cities of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), though the customs of the coastal cities are influenced by the maritime culture of the littoral and the celebration has acquired some of the character of the festivals of the sea.

## Significance

The Festival of the Long Night is the principal midwinter celebration of the temperate and the northern regions of the known world, and the festival is observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The festival is a celebration of the endurance of the light through the longest of the nights, and the rituals that accompany it are directed toward the renewal of the sun's strength and the return of the longer days. The chronicler notes that the festival has acquired a civic as well as a religious significance that is recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed, and the lighting of the fires is one of the customary acts of the rulers of the central kingdoms at the winter solstice. The festival endures as a celebration of the turning of the year and as a reminder that the longest night is followed by the return of the light.
