## The Dwarven Decorative Tradition

The Inlay Art is the principal decorative tradition of the [dwarven](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) communities of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), a body of practice and custom that has grown up over the centuries around the working of the metals and stones that the dwarven mines produce. The art encompasses the inlay of metal into stone, stone into metal, and one stone into another, and its techniques are transmitted from master to apprentice through the long apprenticeships that dwarven crafts require. The art is among the most distinctive of dwarven cultural traditions, and the works produced under it are among the most valued of dwarven exports to the surface world.

## Practical Necessity Becoming Craft

The origins of the art lie in the practical necessities of dwarven metalwork. Early dwarven smiths discovered that joints between metals and stones in their constructions could be strengthened and decorated by the careful fitting of one material into another. Over generations, these early practitioners developed a repertoire of techniques that were refined into the tradition observed in the present age. The tradition is not confined to metalwork and stonework of constructions; the techniques of inlay have been applied to smaller objects such as jewellery and personal ornaments produced for the surface trade.

The art is also a social practice of considerable importance in dwarven communities. It expresses the dwarven sense of the order and harmony of the materials the mountain provides. Dwarven craftsmen regard inlay as a means of revealing the inherent beauty of the materials, saying that the inlay brings the materials into the relationship they were always intended to have. This sense of the materials is a distinctive feature of the dwarven aesthetic, and the works produced under the tradition measure the dwarven capacity to combine the practical and the decorative in a single act of making.

## Metals, Stones, and the Apprentice's Labour

The materials employed in inlay are the metals and stones that dwarven mines produce, prepared with a care that craftsmen regard as the foundation of the work. The most commonly employed metals are copper and silver, alloyed in the proportions the tradition requires. The most commonly employed stones are granite and marble, selected for colour and grain that will best display the figure of the inlay. Apprentices bound to the master of the craft prepare the materials under strict supervision.

The preparation includes cutting the metal and stone to the precise dimensions the design requires. Metal is cut with chisel and file, stone with saw and grinder. The cutting is a delicate operation requiring a steady hand and precise eye; apprentices serve a long period of training before they are entrusted with the more valuable materials. The quality of the cutting is the measure of the craftsman's skill.

## Pouring Molten Metal into Channels

The inlay of metal into stone is the most ancient technique of the art, employed in the decoration of the great halls and public buildings of dwarven communities. A channel is cut in the surface of the stone, and the channel is filled with prepared metal. The metal is heated until molten, poured into the channel, and allowed to cool slowly, which brings the metal into a close and uniform bond with the surrounding stone. The surface is then polished with abrasives developed for the purpose, revealing the figure of the inlay in its final form.

The designs are typically geometric, including the interlaced patterns that are a distinctive feature of the dwarven decorative tradition. Figurative designs include representations of the creatures of the mountain and scenes from the history of dwarven communities, common features of the great halls. The master of the craft draws the designs upon the surface of the stone, and the drawing is the pattern the apprentices follow in cutting the channel. The masters say that the drawing is the soul of the inlay.

## Setting Stone into Recessed Metal

The inlay of stone into metal is a more recent development, employed in the decoration of smaller objects produced for the surface trade. A recess is cut in the surface of the metal, and the recess is filled with prepared stone cut to precise dimensions. The stone is set into the recess and secured with adhesive developed by dwarven craftsmen. The surface is polished with abrasives, revealing the figure of the inlay in its final form.

The designs are typically more delicate than those of metal into stone, including floral and foliate patterns that are a distinctive feature of smaller objects of the dwarven decorative tradition. Figurative designs include representations of the creatures of the mountain and scenes from dwarven legends, common on personal ornaments. The master of the craft draws the designs upon the surface of the metal. The inlay of stone into metal requires a particularly precise eye, and the masters say that this technique is the test of the craftsman's skill.

## How the Skill Passes from Master to Apprentice

The transmission of the art from master to apprentice occurs through long apprenticeships that dwarven crafts require. The apprentice is bound to the master at the age of twelve or thirteen and serves for twenty years or more before being admitted to the rank of journeyman. The journeyman serves for a further period before admission to the rank of master, the formal recognition of the journeyman's competence to practice the craft independently. The long apprenticeship measures the complexity of the craft and is regarded as the foundation of the quality of the work.

The art is also transmitted through the works the masters have produced, preserved in the halls of the mountain. These works serve as reference for apprentices learning the techniques of the craft and as the standard by which masters are judged. The works are displayed in the halls of the mountain as a measure of the achievement of the masters who produced them, and they form the foundation of the reputation of the mountain in the surface world. Masters maintain detailed pattern-books in which the designs of notable inlays are recorded, and these books are themselves regarded as treasures of the hold, consulted by aspiring craftsmen seeking to understand the principles of composition that distinguish the finest work.

## The Art's Meaning to the Dwarven People

The Inlay Art is the principal decorative tradition of the dwarven communities of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and the art is among the most distinctive of dwarven cultural traditions. The works produced under the tradition are among the most valued of dwarven exports to the surface world, and the art measures the dwarven capacity to combine the practical and the decorative in a single act of making. The art endures as a tradition of the mountain, affirming that the materials the earth provides are capable of a beauty that the hand of the craftsman may reveal.
