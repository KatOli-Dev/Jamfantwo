## Play Across the Known World

Games, sports, and pastimes occupy the hours that lie outside the labour of the field, the forge, and the counting house, and every people of the known world has developed forms of play suited to its resources, its climate, and its social structure. Some games test the strength or the skill of the body, others the memory or the wit, and still others depend on nothing more than chance and the willingness of the players to risk something of value on the outcome. The character of a people's games often reflects the character of its work: the herding peoples favour contests of horsemanship and endurance, the mercantile cities favour games of calculation and wagering, and the mountain halls favour games that can be played by lamplight in a confined space.

Games also serve purposes beyond amusement. They are used to settle minor disputes without recourse to violence, to train the young in skills they will need as adults, and to mark the passage of festivals and the changing of the seasons. A contest held at a fair or a gathering draws spectators as well as participants, and the reputation of a skilled player or a favoured champion can extend well beyond the household or the village in which the player was raised.

## The Counting-Boards of the Aldreth Realm

The towns and cities of [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) favour board games played on a grid of squares with pieces representing soldiers, merchants, or figures of the court. The most widespread of these is a game the Aldreth call the counting-board, played by two opponents who move pieces across a board of eight rows and eight columns, capturing an opponent's pieces by trapping them between two of the mover's own. The counting-board is found in the household of every rank, carved from wood in the homes of farmers and inlaid with ivory and coloured stone in the households of the nobility. Matches are conducted according to customary rules enforced by the players themselves, and disputes over an unclear move are commonly settled by an elder of the household rather than by any formal authority.

Public halls in [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) and [Caerwyn](/content/location/settlement/city/caerwyn) maintain permanent tables for the counting-board, where players of standing gather in the evenings to compete and to observe. A player who has defeated a succession of challengers at one of these tables acquires a local reputation, and matches between the leading players of rival halls are attended by crowds who wager on the outcome according to the customs the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) tolerate but do not formally regulate. Aldreth children learn a simplified version of the counting-board using painted pebbles before they are permitted to play with the carved pieces, and the game is regarded as part of a child's early education in patience and forethought.

## Games of Chance in the Sahrani City-States

The [Sahrani city-states](/content/culture/sahrani-city-life) of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) favour games of chance played with marked tiles or knucklebones, wagered for small sums in the courtyards of the suq and for considerably larger sums in the private rooms maintained by the merchant houses. The most common of these games involves the casting of a set of marked tiles and the reading of the resulting combination against an agreed table of values, a practice that Sahrani merchants have adapted into a method of settling minor disputes over the division of a shared cargo when a formal accounting would take longer than the parties are willing to wait.

Wrestling is also practised in the Sahrani city-states, conducted in a marked circle in the open ground outside the city walls, where a challenger may call out an opponent before an assembled crowd. The bout ends when one wrestler is thrown to the ground or forced outside the circle, and champions of the wrestling ground are honoured with a share of the purse raised by the spectators. Sahrani wrestling is also a feature of the [Sands' Assembly](/content/culture/sands-assembly), where young men of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) compete before the gathered kindreds, though the desert form of the sport follows somewhat different customary rules from the city version and permits a wider range of throws.

## The Wrestling Grounds and Riddle-Contests of the Plains

The [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) and the human herders of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) hold wrestling and archery as the principal tests of a young adult's readiness to take a full share of the herding and defence of the camp, contests that are conducted at the summer gatherings alongside the horse fairs and races described among the [Plains' High Days](/content/culture/plains-high-days). Archery is judged both for accuracy at a fixed mark and for the ability to strike a moving target from horseback, a skill the plains peoples regard as inseparable from the defence of the herds against raiders and predators. Wrestling among the centaurs differs from the human form of the sport in that a bout may be won by forcing an opponent's forequarters to the ground, a rule that accounts for the centaur's greater mass and different centre of balance.

The evening gatherings of the plains camps also include riddle-contests, in which an elder poses a riddle drawn from the traditional store of the tribe and the assembled children compete to answer first. The riddle-contest is regarded as an extension of the oral education described in the account of [childhood and education](/content/culture/childhood-and-education), training memory and quickness of thought in a form the children experience as play rather than instruction.

## The Stone Games of the Dwarven Halls

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of [Stoneheim](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and [Karhold](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) favour games suited to play within the confines of the mountain halls, where the absence of open ground and the changing weather of the surface has little bearing on the choice of pastime. The most widespread of the dwarven games is played with a set of carved stone tokens on a board of concentric rings, in which one player commands a small force attempting to break through to the centre of the board while the opposing player commands a larger force attempting to encircle and immobilise the attacker before the breakthrough is achieved. The game is played at the stone tables described in the account of [dwarven hall life](/content/culture/dwarven-hall-life), often continuing across several sittings as the players deliberate each move at length.

Dwarven halls also hold contests of strength associated with the forge and the quarry, including the lifting and carrying of weighted stones and tests of endurance at the bellows, conducted at festivals when the ordinary labour of the hall is set aside. A dwarf who has proven exceptional strength in these contests is accorded a measure of standing within the hall independent of the dwarf's rank in the crafting hierarchy, though the standing gained from a contest of strength does not carry the same weight as mastery of a trade.

## Children's Games

Certain forms of play recur among the children of nearly every people of the known world with only minor variation: games of pursuit and evasion played in open ground, games in which a ball or a bundle of rags is thrown or kicked between players, and games of hiding and searching conducted around the buildings of a village or the wagons of an encampment. These games require no special equipment and are typically organised by the children themselves rather than by adult instruction, and they serve to develop the strength, the speed, and the cooperation that the children will need in adult life without being framed by the participants as training of any kind.

Children of the coastal towns supplement these common games with contests specific to their environment, including races along the shore and competitions to judge the distance a flat stone can be made to skip across calm water, while children of the mountain halls adapt the pursuit games to the tunnels and chambers of the dwarven halls, a version the dwarves regard with some anxiety on account of the many unlit side-passages into which an unwary child might wander.

## The Place of Play in the Known World

The games and pastimes of the known world, though varied in form, share a common function: they occupy the time not consumed by labour, they train the young in skills the community values, and they provide an outlet for rivalry and reputation that does not require recourse to arms or to the courts. A traveller moving between the realms of the known world encounters a different board, a different ball, and a different set of customary rules at every stop, but finds in each place the same underlying impulse to test skill against skill and chance against chance in the company of others.
