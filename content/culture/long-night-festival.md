## Overview

The Long Night Festival is the principal midwinter celebration of the peoples of the central and northern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed on the shortest day of the year when the sun is at its lowest and the hours of daylight are at their fewest. The festival is observed in every nation of the temperate zone and in many communities of the northern coast, though customs vary from one people to another. It celebrates the endurance of the light through the longest of the nights, and its rituals are directed toward the renewal of the sun's strength and the return of the longer days.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the festival are prehistoric. Scholars of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) propose that it was originally an agricultural observance, marking the moment when preparations for the spring planting must begin if the crops are to be ready for the harvest of the coming year. Scholars of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) argue that the festival is older than the agriculture of the lowlands, descending from a hunting ritual of the prehistoric forest peoples. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) observe a festival of their own upon the same day, reckoned by mountain chroniclers to be older than any of the human celebrations.

Regardless of its origins, the festival celebrates the longest night and the first turning of the year toward spring. The festival is observed by peoples of every religion and of none, and holds both civic and religious significance recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed. The proclamation of the festival is one of the customary acts of the rulers of the central kingdoms at the winter solstice, accompanied by the lighting of the great fires that are the most visible of the festival's observances.

## The Lighting of the Fires

The central rite of the festival is the lighting of fires kindled upon the hills and high places of every community that observes the celebration. The fires are lit at sunset on the shortest day, signalling the commencement of the feasting and merry-making. The fires are kindled from the embers of the previous year's hearth-fires, which have been carefully preserved through the year for this purpose; the preservation of the embers is one of the customary duties of the head of every household. A household that has let its embers die is subject to a fine levied by the community.

The fires are of considerable size, and the wood consumed represents a substantial proportion of the timber the community has gathered for the purpose. The gathering of the wood is itself a customary observance of the weeks preceding the festival, and the parties that go out to the forests and hillsides are occasions of communal activity that form a part of the festival. The fires are permitted to burn through the night, and the ashes remaining in the morning are distributed among the households to be scattered upon the fields and gardens as a blessing upon the coming year's planting. The distribution of the ashes is one of the closing rites, performed at dawn on the day following the longest night.

## The Feast and the Giving of Gifts

The feast is the principal social occasion of the winter season, prepared with care the household observes throughout the year in anticipation of the celebration. The principal dish is the roast meat of the season, accompanied by preserved fruits and root vegetables gathered in the autumn and stored for the winter. The bread is baked in the form of a wheel or a sun, recalling the celestial body whose return the festival celebrates. Spiced ale and mead are brewed in the weeks preceding the festival, and the quality of the brewing is a matter of household pride and friendly rivalry among neighbours.

The giving of gifts accompanies the festival, exchanged among the members of the household and the wider community at the feast. The gifts are typically of a practical nature — tools and clothing the recipient will require in the coming year — and are accompanied by a word of blessing from the giver. The custom of the [Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/open-hand) requires that strangers and travellers be received at the feast and provided with a share of food and merriment, and households take turns providing lodging for visitors.

## The Vigil and the Watch

The vigil is the solemn counterpart of the merry-making, observed in the hours between the lighting of the fires and the dawn of the following day. The elders and councillors of the community gather at the principal fire and watch through the night in contemplation of the year that is ending and the year to come. The vigil is also the occasion for the reading of the chronicles of the community, and the deeds of the ancestors are recalled for the benefit of the young who are present.

The watch is maintained upon the walls of the towns and the boundaries of the communities, for the longest night is the occasion upon which the spirits of the cold and the dark are believed to be most active. The young men of the community conduct the watch, patrolling the boundaries with torches and weapons, renewed at intervals through the night. The watch is not a military operation, as the spirits feared are not the kind that may be turned aside by force of arms, but it is a symbol of the community's vigilance against the dangers of the season. In the more sober communities, the watch also serves a practical purpose, as the patrols are useful in detecting fires that may have escaped from the principal bonfires.

## Variations among the Peoples

The [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) observes the festival upon the open grasslands, lighting the fires at the great camps where the people of the plains gather for the winter. The fires of the plains are the largest kindled upon the festival, and their light may be seen at a distance of many miles across the open country. The mounted warriors of the plains conduct a ceremonial ride upon the day of the festival, regarded as one of the most impressive displays of horsemanship in the known world.

The [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) observe a festival of their own upon the same day, more concerned with the spirits of the desert than with the sun of the temperate zones. The festival of the desert is a celebration of the endurance of the people through the cold nights of the desert winter, and the lighting of the fires is performed at the oases and wells that are the gathering points of the tribes. The festival is also observed in the coastal cities of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), though the customs of the coastal cities are influenced by the maritime culture of the littoral, and the celebration has acquired some of the character of the festivals of the sea.

## Significance

The Long Night Festival is the principal midwinter celebration of the temperate and northern regions of the known world, observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The festival holds both civic and religious significance, and the lighting of the fires is one of the customary acts of the rulers of the central kingdoms at the winter solstice. The festival endures as a celebration of the turning of the year, affirming that the longest night is followed by the return of the light.
