## Overview

Hands Joined is the principal marriage custom of the human communities of the temperate and northern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed by the families of those who have reached the age and condition that customary law requires for the formation of a marriage bond. The custom is observed in every nation of the temperate zone, though the precise form of the ceremony varies from one people to another. It acknowledges the bond between the two persons to be married and the obligations the bond entails, and the rituals are directed toward the formal recognition of the marriage by the community.

## Origins and Meaning

The origins of the custom are prehistoric. Scholars of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) propose that it began as a religious observance of the polytheistic peoples of the central lowlands, recognising that the formation of a household was a matter of significance to the gods of the community. Scholars of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) argue that the custom is older than settled agriculture, descending from a pastoral ritual of prehistoric grassland peoples. Regardless of its origins, the custom is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The marriageable age varies with community and climate. Those in the warmer southern regions marry at an earlier age than those in the colder north. The persons to be married must have reached the age of majority and demonstrated the competence to maintain a household. The consent of both families is a customary requirement enforced through the community's elders. Marriages between families of markedly different standing are uncommon in practice, for the contributions required of each side and the expectations of the community tend to keep unions within the same broad station.

## The Betrothal

The betrothal is the formal acknowledgement of the intention of the two persons to be married, conducted in the weeks or months preceding the ceremony. It is a contract between the two families specifying the date of the ceremony, the dwelling in which the newly married couple will reside, and the contributions each family will make to the establishment of the new household. Contributions typically include a portion of land or livestock from the bride's family, a sum of money or quantity of goods from the groom's family, and the customary gifts presented by the family and community upon the occasion of the ceremony.

The betrothal is conducted in the presence of the community elders and the heads of the two families, accompanied by the exchange of customary tokens. The groom gives a ring or bracelet to the bride, and the bride gives a cloak or garment to the groom. These tokens are not the wedding rings, which are exchanged at the ceremony itself, but are the first formal acknowledgement of the bond to be established. The betrothal is a binding contract in the customary law of the community, and breaking a betrothal is a serious matter that may require the intervention of the elders.

## The Ceremony

The central element of Hands Joined is the ceremony itself, conducted on a day appointed by the families and community elders. The day is typically in late spring or early summer, when the weather is favourable and the community gathers for the planting of the fields. The ceremony is held at the community's gathering place, attended by the families of the persons to be married and by the wider community. The ceremony is presided over by an appointed elder, assisted by the heads of the two families.

The ceremony begins with customary invocations and prayers, the form of which varies with the religion of the persons to be married. The vows follow, spoken by the two persons in turn, each addressing the other in the presence of the assembly. The vows are a pledge of fidelity, support, and companionship, spoken in a form established by long custom. The vows are followed by the exchange of wedding rings, placed upon the fingers of the two persons by the presiding elder. The exchange of rings is the formal moment of the marriage, marked by the customary words the elder addresses to the assembly.

## The Feast and the Customs

The feast that follows the ceremony is one of the principal social occasions of the community year, attended by the community and by visitors who have come to witness the occasion. The principal dish is the roast meat of the season, accompanied by breads and preserves prepared by the households of the community. The ale and mead are brewed in the weeks preceding the ceremony, and the quality of the brewing is a matter of household pride. The feast is also the occasion for the giving of customary gifts, presented to the newly married couple by the community in a formal ceremony conducted by the elder.

Customs accompanying the feast vary from one community to another, but the most common is the evening dance. The newly married couple leads the dance, joined by the community in sequence determined by customary order, accompanied by the music of pipes and drums that continues until the small hours. The dance is also the occasion for customary games and contests, including wrestling and foot races, which demonstrate the vigour of the community.

## Variations among the Peoples

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) observe a custom adapted to underground life, conducted in the hall of the hold to which the two persons belong. The dwarven custom does not include a feast but includes a binding oath spoken before the elders of the hold, which is the formal acknowledgement of the bond. The elders give gifts, typically the tools of the craft to which the two persons are bound.

The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and other elven communities of the central woodlands conduct the ceremony in the deep wood, attended only by the families of the two persons and the elders. Vows are spoken in the presence of the great trees, in the elven language rather than the [Trade Tongue](/content/language/pseudo/trade-tongue). The elven custom does not include a feast but includes the giving of gifts, typically the products of the forest.

In the maritime communities of the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast), the ceremony includes an additional element in which the couple exchanges a length of rope that has been braided from fibres gathered from both families' boats, symbolising the binding of two households and two vessels. This rope is later displayed in the couple's home as a token of the marriage.

## Significance

Hands Joined is the principal marriage custom of the human communities of the temperate and northern regions of the known world, observed by peoples of every religion and of none. The custom holds both civic and religious significance, recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed, and the ceremony is one of the customary acts of the community at the formation of a new household. The custom endures as a celebration of the bond between two persons and as a reminder that the household is the foundation of the community.
