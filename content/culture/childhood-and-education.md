## The Beginnings of Life

Childhood across the known world is recognised as a distinct stage of life, a period of growth and learning that prepares the young for the responsibilities of adulthood. The length and the character of childhood vary considerably from one people to another, reflecting the different conditions and values of each culture, but the fundamental purpose of childhood is the same everywhere: the transmission of the skills and the knowledge that the child will need to become a functioning member of the community.

The earliest years of childhood are the same across most cultures. The infant is cared for by the mother and the other women of the household, nursed and protected and gradually introduced to the world beyond the immediate circle of the family. The first words, the first steps, and the first signs of understanding are celebrated by the family as the markers of the child's progress toward independence. The customs that attend these early achievements vary from one community to another, but the joy that they bring to the family is universal.

## The Education of the Vaelic Child

In the Vael river valley, the education of the child begins at the age of five or six, when the child is introduced to the tasks of the household and the fields. The child of the farming household learns the work of the farm by participating in it, starting with the simpler tasks of tending the smaller animals and gathering the eggs and the vegetables from the garden and progressing to the more demanding work of the fields as the child grows older.

The formal education of the Vaelic child is conducted by the priest of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame), who teaches the children of the parish the principles of the faith and the elementary skills of reading and writing. The school is held in the temple or in the village hall, and the children attend in the winter months when the demands of the farm work are lighter. The curriculum includes the memorisation of the scriptures of the Still Flame, the learning of the basic prayers and the rituals, and the study of the traditional histories of the valley.

The education of the girls of the Vael valley includes additional instruction in the domestic arts: the weaving, the preserving, and the management of the household that will be the girl's responsibility as an adult. The education of the boys includes instruction in the crafts and trades that the community requires: the smithing, the carpentry, and the stonework that are the foundation of the valley's material culture.

## The Childhood of the Plains Peoples

The children of the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) and the human herders of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) grow up in the open air, learning the skills of the nomadic life from their earliest years. The centaur foal learns to walk and to run within hours of birth, and the centaur child is expected to be capable of travelling with the herd by the age of three or four. The human child of the plains is riding the small ponies of the grasslands by the age of five or six, learning to manage the horses and the sheep that will be the foundation of the child's adult livelihood.

The education of the plains child is conducted by the elders of the tribe, who teach the children the traditional knowledge of the grasslands: the locations of the waters and the pastures, the habits of the animals, and the skills of the hunt. The children also learn the histories and the legends of the tribe, the genealogies that connect the present generation to the ancestors, and the customs that govern the conduct of the tribe. The education is conducted in the evening gatherings at the campfire, when the elders recite the traditional stories and the children are expected to repeat them until they are memorised.

## The Apprenticeship of the Dwarven Child

The childhood of the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) is shorter than the childhood of the human peoples, reflecting the faster development of the dwarven body and the demands of the apprenticeship system that is the foundation of dwarven education. The dwarven child is considered ready for apprenticeship at the age of twelve or thirteen, when the child is bound to a master of the craft that the child will pursue for the remainder of life.

The apprenticeship is a period of strict discipline and intensive training. The apprentice lives in the master's household, learning the techniques and the secrets of the craft through direct participation in the work. The apprentice is required to master the skills of the forge, the stonework, or the craft to which the master is devoted, and the progress of the apprentice is measured by the works that the apprentice produces. The apprenticeship lasts for twenty years or more, and the apprentice is not considered a full member of the dwarven community until the apprenticeship is completed and the apprentice is admitted to the rank of journeyman.

## The Education of the Sahrani Child

The children of the [Sahrani city-states](/content/culture/sahrani-city-life) are educated in the traditions of the mercantile culture that is the foundation of the Sahrani civilisation. The child of the merchant household learns the principles of accounting and the management of trade from the earliest years, assisting in the counting house and accompanying the parent to the suq and the harbour.

The formal education of the Sahrani child is conducted by the scribes of the temple, who teach the children of the city the skills of reading and writing in the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) language and the [Trade Tongue](/content/language/pseudo/trade-tongue). The curriculum includes the study of the traditional histories of the city-states, the principles of the law that governs the trade, and the religious traditions that are the foundation of the community's identity.

## The Coming of Age

The transition from childhood to adulthood is marked by the [Coming of Age Rites](/content/culture/coming-of-age-rites) that are observed by every people of the known world. The precise form of the rite varies from one culture to another, but the purpose is the same: the formal recognition of the child's attainment of adult status and the acceptance of the responsibilities that adult status entails. The rite is typically conducted at the age of twelve to sixteen years, varying with the physical development of the child and the traditions of the community.

## The Universal Pattern

Despite the differences in the forms of education across the known world, the fundamental pattern of childhood is the same everywhere. The child is nurtured by the family, educated by the community, and gradually prepared for the responsibilities of adult life. The content of the education varies with the conditions of the environment and the values of the culture, but the purpose of education is universal: the transmission of the knowledge and the skills that the child will need to contribute to the welfare of the community and to continue the traditions that the ancestors have established. Childhood is the foundation upon which the adult is built, and the quality of the childhood that a community provides is the measure of the community's commitment to the future.
