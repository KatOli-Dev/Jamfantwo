## The Final Passage

The treatment of the dead and the remembrance of the departed are among the most important concerns of every culture in the known world. The customs that govern death and remembrance are based on the beliefs that each people holds about the nature of the soul and the fate that awaits the departed, but the fundamental purpose of the customs is the same everywhere: the proper treatment of the body of the deceased, the comfort of the bereaved, and the preservation of the memory of the departed within the community.

The diversity of death customs across the known world reflects the diversity of beliefs about the afterlife and the relationship between the living and the dead. The [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) teaches that the soul of the deceased returns to the eternal flame from which it came, while the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/culture/sky-walkers-way) teaches that the soul journeys to the sky realm where the ancestors dwell. The traditions of the northern peoples teach that the soul sails across the sea to the islands of the dead, while the traditions of the desert nomads teach that the soul becomes one with the sands and the winds of the desert.

## Burial in the Vael Valley

The burial customs of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) river valley are conducted according to the traditions of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame), which teaches that the body of the deceased is to be returned to the earth from which it was formed. The burial is conducted at the temple of the village, presided over by the priest, and is attended by the family of the deceased and the adult members of the parish.

The body of the deceased is prepared by the family, who wash the body and dress it in the ceremonial garments that are kept for this purpose. The body is laid in a simple wooden coffin, and the coffin is carried in procession to the burial ground that adjoins the temple. The burial ground is consecrated ground, set apart for the purpose of burial, and the graves are marked by simple stones that bear the name of the deceased and the dates of the birth and the death.

The burial ceremony includes the prayers of the Still Flame, the kindling of a flame at the graveside, and the words of committal spoken by the priest. The family of the deceased casts a handful of earth onto the coffin before the grave is filled, and the mourners depart in silence. The burial is followed by a gathering at the household of the deceased, where the family and the friends share the meal that is the customary consolation of the bereaved.

## The Deep Mourning of the Coast

The maritime communities of the known world observe the [Deep Mourning](/content/culture/deep-mourning), the funeral rite that commits the body of the mariner to the sea. The [Deep Mourning](/content/culture/deep-mourning) is the principal funeral rite of the coastal settlements, observed upon the death of those whose lives were bound to the water.

The rite begins with the preparation of the body by the family, who wash the body in salt water drawn from the sea and dress the body in the deceased's preferred clothing. The body is placed upon a bier draped with cloth of the colour that the tradition of the community requires, and the bier is transported in procession to the boat that will carry the body to the place of committal.

The committal at sea is the central rite of the Deep Mourning. The body in its bier is placed upon a platform at the stern of the boat, and the platform is tilted to allow the bier to slide into the water. The family and the mourners cast offerings into the water alongside the bier, speaking words that acknowledge the deceased's life and wish for peace in the waters that were the principal element of that life.

## The Stone of the Dwarven Ancestors

The burial customs of the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) reflect the relationship of the dwarven people to the stone of the mountain. The body of the deceased dwarf is returned to the rock from which the dwarves believe their people were first formed, entombed in the burial chambers that the dwarves have carved deep within the mountain.

The body of the deceased is prepared by the master of the forge, who washes the body and dresses it in the garments of the craft that the deceased practised. The body is placed upon a stone bier, and the master of the forge conducts the ceremony that commits the body to the rock. The burial chamber is sealed with a stone door, and the name of the deceased is carved into the stone so that the memory of the deceased may be preserved.

The dwarves maintain the [Memory Houses](/content/religion/houses-of-memory), the chambers in which the records of the ancestors are preserved. The [Memory Houses](/content/religion/houses-of-memory) contain the genealogies of the lineages, the chronicles of the halls, and the works of the craftsmen who have contributed to the glory of the dwarven people. The Houses of Memory are the most sacred places of the dwarven community, and the dwarves visit them regularly to honour the memory of the ancestors and to consult the records that the ancestors have left.

## The Burning of the Plains

The funeral customs of the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) and the human herders of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) are adapted to the nomadic conditions of the grasslands. The body of the deceased is burned on a pyre of the dry grass and the wood that the tribe has gathered for the purpose, and the ashes are scattered across the plains so that the deceased may become one with the grasslands that were the home of the living.

The burning ceremony is conducted at the evening gathering, when the tribe assembles at the pyre and the elders speak the words that commemorate the life of the deceased. The family of the deceased casts the traditional offerings into the flames, and the tribe stands in silence as the fire consumes the body. The ashes are gathered by the family and scattered across the grasslands at dawn, when the spirits of the ancestors are believed to be closest to the living.

The centaurs also maintain the custom of the memory stones, the markers that the tribe erects at the place where the ashes of the deceased have been scattered. The memory stones are not burial markers in the sense of the settled peoples, but they serve the same purpose: the preservation of the memory of the deceased within the community. The memory stones are carved with the name of the deceased and the symbols that represent the life that the deceased led, and they are maintained by the family for as long as the family remembers the deceased.

## The Desert Burial

The funeral customs of the [Kharesh](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) and the [Vashari](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) desert nomads are adapted to the conditions of the desert environment. The body of the deceased is wrapped in a shroud of undyed cloth and buried in the sand at a depth that will protect the body from the scavengers of the desert.

The burial ceremony is conducted at the place of death, presided over by the shaman of the tribe, who speaks the words that commit the soul of the deceased to the spirits of the desert. The family of the deceased casts sand into the grave, and the grave is marked by a cairn of stones that will serve as a guide for the travellers who cross the desert.

## The Universal Remembrance

The customs that govern death and remembrance are among the most ancient and the most important of the traditions that the peoples of the known world maintain. The diversity of the customs reflects the diversity of the beliefs about the fate of the soul, but the fundamental purposes of the customs are the same everywhere: the proper treatment of the body, the comfort of the bereaved, and the preservation of the memory of the departed. The memory of the dead is the foundation of the identity of the living, and the customs of death and remembrance are the means by which the community maintains the continuity between the past and the present. The dead are not forgotten, and the memory of their deeds and their lives remains within the community that they helped to build.
