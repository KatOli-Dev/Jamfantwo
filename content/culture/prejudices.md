## The Nature of Prejudice in the Known World

The peoples of the known world encounter one another more often in co-operation than in conflict, but prejudice is not unknown. Where it appears, it is recorded by the chroniclers and observed by travellers as a notable departure from the general disposition toward peaceful coexistence. The instances collected here are those that have been remarked upon precisely because they are not universal — they represent the exceptions that the academies consider worth documenting rather than the rule of daily life.

Most communities conduct their dealings with other peoples on terms of practical reciprocity shaped by trade and shared territory. The attitudes described below are documented in specific places and periods and should not be taken as characteristic of entire species or cultures.

## Human, Dwarven, and Elven Dispositions

Humans, elves, and dwarves each possess a sense of their own ways, but this seldom rises to active prejudice against others. Mutual incomprehension exists — an elf may find human haste bewildering, a dwarf may consider elven deliberateness impractical — but such perceptions are more often a source of humour or mild frustration than of exclusion or hostility.

Human attitudes toward other species vary considerably by region. In cosmopolitan ports and trading cities, where contact with non-humans is frequent and sustained, humans regard elves, dwarves, and others as familiar partners in commerce and daily life. In isolated rural communities that rarely encounter other species, wariness may appear, but it is a wariness born of unfamiliarity rather than conviction. The stereotype that orcs are dangerous or goblins untrustworthy is repeated in some frontier settlements where conflict has occurred, but it carries little weight in the cities where most humans live.

Dwarven attitudes toward surface peoples are shaped by a preference for their own kind and their own deep halls, but this is a matter of temperament rather than contempt. Dwarves who trade regularly with humans and elves develop the same practical relationships that characterise commerce everywhere. The restrictions that exist in certain dwarven cities — limiting where surface dwellers may go within the mountain — are customs of privacy and security rather than expressions of superiority. A few dwarven chroniclers, particularly in the older holds, have written dismissively of surface peoples, but these writings are regarded by most dwarves as the grumbling of eccentrics rather than the voice of their people.

Elven attitudes toward shorter-lived peoples are shaped by a perspective that spans centuries. Humans appear to elves as creatures of haste, their generations passing in what seems a single season of elven observation — clever and energetic but given to decisions that look hasty to a longer view. Dwarves are regarded with respect for their craft and patience. These are observations, not prejudices; elven communities that dwell alongside humans and dwarves maintain cordial relations, and elven merchants, scholars, and artisans are common sights in the mixed cities of all three continents.

## Orcs, Goblins, Halflings, and Gnomes

The peoples whose populations are smaller or more localised are treated in most places with the same practical regard as any other trading partner.

Orcs are regarded with caution by many settled peoples, particularly in regions where orcish hunting parties have raided farms and caravans. These incidents are real, but they are local and intermittent rather than characteristic of all orcish communities. In regions where orcs and settled peoples have established stable trading relationships, the caution fades into the ordinary wariness that marks any interaction between strangers. The depiction of orcs as inherently savage is confined to a few frontier chronicles and is not accepted by the academies, whose scholars note that orcish communities vary as widely in their dispositions as any other people.

Goblins are sometimes blamed for misfortunes in frontier communities, particularly where goblin bands have been known to raid stores and fields. Such scapegoating is documented in a handful of settlements during periods of scarcity, but it is not a general feature of human or dwarven dealings with goblins. In the cities and towns where goblins trade and work alongside other peoples, they are treated according to their reputation as individuals rather than according to the reputation of their species. The suspicion that clings to goblins in some quarters is a local phenomenon, sustained by particular histories of conflict rather than by any widespread prejudice.

Halflings coexist with humans across much of their range on terms of mutual benefit. Human lords and halfling communities have worked out arrangements of tenancy and land use that suit both parties over centuries, and the displacement of halfling communities by human expansion is rare enough to be recorded when it occurs. Halflings prefer their own quiet way of life and keep their dealings with human authorities to a practical minimum, but this is a choice rather than a consequence of exclusion.

Gnomes who trade in human towns are generally valued for the quality of their craftsmanship. An occasional prejudice that gnomish work must be inferior to that of a human artisan appears in isolated markets, but it is remarked upon by travellers precisely because it contradicts the usual experience, in which gnomish goods command good prices and gnomish merchants are treated with respect.

## Political Restrictions by Species

In most cities and nations of the known world, political participation is determined by citizenship, property, and residence rather than by species. The instances in which non-human residents are formally excluded from governance are the exception rather than the rule, and they are noted by the chroniclers as departures from the common practice.

In the city of Amurra, the council is drawn from the old merchant families, and no foreigner has ever been elected. This restriction is a matter of family and tradition rather than species — the salawa of the desert who trade with Amurra do not seek seats on the council, and the boundary is understood as a custom of civic identity rather than of exclusion.

In Khalisar, the council is restricted to human citizens of the city. This is a point of tension in an otherwise tolerant city, and civic reformers have called for its expansion, but the restriction has not been changed. The harbour court in Khalisar has been known to favour human defendants in disputes with non-human plaintiffs, though such cases are infrequent and the practice is condemned by the academy.

In the dwarven cities of Karhold and Stoneheim, the deep chambers are reserved for dwarven residents by custom, and surface dwellers — whether human or gnome — live and work in the upper halls. These arrangements are understood by all parties as cultural boundaries rather than statements of worth, and the surface-dwelling communities within these cities are prosperous and well-treated.

In most other cities, non-human residents participate in civic life without formal restriction. Eldreth, Nordhaven, and the cities of the Thessaran League admit non-human residents to their councils, guilds, and courts on the same terms as human citizens, and the exceptions that appear in certain offices or industries are the result of informal tradition rather than codified exclusion.

## Barriers of Sex and Social Rank

Prejudices of gender are documented in the chronicles but are far from universal. Many societies have produced women of standing in scholarship, governance, and the arts without remark, and the instances in which gender has been a barrier are recorded precisely because they are notable exceptions.

Sera Ambervale, the most celebrated ruler of the central kingdoms of Aethelar, was dismissed by some of the lords who opposed her as a girl playing at war — a taunt she used to her advantage by striking before they took her seriously. The academy of the Amurra Republic admitted women to full fellowship generations ago, and Halen Amurra's mother was a respected scholar of her day; that her lineage was occasionally cited by opponents of the change is remembered because such objections were already fading. Iren Khalisar holds the position of chief cartographer of the academy of Khalisar, a profession in which women have served before her; the prejudice against women in scholarly roles persists in a few of the less cosmopolitan cities of the interior, but Iren's reputation has overcome such doubts wherever her charts are used.

Prejudices of station appear in certain customs. In the Deep Mourning practice of coastal communities, the elaboration of funeral offerings reflects the deceased's means — a merchant's family may cast silver while a fisher's family offers bread and flowers — but this is a matter of what the family can afford rather than a judgment of worth. In the Hands Joined marriage custom of the temperate lowlands, marriages between families of different standing are less common, but this is a consequence of the circles in which people move rather than of any formal barrier.

## The Stone Plague and the Wars

The historical record preserves a few instances in which prejudice shaped the course of events, and these are remembered because they stand out against the general disposition toward peaceful coexistence.

The Stone Plague, the great pestilence that swept through the known world in a forgotten age, revealed the worst that fear could produce. In several cities, goblin communities were attacked on the suspicion that they had brought the disease, though goblins sickened and died at the same rate as any other people. In the dwarven holds, human merchants were turned away from the passes even after they produced proof of clean passage. These acts were condemned by the magistrates and the academies, and they are recorded in the chronicles as a shameful accompaniment to the greater tragedy — notable because such behaviour was recognised even at the time as a departure from decency.

The wars of the known world have sometimes deepened the divisions between peoples. The Twin-Reaches Wars between the Amurra Republic and the Vashari Pact were accompanied by cultural contempt on both sides, each regarding the other as provincial or decadent — stereotypes that outlasted the conflict. The Stoneheim Pass War between the dwarven holds and a coalition of lowland princes is remembered differently by dwarven and human chroniclers, each emphasising the other's failures. These wartime prejudices faded in the generations of peace that followed, and the former adversaries now trade as freely as any other partners.

## Local Conditions and the Overcoming of Division

Where prejudice does appear, it is most often a local phenomenon — the product of particular histories, isolated conditions, or times of crisis rather than a feature of the world's permanent disposition. The same forces that produce it — trade, travel, and the exchange of knowledge — also overcome it, and the steady contact between peoples teaches each generation that strangers are not so strange after all.
