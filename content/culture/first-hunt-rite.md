## The Coming-of-Age Hunt

The First-Hunt Rite is the principal coming-of-age ceremony of the human and elven communities of the temperate and northern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), observed by young people of both sexes at the age of maturity. The rite is observed in every nation of the temperate zone, though customs vary from one people to another. It demonstrates the competence of the young person to provide for and defend the community, and the rituals acknowledge the transition to adult status.

## Early Beginnings and Varied Interpretations

The origins of the rite are prehistoric. Scholars of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) propose that it began as a hunting ritual of the forest peoples, testing the ability of the young to participate in the great woodland hunts. Scholars of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) argue that the rite is older than settled agriculture, descending from a pastoral ritual of prehistoric grassland peoples. Regardless of its origins, the rite is observed by peoples of every religion and of none.

The rite takes place at the age of fifteen or sixteen in most communities, though the precise age varies with climate and constitution. Young people of the colder northern regions mature later than those of the warmer south, and the rite is correspondingly delayed in mountain communities. The young person must undergo the test of the hunt, and successful completion is required for formal acknowledgement of adult status. Failure is not considered a disgrace; those who fail may attempt the test again the following year.

## Fasting, Lore, and Readiness

Preparation begins in the weeks before the hunt under the supervision of community elders. The young person is instructed in the lore of the hunt — reading the signs of the quarry and conducting the chase — as well as in the customary observances that accompany the hunt. An appointed elder is responsible for assessing the young person's readiness, though the consent of the parents and household is also required before the young person may proceed to the test.

The preparation includes a period of fasting and contemplation in the days immediately preceding the hunt. The fasting is not severe; the young person may take a light meal in the evening, but the restriction is sufficient to encourage contemplation of the seriousness of the occasion. The contemplation takes place at the household shrine or another quiet place the community provides, where the young person reflects upon the responsibilities that completing the rite will bring.

## The Solo Pursuit and the Kill

The test is conducted on a day appointed by the elders, typically in late spring or early summer when the weather is favourable and game is in good condition. The elder who oversaw the preparation escorts the young person to the hunting ground and remains at a distance to observe. The young person must pursue and take a single animal of a species customary for the test, which varies with terrain. Forest communities require a deer or wild boar, plains communities a hare or fox, and mountain communities a marmot or similar creature.

The young person must take the quarry without assistance, using only the customary weapon of the community — the bow, the spear, or the sling. The young person must also dress the quarry according to customary practice and bring the carcass back to the community, where the elder examines the work. The examination includes inspection of the wound, which must be clean and must have caused a minimum of suffering to the animal. A wound judged to have caused unnecessary suffering is grounds for failure, even if the quarry was taken.

## The Ceremony of Adulthood and the Shared Meal

The young person who has successfully completed the test is formally recognised as an adult at a ceremony held at the community's gathering place. The elder presents the young person to the assembly and speaks upon the young person's character and conduct. The young person then speaks, acknowledging the responsibilities assumed and pledging service to the community. The speech is followed by the bestowal of customary tokens of adult status — a weapon, a tool, or a piece of clothing that marks the transition.

The feast that follows is one of the principal social occasions of the community year, attended by the community and by visitors who have come to witness the occasion. The principal dish is the meat of the quarry, prepared according to the customary recipe. The meat is served to the young person first, and the young person shares it with the elder who oversaw the preparation. This sharing formally acknowledges the bond established between the young person and the elder, a bond expected to endure through life. Members of the community give gifts of a practical nature that will serve the young person in adult life.

## Dwarven, Elven, and River-Folk Adaptations

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) observe a rite adapted to underground life. The dwarven rite requires the young person to demonstrate competence in the metalwork and stonework that are the customary occupations of dwarven communities. The rite is conducted in the presence of the master of the craft to which the young person is to be bound, and the master assesses the young person's readiness. The dwarven rite does not include a hunting test, as dwarven communities do not hunt the great game of the surface, but it includes a test of the young person's ability to navigate the tunnels and find the way in the dark.

The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and other elven communities of the central woodlands require the young person to spend seven days and seven nights alone in the deep forest, subsisting upon the products of the forest and demonstrating the competence to read the signs of the wood. Elders observe the young person at a distance and assess readiness upon the conclusion of the period of solitude. The elven rite is considered among the most demanding coming-of-age ceremonies of the known world, and its completion is a matter of considerable pride.

Some communities of the river valleys have modified the rite to reflect the dominance of fishing in their economy, replacing the taking of a game animal with the landing of a large fish from the river. In these communities the young person must bring in a mature salmon or sturgeon using only the customary gear of the fisherfolk, and the fish is then prepared and shared according to the same customs that govern the quarry in the hunting communities. This variant is known as the First-Catch Rite and follows the same structural pattern, including the preparatory period, the test performed without assistance, and the recognition feast.

## The Rite's Role in Community Life

The First-Hunt Rite is the principal coming-of-age ceremony of the human and elven communities of the temperate and northern regions of the known world. The rite demonstrates the competence of the young person to provide for the community and defend it against the dangers of the wilderness, and its rituals acknowledge the young person's transition to adult status. The rite holds both civic and religious significance, recognised by the authorities of the nations in which it is observed. It endures as a celebration of the renewal of the community and as a reminder that the responsibilities of adulthood must be entered into with a full understanding of their character.
