## The Principle from Which the Art Draws

The source of the Art is resonance, a property of the world by which like things act upon like. The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) state the principle in the most widely cited form: every thing in the world has its likeness, and every likeness is a path by which the thing may be reached. Resonance is not a force that flows between things nor a substance that can be gathered and spent. It is the principle of sympathy itself, and the practitioner of the Art is one who has learned to walk the paths of sympathy without losing the way.

This page sets down the principle as observed and as taught, with the principal variants of the formulation and the principal disputes about its meaning. The disputes are not resolved here.

## Two Words for the Same Principle

The principle of resonance is older than any of the traditions that teach the Art. The earliest named practitioners spoke of sympathy rather than resonance, and the older word is still preferred by the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) and by several conservative lineages of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael). The shift in vocabulary was introduced by the chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) in the generation after [Sera's Reign](/content/history/seras-reign) and has been adopted by most academies founded since.

The two words are taken by the traditions to mean the same thing, though the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) and the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) have occasionally argued for a distinction. The Fellowship treats sympathy as the narrower term, denoting the act by which one pattern reaches another, and resonance as the wider term, denoting the underlying order that makes sympathy possible. The Council treats the two as synonyms, holding that the distinction introduces a complexity the practice does not require. The disagreement is not merely verbal, for the position one takes affects the working one prefers when the pattern to be reached is far from the practitioner's own.

## Not Measurable, Not Storable, Not Divine

The traditions agree that resonance is not measurable in the manner of weight or length and cannot be stored in a vessel. The practitioners of the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) have been particularly insistent on this point and have published several accounts of failed experiments in which an attempt was made to gather resonance into a containment and release it at need. The accounts agree that the containment gathered nothing and the release produced nothing; they are taken as the strongest evidence that resonance is not a substance.

The traditions also agree that resonance is not the gift of any god. The priests of [Sahran](/content/location/settlement/city/sahran) and the temples of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) have on occasion claimed that the Art is a divine favour, a claim received by practitioners with courtesy and polite refusal to engage. The temples have not pressed the claim, contenting themselves with the observation that the Art is part of the order of the world, a matter upon which the temples and the practitioners may agree.

## Observed at Many Scales beyond the Art

The principle of resonance is observed in many places and at many scales, and practitioners do not regard resonance as peculiar to the Art alone. The [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) responds to the moon in a pattern that resembles the response of water in a bowl, and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) responds to the wind in a pattern repeated at every scale. The Art is not the source of these observations nor the explanation of them; it is the discipline by which a practitioner learns to walk the paths that the patterns reveal.

The traditions treat the world as a fabric of patterns in which every part is in sympathy with every other part, and the practitioner is one who has learned to read the fabric and follow the threads. The reading is not a passive observation, for the reading itself changes the reader. The practitioner who has read a pattern deeply enough to reshape it is no longer the same practitioner who began the reading, and this change is part of the [cost](/content/magic/law/cost) of the Art.

## Walking the Paths of Sympathy

The older accounts describe the practitioner as one who walks the paths of resonance, a description preserved in the discipline of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship). The paths are not physical nor visible to the uninitiated. They are the relations between patterns, and the practitioner follows a path from their own pattern to the pattern of the subject through attention and attunement, which is the foundation of the reshaping that follows.

The practitioners of the academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) prefer a different figure: the practitioner as one who adjusts a tuning until practitioner and subject vibrate together. The two figures describe the same discipline in different terms and are taken to be equivalent in practice. The choice of figure has been a matter of dispute between the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and the academies, carried on for several generations without resolution.

## Whether Sympathy Can Be Deepened

The traditions agree that resonance is the source of the Art and that resonance is the principle of sympathy. They disagree about how far sympathy extends and whether it may be increased by any act of will or of the Art. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) maintains that sympathy is fixed in the order of the world and that the practitioner may only follow the paths that already exist. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) maintains that sympathy may be deepened by long practice and that a practitioner of sufficient skill may reach patterns an untutored practitioner cannot. The two positions are not far apart in practice: both agree that the practitioner must work within the sympathy that is given and cannot create a sympathy that does not exist.

The disputes are of greater interest to the traditions themselves than to the practitioner at work, who is occupied with reading and reshaping and has little time for theoretical questions. The questions are not without importance, for the answers the traditions give shape the disciplines they teach, and the disciplines in turn shape the workings they make their own.
