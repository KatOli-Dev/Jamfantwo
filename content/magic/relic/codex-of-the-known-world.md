## Overview

The Codex of the Known World is the foundational work of natural philosophy compiled by [High Chronicler Vaelen](/content/people/historical/high-chronicler-vaelen) in the latter half of the second age, and it remains the single most influential work of natural philosophy in the history of the continents. The work was begun in the chronicler's thirty-fifth year and completed in his sixty-eighth, a span of thirty-three years during which he travelled to every continent and every region of the known world that was accessible to a solitary scholar of modest means. The Codex is divided into seven books, each treating a different aspect of the natural order: the first book describes the geography and the climate of the continents, the second the flora of the known world, the third the fauna, the fourth the sapient peoples, the fifth the minerals and the metals, the sixth the waters and the currents, and the seventh the heavens and the measurement of time. The method that Vaelen employed in the compilation was unprecedented in its rigour, and the work is the foundation upon which all later natural philosophy in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and beyond has been built.

The Codex is preserved in its original manuscript in the library of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) that Vaelen himself founded, and the copies that were carried to the libraries of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league), the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the [Deep Realm of Karhold](/content/government/national/deep-realm-of-karhold), and the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) became the foundation of the natural sciences in those regions. The chronicler records that the Codex has been copied and recopied in every centre of learning, and the copies that survive in the present age are reckoned among the most valuable possessions of the libraries that hold them. The work is the cornerstone of the natural sciences, and the scholars of every academy of the known world are trained in the Vaelenic method that the Codex established.

## The First Book: Geography and Climate

The first book of the Codex describes the geography and the climate of the continents, and the book is the earliest surviving systematic account of the three great landmasses in their relation to one another. The chronicler records that Vaelen set down the relative positions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) with a precision that the scholars of the later age have confirmed in nearly every particular. The maps that were drawn from Vaelen's descriptions remained the standard reference for more than two centuries after his death, and the geographical chapters of the Codex are still cited in the academies as the foundational account of the disposition of the continents.

The book on geography contains the earliest surviving description of the principal oceans and the seas that lie between the continents, and Vaelen's account of the currents of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) was the first to recognise that the movement of water in the seas followed patterns that could be predicted and charted. The chronicler records that Vaelen observed the currents from the decks of the vessels that carried him between the continents, and the observations were supplemented by the testimony of the pilots and the captains with whom he sailed. The geographical book also contains the first systematic account of the climate of each region, and the descriptions of the temperature, the rainfall, and the seasonal variation have served as the basis of every later study of the climate of the known world.

The chronicler observes that the first book of the Codex is also the most practical of the seven, for the geographical descriptions have been of immediate use to the mariners and the merchants who travel between the continents. The pilots of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles) and the captains of the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) have long relied upon the geographical chapters of the Codex for the navigation of the routes that the chronicler describes, and the descriptions have been amended by the observations of the mariners but not fundamentally altered. The first book is the foundation of the practical art of navigation, and the chronicler notes that the art of navigation in the present age is the direct heir of the Vaelenic method.

## The Second and Third Books: Flora and Fauna

The second and third books of the Codex describe the flora and the fauna of the known world, and the books are the earliest systematic accounts of the living things of the continents. Vaelen recorded only those species that he had observed himself or that had been reported to him by witnesses whose reliability he had assessed, and the chronicler records that Vaelen distinguished clearly in his text between what he had seen with his own eyes and what he had been told by others. The practice of distinguishing between observation and inference is the foundation of the [Cataloguing of Flora and Fauna](/content/belief/science/physical/cataloguing-of-flora-and-fauna) that the scholars of the present age continue to employ, and the Vaelenic method is the standard of the natural sciences.

The second book of the Codex describes the plants of the known world, arranged according to the structure of their bodies, the manner of their reproduction, and the environments in which they are found. The categories that Vaelen established have been refined by the naturalists of the later age but not fundamentally altered, and the names that Vaelen assigned to the major groups of plants are the standard terms of the natural sciences. The chronicler records that the second book contains more than a thousand entries, each with a description of the plant, a note of the local names in the languages of the peoples who use them, and a drawing that would allow a reader in a distant land to identify the subject of the passage.

The third book of the Codex describes the animals of the known world, arranged according to the same principles as the second book. The book on the fauna is the most extensive of the seven, and the chronicler records that Vaelen devoted more pages to the description of the animals than to any other subject. The great whales of the cold water, the great cats of the grasslands, the serpents of the warm rivers, the birds of the inland forests, and the smaller creatures of the marshes and the shores are all described with a care that the chronicler has described as the most attentive observation in the known world. The third book is the foundation of the natural history of the animals, and the scholars of the present age are trained in the Vaelenic method of description.

## The Fourth Book: The Sapient Peoples

The fourth book of the Codex describes the sapient peoples of the known world, and the book is the most remarkable of the seven for the impartiality that Vaelen brought to the study of the thinking species. The chronicler records that Vaelen approached the study of the sapient peoples with a care that was rare in his age, describing the customs, the languages, and the physiologies of each people without the judgements of superiority that coloured the writings of his contemporaries. The work is the first attempt in the known world to describe the full range of sapient life in a single systematic account, and it is the foundation upon which all later ethnological study has been built.

Vaelen recorded the physical characteristics of the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves), the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves), the [orcs](/content/species/sapient/orcs), the [goblins](/content/species/sapient/goblins), the [halflings](/content/species/sapient/halflings), and the [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) of the northern continents, and he drew comparisons between the northern peoples and the [tortolans](/content/species/sapient/tortolans), the [felinids](/content/species/sapient/felinids), the [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines), the [corvids](/content/species/sapient/corvids), and the [noctules](/content/species/sapient/noctules) of the southern land. The chronicler records that Vaelen travelled to the courts of the southern peoples and observed the customs of each at first hand, and the descriptions he recorded are the primary source for the study of the sapient peoples of the southern continents. The fourth book is the foundation of the comparative study of the sapient species, and the scholars of the present age are trained in the Vaelenic method of impartial description.

The fourth book is also notable for the care that Vaelen took to record the local names for each people in the languages of the peoples themselves, and the chronicler records that Vaelen spent considerable time learning the languages of the peoples he visited so that he could record the names with accuracy. The practice of recording the local names is one of the most distinctive features of the Vaelenic method, and the chronicler notes that the practice has been adopted by every later ethnological scholar who has worked in the Vaelenic tradition. The fourth book is the foundation of the respectful study of the sapient species, and the chronicler observes that the impartiality of the description is a testament to the chronicler's character as well as to his method.

## The Fifth and Sixth Books: Minerals and Waters

The fifth book of the Codex describes the minerals and the metals of the known world, and the book is the earliest systematic account of the mineral wealth of the continents. Vaelen recorded the occurrence of the principal metals and the principal minerals in the regions where they are found, and the descriptions are accompanied by the local names in the languages of the peoples who work the mines. The chronicler records that Vaelen visited the principal mines of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the southern ranges, and the descriptions he set down are the primary source for the study of the mineral wealth of the known world. The fifth book is the foundation of the practical art of mining, and the chronicler notes that the art of mining in the present age is the direct heir of the Vaelenic method.

The sixth book of the Codex describes the waters and the currents of the known world, and the book is the most detailed of the seven in its treatment of the movement of the seas. Vaelen recorded the principal currents of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) with a precision that the chronicler has described as unprecedented in the known world, and the descriptions are accompanied by charts of the prevailing winds and the seasonal variation of the currents. The sixth book is the foundation of the practical art of navigation upon the open water, and the chronicler notes that the art of the pilots of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles) is the direct heir of the Vaelenic descriptions of the currents.

The chronicler observes that the fifth and sixth books of the Codex are the most practical of the seven, for the descriptions of the minerals and the waters have been of immediate use to the miners and the mariners who work the metals and the seas. The chronicler records that the merchants of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) have long relied upon the geographical chapters of the Codex for the planning of their ventures, and the descriptions have been amended by the observations of the miners and the mariners but not fundamentally altered. The fifth and sixth books are the foundation of the practical arts of mining and navigation, and the chronicler notes that the arts in the present age are the direct heirs of the Vaelenic method.

## The Seventh Book: The Heavens and the Measurement of Time

The seventh book of the Codex describes the heavens and the measurement of time, and the book is the most theoretical of the seven. Vaelen recorded the movements of the sun, the moon, and the principal planets as he had observed them during his travels, and the descriptions are accompanied by the local calendars in use among the peoples of the continents. The chronicler records that Vaelen was the first scholar to recognise that the calendars of the different peoples were measuring the same celestial movements by different conventions, and the seventh book contains the first attempt to set down a unified account of the heavens that all peoples could recognise. The seventh book is the foundation of the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/cartography-of-the-spheres), and the chronicler notes that the theoretical science in the present age is the direct heir of the Vaelenic method.

The seventh book also contains the first systematic account of the measurement of time by means of the heavens, and the descriptions of the sundial, the water clock, and the timed glass are the foundation of the practical art of horology. The chronicler records that Vaelen observed the instruments of timekeeping in every region he visited, and the descriptions he set down are the primary source for the study of the instruments of the ancient world. The seventh book is the foundation of the practical art of horology, and the chronicler notes that the art in the present age is the direct heir of the Vaelenic method.

The chronicler observes that the seventh book of the Codex is also the most speculative of the seven, for the descriptions of the heavens are accompanied by Vaelen's own attempts to account for the movements he had observed. The attempts are cautious and clearly marked as speculation, and the chronicler records that Vaelen distinguished carefully in the text between what he had observed and what he had inferred. The practice of distinguishing between observation and inference is the foundation of the natural sciences, and the chronicler notes that the practice has been adopted by every later scholar who has worked in the Vaelenic tradition. The seventh book is the foundation of the theoretical science of the heavens, and the scholars of the present age are trained in the Vaelenic method of careful speculation.

## The Method and the Influence

The method that Vaelen employed in the compilation of the Codex is the foundation of the natural sciences, and the method is the most enduring of the chronicler's contributions to the learning of the known world. The method consists of careful observation, the recording of the local names, the drawing of the subjects, the distinguishing of observation from inference, and the revision of the text in the light of new evidence. The chronicler records that Vaelen revised the text of the Codex three times before he was satisfied with its completeness, and that the final revision required the copying of the entire work by a team of scribes who worked under his supervision for a period of five years. The revision of the text is the foundation of the scholarly practice of amendment, and the chronicler notes that the practice has been adopted by every later scholar who has worked in the Vaelenic tradition.

The influence of the Codex upon the intellectual life of the known world is difficult to overstate. The work was copied and recopied in every centre of learning, and the copies that were carried to the libraries of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league), the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the [Deep Realm of Karhold](/content/government/national/deep-realm-of-karhold), and the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) became the foundation of the natural sciences in those regions. The chronicler records that the schools of natural philosophy that Vaelen inspired have produced generations of scholars who have extended and refined his work, and the discipline that he founded is still the most respected of the intellectual pursuits in the academies of the known world.

The Codex is cited as the foundational work of the natural philosophy that the [magic](/content/magic/overview) of the present age builds upon, and the chronicler records that the libraries of [Avaros](/content/location/settlement/city/avaros) and the academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) hold copies of the work as the foundation of the theoretical sciences. The influence of the Codex extends beyond the natural philosophy into the study of the [Cataloguing of Flora and Fauna](/content/belief/science/physical/cataloguing-of-flora-and-fauna), whose practitioners continue to employ the Vaelenic method that the Codex established. The chronicler observes that the Codex is the most cited work in the natural sciences, and the citations are a testament to the enduring influence of the chronicler's method.

## The Manuscript and the Library of Eldreth

The original manuscript of the Codex is preserved in the library of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) that Vaelen himself founded, and the manuscript is the most valuable single document in the natural sciences. The library houses the original manuscript together with the journals, the drawings, and the specimens that Vaelen collected during his travels, and the collection has been maintained by a succession of scholars who have continued his work of observation and classification. The chronicler records that the library is the most important institution of natural philosophy on the continent, and the scholars who work there are the custodians of the Vaelenic tradition.

The chronicler records that the Codex was written upon vellum in the careful hand of the chronicler himself, and the manuscript is bound in seven volumes that correspond to the seven books of the work. The volumes are kept in a cabinet of seasoned oak in the principal reading room of the library, and the cabinet is opened only by the head librarian in the presence of the visiting scholars who have come to consult the original. The chronicler notes that the manuscript shows the signs of the chronicler's long labour, and the marginalia of the three revisions are preserved in the binding. The manuscript is the foundation of the natural sciences, and the chronicler observes that the manuscript is the most venerated object in the library of Eldreth.

The chronicler observes that the library of Eldreth is the heir of the chronicler's foundation, and the scholars who work there are the direct successors of the tradition that the chronicler established. The library is supported by the city of Eldreth and by the merchants who trade in the harbour, and the chronicler records that the library has been maintained without interruption since the chronicler's death. The Codex is the foundation of the library's collection, and the scholars who work there are the custodians of the work that the chronicler began. The chronicler notes that the library is the most important institution of natural philosophy on the continent, and the Codex is the foundation of the library's enduring significance.

## Significance

The Codex of the Known World is the foundational work of natural philosophy in the history of the continents, and the work is the most influential single contribution to the learning of the known world. The seven books of the Codex describe the geography, the flora, the fauna, the sapient peoples, the minerals, the waters, and the heavens of the three continents, and the descriptions are the foundation upon which every later work of natural philosophy has been built. The chronicler records that the work has been copied and recopied in every centre of learning, and the copies that survive in the present age are reckoned among the most valuable possessions of the libraries that hold them. The Codex is the cornerstone of the natural sciences, and the scholars of every academy of the known world are trained in the Vaelenic method that the work established.

The chronicler finds in the Codex the most complete and the most influential of the works of natural philosophy, and the work is the foundation of the enduring tradition of the natural sciences in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and beyond. The careful observation, the recording of the local names, the drawing of the subjects, the distinguishing of observation from inference, and the revision of the text in the light of new evidence are the practices that the scholars of the present age continue to employ, and the practices are the direct heirs of the Vaelenic method. The Codex endures in the library of Eldreth and in the libraries of every centre of learning as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the observation and the classification of the natural world. The chronicler notes that the name of Vaelen is spoken with reverence in every academy of the known world, and the Codex is the book that every scholar of the natural sciences must know, for it is the foundation upon which the entire edifice of the natural philosophy of the age is built.
