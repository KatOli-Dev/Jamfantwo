## A Disputed but Influential Text

The Letters on Pattern is a disputed but widely-circulated text attributed to the practitioners of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship), a body of writing that has shaped the academic study of the Art in the centuries since its first appearance in the libraries of the academies. The Letters are not a single treatise but a sequence of seven shorter texts, each addressed by a practitioner of the Fellowship to a younger colleague upon the subjects of reading, attunement, and the reshaping of pattern. They are bound together in the form of a correspondence that the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described as the most accessible introduction to the discipline of the Art that the traditions have produced. The Letters are the foundation of the academic study of the Art and the most cited of the texts that the academies employ in the instruction of their students.

## Whether the Heartwood Fellowship Wrote the Letters

The Letters are attributed to the practitioners of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship), an attribution recorded in the prefatory note that accompanies the earliest surviving manuscripts. The note records that the Letters were composed by a practitioner whose name is not given, a member of the Fellowship at the clearing of Greenwell. The prefatory note is not consistent with the customary practice of the Fellowship, which does not ordinarily publish the work of its members; the publication of the Letters would have been a notable departure from its customary reserve. The prefatory note is therefore either a genuine departure from custom or a fabrication that the publication required.

The dispute over the attribution is one of the most enduring controversies surrounding the study of the Art and has divided the scholars of the academies for as long as the Letters have been in circulation. Those who accept the attribution hold that the Letters are consistent with the discipline of the Fellowship, pointing to the careful attention to the limits of the Art and the costs of practice that the Letters exhibit. Those who reject the attribution hold that the Letters are too systematic in their treatment of the laws to be the work of a single practitioner and point to passages they consider inconsistent with the customary reserve of the Fellowship. The dispute has not been resolved and is unlikely to be resolved by the discovery of further manuscripts, for the surviving manuscripts are all consistent with the prefatory note, and the note is the only evidence of the attribution.

The dispute over the attribution has been of less consequence to the study of the Art than the dispute over the doctrine. The doctrine of the Letters is a particular reading of the laws of the Art, not accepted by all traditions. The practitioners of the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) and the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) have published critiques of the doctrine, which have been the subject of extended debate in the academies. The debate is conducted with a civility unusual in disputes between the traditions and is the foundation of the academic study of the Art.

## Seven Letters, Seven Disciplines

The Letters are seven in number, each addressed to a younger colleague whose name is not given. The first letter treats the subject of reading and sets out the discipline of attending to the pattern of the subject; the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described it as the most accessible introduction to the subject that the traditions have produced, and it is the text the academies employ in the opening weeks of instruction. The second letter treats attunement, setting out the discipline of bringing the practitioner's pattern into sympathy with the subject's. The third letter treats reshaping, the act of the working itself. The fourth letter treats the limits within which the Art must work.

The fifth letter treats the costs that the practitioner must pay. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described the fifth letter as the most demanding of the seven, and it is the text the academies employ in the closing weeks of instruction. The sixth letter treats the tradition, setting out the practitioner's understanding of the place of the Art in the order of the world. The seventh letter treats the practitioner and the change that practice works in those who pursue it; it is the most personal of the seven and is reserved for the most advanced students.

The Letters are written in a measured prose that avoids the technical vocabulary of the academies and employs the ordinary language of the practitioner in the field. The prose is one of the reasons the Letters have been so widely circulated and so often translated into the languages of other traditions. The translations are not always consistent, and the inconsistencies have been the source of some of the disputes the doctrine has provoked, but the prose of the original is held by scholars to be clear enough to be reconciled with customary readings.

## A Living Order, Not a Passive Substance

The doctrine set out in the Letters is a particular reading of the laws of the Art, distinguished by its attention to the second law, which the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) names the medium and which the older practitioners of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) prefer to call pattern. The Letters hold that pattern is not a passive substance upon which the Art operates but a living order that the practitioner must learn to read and follow; a practitioner who treats pattern as passive will find workings resistant and the bounds of the Art more restrictive than they need be. This doctrine is consistent with the discipline of the Fellowship and is one of the reasons the attribution has been so widely accepted.

The Letters also set out a particular reading of the first law, which the Sylvan Court names the [Source](/content/magic/law/source) and which the older practitioners of the Heartwood Fellowship prefer to call resonance. The Letters hold that resonance is the sympathy the practitioner walks when following the paths of pattern and that treating resonance as a substance to be gathered and spent will leave workings empty and the bounds more restrictive than necessary. This doctrine of resonance is consistent with the doctrine of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) but not with that of the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact), which holds that resonance is a substance the practitioner may gather. The dispute is one of the most enduring controversies surrounding the study of the Art.

## How the Letters Shaped the Curricula

The Letters have shaped the academic study of the Art since their first appearance in the libraries, and their influence is observable in the curricula of the academies of every continent. The first letter is the text the academies of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) employ in the opening weeks of instruction, and the fifth letter is the text they employ in the closing weeks. The [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) and the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) have adopted the Letters as the foundation of their own curricula, and the academies of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) have included the Letters in the list of texts candidates for higher degrees must master. The influence extends beyond the academies: wandering practitioners of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) have carried the Letters to the villages and outposts of the forest margin, and the Letters are read aloud at gatherings of sworn fellows as a text of instruction.

The Letters are also the most cited of the texts that natural philosophers of the present age employ. The scholars of the [Eldreth Library](/content/magic/relic/library-of-eldreth) have published an edition of the Letters with a commentary described as the most thorough of any edition the libraries have produced; this edition is the standard text the academies employ in their instruction. The publication of this edition was the occasion of the most extensive debate the academic study of the Art has witnessed.

## The Fellowship's Reserved Position

The relation of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) to the Letters is the subject of the most enduring controversy surrounding the text. The Fellowship has never formally acknowledged the Letters as the work of one of its members nor formally rejected the attribution. The chief warden of the Fellowship has on several occasions issued statements to the effect that the Fellowship does not take a position on the attribution, the closest the institution has come to a public position. The reserve of the Fellowship is consistent with its customary practice and is one of the reasons the attribution has remained in dispute.

Fellows who have served at the clearing of Greenwell have, in private communications with scholars of the academies, been more forthcoming. They have admitted that the Letters are consistent with the discipline of the Fellowship and that practitioners of the Fellowship have used passages from the Letters in the instruction of apprentices. They have not, however, identified the author of the Letters nor produced any documentary evidence that would resolve the dispute.

## Preservation and Publication of the Manuscript

The Letters are preserved in the libraries of every academy of natural philosophy in the known world. The original manuscript is held by the [Eldreth Library](/content/magic/relic/library-of-eldreth) and is consulted by scholars who come from every continent. The manuscript is in a state of preservation ensured by the careful stewardship of the librarians and is the standard against which the copies held in other libraries are compared.

The first complete publication was undertaken by the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) in the generation after the foundation of the academic study of the Art and was the occasion of the first extended debate over the doctrine. The publication was conducted with a care that the libraries have emulated in their own editions and is the foundation of the tradition of open dissemination of the text that the libraries have maintained.

## The Letters' Enduring Role in Instruction

The Letters on Pattern are a disputed but widely-circulated text attributed to the practitioners of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) and are the foundation of the academic study of the Art in the academies of the present age. The doctrine set out in the Letters, with its particular reading of pattern and resonance, is the most influential of any doctrine in the literature of the Art and is the foundation of the curricula of the academies of every continent. The dispute over the attribution has not been resolved and is unlikely to be resolved by the discovery of further evidence, but the dispute has not diminished the influence of the text. The Letters on Pattern remain one of the most accessible and least displaced of the texts of the Art, studied not by a ruling house or a priesthood but by the steady attention of scholars who have continued the work of instruction in the academies of the present day. The text endures in the libraries of the academies and in the instruction of practitioners as the living inheritance of those whose work is bound up with the systematic study of the Art.
