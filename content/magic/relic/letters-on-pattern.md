## Overview

The Letters on Pattern is a disputed but widely-circulated text attributed to the practitioners of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood), a body of writing that has shaped the academic study of the Art in the centuries since its first appearance in the libraries of the academies. The Letters are not a single treatise but a sequence of seven shorter texts, each addressed by a practitioner of the Fellowship to a younger colleague upon the subjects of the reading, the attunement, and the reshaping of pattern, and the texts are bound together in the form of a correspondence that the chronicler of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described as the most accessible introduction to the discipline of the Art that the traditions have produced. The chronicler of the [Magic](/content/magic/overview) notes that the Letters are the foundation of the academic study of the Art, and the Letters are the most cited of the texts that the academies employ in the instruction of their students.

## The Attribution and the Dispute

The Letters are attributed to the practitioners of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood), and the attribution is recorded in the prefatory note that accompanies the earliest of the manuscripts that have survived. The note records that the Letters were composed by a practitioner whose name is not given, and the note records that the practitioner was a member of the Fellowship at the clearing of Greenwell. The chronicler of the Sylvan Court has noted that the prefatory note is not consistent with the customary practice of the Fellowship, for the Fellowship does not ordinarily publish the work of its members, and the publication of the Letters would have been a notable departure from the customary reserve. The chronicler observes that the prefatory note is therefore either a departure from the customary practice or a fabrication that the publication required, and the chronicler declines to adjudicate between the two possibilities.

The dispute over the attribution is one of the most enduring of the controversies that surround the study of the Art, and the dispute has divided the scholars of the academies for as long as the Letters have been in circulation. The scholars who accept the attribution hold that the Letters are consistent with the discipline of the Fellowship, and the scholars point to the careful attention to the limits of the Art and to the costs of the practice that the Letters exhibit. The scholars who reject the attribution hold that the Letters are too systematic in their treatment of the laws to be the work of a single practitioner, and the scholars point to passages that they hold to be inconsistent with the customary reserve of the Fellowship. The chronicler notes that the dispute has not been resolved, and the chronicler observes that the dispute is unlikely to be resolved by the discovery of further manuscripts, for the manuscripts that have survived are all consistent with the prefatory note and the note is the only evidence of the attribution.

The chronicler of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has observed that the dispute over the attribution has been of less consequence to the study of the Art than the dispute over the doctrine, and the dispute over the doctrine is the more important of the two. The doctrine of the Letters is a particular reading of the laws of the Art, and the reading is not accepted by all of the traditions. The practitioners of the [Council of Khalisar](/content/government/local/council-of-khalisar) and the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) have published critiques of the doctrine, and the critiques have been the subject of extended debate in the academies. The chronicler records that the debate is conducted with a civility that is unusual in the disputes between the traditions, and the debate is the foundation of the academic study of the Art.

## The Letters Themselves

The Letters are seven in number, and each is addressed to a younger colleague whose name is not given. The first letter treats the subject of the reading, and the letter sets out the discipline of the practitioner in attending to the pattern of the subject. The chronicler of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described the letter as the most accessible introduction to the subject that the traditions have produced, and the letter is the text that the academies employ in the opening weeks of their instruction. The second letter treats the subject of the attunement, and the letter sets out the discipline of the practitioner in bringing the pattern of the practitioner into sympathy with the pattern of the subject. The third letter treats the subject of the reshaping, and the letter sets out the discipline of the practitioner in the act of the working itself. The fourth letter treats the subject of the limits, and the letter sets out the bounds within which the Art must work.

The fifth letter treats the subject of the costs, and the letter sets out the prices that the practitioner must pay for the workings that the practitioner performs. The chronicler of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has described the fifth letter as the most demanding of the seven, and the letter is the text that the academies employ in the closing weeks of their instruction. The sixth letter treats the subject of the tradition, and the letter sets out the practitioner's understanding of the place of the Art in the order of the world. The seventh letter treats the subject of the practitioner, and the letter sets out the practitioner's understanding of the change that the practice works in the practitioner who pursues it. The chronicler records that the seventh letter is the most personal of the seven, and the letter is the text that the academies reserve for the most advanced of their students.

The Letters are written in a measured prose that the chronicler of the [Magic](/content/magic/overview) has described as the most careful of any work in the literature of the Art, and the prose is the foundation of the reputation of the Letters as a model of the discipline. The prose avoids the technical vocabulary of the academies, and the prose employs the ordinary language of the practitioner in the field. The chronicler notes that the prose is one of the reasons that the Letters have been so widely circulated, and the prose is the reason that the Letters have been so often translated into the languages of the other traditions. The translations are not always consistent, and the inconsistencies have been the source of some of the disputes that the doctrine has provoked, but the prose of the original is held by the scholars to be clear enough to be reconciled with the customary readings.

## The Doctrine of Pattern

The doctrine that the Letters set out is a particular reading of the laws of the Art, and the reading is distinguished by the attention that it pays to the second of the laws, the law that the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has named the medium and that the older practitioners of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood) prefer to call pattern. The Letters hold that pattern is not a passive substance upon which the Art operates but a living order that the practitioner must learn to read and to follow, and the Letters hold that the practitioner who treats pattern as a passive substance will find the workings of the Art resistant and the bounds of the Art more restrictive than they need be. The chronicler of the Sylvan Court has observed that the doctrine is consistent with the discipline of the [Forest Fellowship](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood), and the chronicler has noted that the doctrine is one of the reasons that the attribution of the Letters to the Fellowship has been so widely accepted.

The Letters also set out a particular reading of the first of the laws, the law that the Sylvan Court has named the [Source](/content/magic/law/source) and that the older practitioners of the Forest Fellowship prefer to call resonance. The Letters hold that resonance is the sympathy that the practitioner walks when the practitioner follows the paths of pattern, and the Letters hold that the practitioner who treats resonance as a substance to be gathered and spent will find the workings of the Art empty and the bounds of the Art more restrictive than they need be. The chronicler notes that the doctrine of resonance in the Letters is consistent with the doctrine of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) but not with the doctrine of the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact), which holds that resonance is a substance that the practitioner may gather. The dispute is one of the most enduring of the controversies that surround the study of the Art, and the Letters are the most cited of the texts that the academies employ in the instruction of the doctrine.

## Influence on the Academic Study

The Letters have shaped the academic study of the Art in the centuries since their first appearance in the libraries, and the influence is observable in the curricula of the academies of every continent. The chronicler of the [Magic](/content/magic/overview) records that the first letter is the text that the academies of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) employ in the opening weeks of their instruction, and the fifth letter is the text that the academies employ in the closing weeks. The [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) and the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) have adopted the Letters as the foundation of their own curricula, and the academies of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) have included the Letters in the list of texts that the candidates for the higher degrees must master. The chronicler notes that the influence of the Letters is not confined to the institutions that the academies maintain, for the wandering practitioners of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood) have carried the Letters to the villages and the outposts of the forest margin, and the Letters are read aloud at the gatherings of the sworn fellows as a text of instruction.

The influence of the Letters is also observable in the publications of the academies, and the Letters are the most cited of the texts that the natural philosophers of the present age employ. The scholars of the [Library of Eldreth](/content/magic/relic/library-of-eldreth) have published an edition of the Letters with a commentary that the chronicler has described as the most thorough of any edition that the libraries have produced, and the edition is the standard text that the academies employ in their instruction. The chronicler notes that the publication of the edition was the occasion of the most extensive debate that the academic study of the Art has witnessed, and the debate is the foundation of the present state of the discipline.

## The Forest Fellowship and the Text

The relation of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood) to the Letters is the subject of the most enduring of the controversies that surround the text. The Fellowship has never formally acknowledged the Letters as the work of one of its members, and the Fellowship has never formally rejected the attribution. The chief warden of the Fellowship has, on several occasions, issued statements to the effect that the Fellowship does not take a position on the attribution, and the statements are the closest that the institution has come to a public position. The chronicler of the [Magic](/content/magic/overview) has observed that the reserve of the Fellowship is consistent with the customary reserve of the institution, and the reserve is one of the reasons that the attribution has remained in dispute.

The Fellows who have served at the clearing of Greenwell have, in their private communications with the scholars of the academies, been more forthcoming. The Fellows have admitted that the Letters are consistent with the discipline of the Fellowship, and the Fellows have admitted that the practitioners of the Fellowship have used passages from the Letters in their instruction of the apprentices. The Fellows have not, however, identified the author of the Letters, and the Fellows have not produced any documentary evidence that would resolve the dispute. The chronicler records that the reserve of the Fellows is one of the most distinctive features of the controversy, and the reserve is the foundation of the academic study of the text.

## The Text in the Libraries

The Letters are preserved in the libraries of every academy of natural philosophy in the known world, and the libraries are the principal repositories of the text. The original manuscript of the Letters is held by the [Library of Eldreth](/content/magic/relic/library-of-eldreth), and the manuscript is consulted by the scholars who come to the library from every continent. The chronicler records that the manuscript is in a state of preservation that the careful stewardship of the librarians has ensured, and the manuscript is the standard against which the copies that are held in the other libraries are compared. The copies that the other libraries hold are derived from the original, and the copies are the texts that the academies employ in their instruction.

The publication of the Letters has been undertaken by the libraries of the academies, and the publications are the foundation of the wide circulation of the text. The first complete publication was undertaken by the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) in the generation after the foundation of the academic study of the Art, and the publication was the occasion of the first extended debate over the doctrine. The chronicler notes that the publication was conducted with a care that the libraries have emulated in their own editions, and the publication is the foundation of the tradition of the open dissemination of the text that the libraries have maintained.

## Significance

The Letters on Pattern are a disputed but widely-circulated text attributed to the practitioners of the [Forest Fellowship of the Heartwood](/content/government/local/forest-fellowship-of-the-heartwood), and the text is the foundation of the academic study of the Art in the academies of the present age. The doctrine that the Letters set out, with its particular reading of pattern and of resonance, is the most influential of any doctrine in the literature of the Art, and the doctrine is the foundation of the curricula of the academies of every continent. The dispute over the attribution has not been resolved, and the dispute is unlikely to be resolved by the discovery of further evidence, but the dispute has not diminished the influence of the text. The chronicler finds in the Letters on Pattern one of the most accessible and least displaced of the texts of the Art, studied not by a ruling house or a priesthood but by the steady attention of the scholars who have continued the work of instruction in the academies of the present day. The text endures, in the libraries of the academies and in the instruction of the practitioners, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the systematic study of the Art.
