## The Principles That Govern the Practice

The Art is not practised without constraint, and the traditions have developed ethical frameworks that govern the conduct of practitioners. The frameworks differ in their details, reflecting the different values of the traditions, but they share a common foundation in the recognition that the reshaping of pattern is an act of consequence and that the practitioner who reshapes pattern bears a responsibility for the effects of the reshaping.

This page describes the ethical frameworks of the principal traditions, beginning with the principles that are common to all traditions, continuing with the distinctive ethical doctrines of each tradition, and concluding with the disputes that remain unresolved.

## The Principle of Consent

The most widely accepted ethical principle in the practice of the Art is the requirement of consent for any working upon a living person. The traditions agree that the pattern of a person is the most complex and the most resistant of any subject and that reshaping a person without consent is a violation of the order of the Art itself. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has been particularly insistent on this point and has published several accounts of practitioners who undertook the reshaping of a person without consent and were harmed in the attempt.

The principle of consent extends, in a lesser degree, to the reshaping of living things that are not persons. The traditions hold that a practitioner should not reshape a living creature without cause and that the reshaping of a creature for mere curiosity or amusement is beneath the dignity of the Art. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) is the most insistent on this point, and its practitioners will not reshape any living thing without first reading its pattern thoroughly and determining that the reshaping will not cause unnecessary harm.

The principle of consent does not apply to the reshaping of dead matter, and the most common workings of the Art involve the improvement of stone, metal, and other materials without any requirement of permission. The traditions differ on whether the reshaping of a place requires the consent of the people who live within it. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the consent of the forest's inhabitants, both human and otherwise, should be sought before any reshaping of the woodland is undertaken. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) holds that the consent of the property owner is sufficient.

## The Principle of Non-Harm

The second most widely accepted ethical principle is that the practitioner should not cause harm through the practice of the Art. The principle is stated in the most widely cited formulation by the chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court): the practitioner who reshapes pattern should ensure that the reshaping does not cause more harm than the original configuration.

The principle of non-harm is the foundation of the prohibition of several workings that the traditions have forbidden. The reshaping of a person without consent is forbidden because it causes harm to the person and to the practitioner. The forging of compelled sympathy is forbidden because it causes harm to the order of the Art. The severing of pattern is forbidden because it causes harm to the subject. The binding of will is forbidden because it causes harm to the person whose will is bound.

The principle of non-harm does not require that the practitioner avoid all harm, for the reshaping of pattern inevitably produces some change and some change may be harmful. The principle requires that the practitioner weigh the harm that the reshaping may cause against the benefit that the reshaping may produce and that the practitioner should not proceed with a reshaping that causes more harm than benefit.

## The Principle of Responsibility

The third widely accepted ethical principle is that the practitioner bears responsibility for the effects of the workings that the practitioner performs. The responsibility extends not only to the immediate effects of the working but also to the effects that the working may have over time and at a distance.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has been particularly insistent on the principle of responsibility and has published several accounts of practitioners who reshaped a pattern without considering the effects that the reshaping would have over time. The accounts describe practitioners who reshaped a building to improve its appearance and later discovered that the reshaping had weakened the structure, causing the building to collapse. The Council holds that the practitioner who reshapes a pattern bears responsibility for the effects of the reshaping, whether the effects were intended or not.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the principle of responsibility extends to the effect of the reshaping upon the practitioner as well as upon the subject. A practitioner who reshapes a pattern without considering the [cost](/content/magic/law/cost) that the reshaping will exact may find that the cost is greater than the benefit, and the Court holds that the practitioner is responsible for bearing the cost as well as for the effects upon the subject.

## The Principle of Restraint

The fourth widely accepted ethical principle is that the practitioner should exercise restraint in the practice of the Art. The principle of restraint is the foundation of the discipline of the long apprenticeship and the requirement of rest between workings, for the practitioner who practises the Art without restraint will cause harm to the practitioner and to the subjects of the workings.

The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) is the tradition most insistent on the principle of restraint, holding that the practitioner should practise the Art only when the practice is necessary and that the practitioner who practises the Art for mere curiosity or amusement is violating the ethical framework of the Art. The Fellowship's practitioners are trained to ask themselves whether a working is necessary before they undertake it and to refrain from working when the working is not necessary.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) takes a more practical view of restraint, holding that the practitioner should practise the Art with restraint because the unrestrained practice of the Art will exhaust the practitioner and diminish the practitioner's ability to practise when the practice is necessary. The Council's practitioners are trained to manage their practice with the same care that they manage their resources.

## The Ethical Frameworks of the Traditions

Each tradition has developed a distinctive ethical framework that reflects the values of the tradition and the patterns to which the tradition is attuned.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) places the greatest emphasis on the principle of non-harm, holding that the practitioner who reshapes pattern should ensure that the reshaping does not cause unnecessary harm to the living things of the forest. The Court's practitioners are trained to read the pattern of a subject thoroughly before reshaping and to consider the effects of the reshaping upon the whole forest rather than upon the subject alone.

The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) places the greatest emphasis on the principle of restraint, holding that the practitioner should practise the Art only when the practice is necessary and that the unrestrained practice of the Art is a violation of the order of the world. The Fellowship's practitioners are trained to ask themselves whether a working is necessary before they undertake it and to refrain from working when the working is not necessary.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) places the greatest emphasis on the principle of responsibility, holding that the practitioner who reshapes pattern bears responsibility for the effects of the reshaping and that the practitioner who causes harm through the practice of the Art is accountable to the community. The Council maintains a register of licensed practitioners and a system of accountability in which a practitioner who causes harm through the practice of the Art may be removed from the register and prohibited from practising.

The [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) places the greatest emphasis on the principle of consent, holding that the practitioner who reshapes pattern should obtain the consent of the subject before the reshaping is undertaken. The Compact's practitioners are trained to obtain the consent of the captain and the crew before performing a working upon a vessel and to obtain the consent of the person before performing a working upon a person.

The academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) have developed a more systematic ethical framework than any other tradition, treating the principles of consent, non-harm, responsibility, and restraint as the foundations of a code of conduct that the academies enforce among their practitioners. The academies' code of conduct is the most detailed of any tradition and is the subject of the most extensive study in the ethics of the Art.

## The Enforcement of Ethics

The traditions enforce their ethical frameworks through the registers of licensed practitioners and the systems of accountability that each tradition maintains. A practitioner who violates the ethical framework of the tradition may be removed from the register and prohibited from practising, and a practitioner who is removed from the register of one tradition is unlikely to be accepted by another.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has the most developed system of enforcement, maintaining a register of licensed practitioners who may be hired for particular workings and a system of accountability in which a practitioner who causes harm through the practice of the Art may be brought before the Council for judgement. The Council's judgements are recorded and published, and the records of the judgements form a body of precedent that the Council consults in the adjudication of new cases.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) maintains a less formal system of enforcement, relying on the discipline of the long apprenticeship and the oversight of the master to ensure that the practitioner observes the ethical framework of the Court. A practitioner of the Court who violates the ethical framework may be expelled from the Court and prohibited from practising in the forest.

The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) does not maintain a formal system of enforcement, holding that the practitioner who has undergone the discipline of the Fellowship will observe the ethical framework without external enforcement. The Fellowship's practitioners are trained to internalise the ethical framework of the Fellowship during the long years of the apprenticeship, and the Fellowship trusts that the practitioner who has internalised the framework will observe it.

## The Unresolved Questions

The ethical frameworks of the traditions are not fully consistent, and several questions remain unresolved. The principal unresolved question concerns the reshaping of a person who cannot give consent, such as a person who is unconscious or a person who is not capable of understanding the nature of the reshaping. The traditions do not agree on whether a practitioner may reshape a person in these circumstances.

The second unresolved question concerns the reshaping of a place that is inhabited by people who do not consent. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the consent of the inhabitants should be sought before the reshaping of a place is undertaken, while the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) holds that the consent of the property owner is sufficient.

The third unresolved question concerns the responsibility of the practitioner for the effects of a working that are not foreseeable. The traditions agree that the practitioner bears responsibility for the effects that could have been foreseen, but they do not agree on whether the practitioner bears responsibility for effects that could not have been foreseen.

These questions are the subject of ongoing debate among the traditions, and the practitioners of the present age continue to weigh the claims of the different frameworks as they practise the Art in the service of their communities.
