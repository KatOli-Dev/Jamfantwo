## Reading the Patterns of What Is to Come

The Art may be applied to the reading of patterns that are not yet fully formed, and the traditions have developed methods by which a practitioner may perceive the shape of things to come. The practice is called divination by some traditions and foresight by others, and the disputes about the nature and limits of the practice are among the most vigorous in the study of the Art.

This page describes the practice of divination and foresight as it is performed across the traditions, beginning with the principles that govern the reading of future patterns, continuing with the methods by which foresight is achieved, and concluding with the limits and risks that the practitioner must understand.

## The Principles of Foresight

The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) define foresight as the reading of patterns that are not yet fully configured. The definition is the most widely cited of any in the literature of the Art, and it has been adopted by every tradition that has produced a written account of the practice. The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) prefers the older term of divination, but the meaning is the same in practice.

The possibility of foresight depends upon the observation that patterns are not static configurations that spring into being at the moment they are perceived. A pattern forms over time, and the traces of its formation may be read before the pattern is fully configured. The practitioner who reads a pattern in its early stages of formation may perceive the shape of the pattern before it is complete, and this perception is the foundation of foresight.

The traditions disagree about the reliability of foresight. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the reading of a pattern in its early stages is reliable to the extent that the practitioner has attuned to the pattern and understands the forces that are shaping it. A practitioner who has read the pattern of a forest for many years may foresee the shape of the forest in the coming season with considerable accuracy, for the practitioner understands the patterns of growth and decay that shape the forest.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) holds that foresight is inherently unreliable, for the patterns of the world are shaped by many forces that the practitioner cannot fully perceive. The Council observes that a practitioner who attempts to foresee the outcome of a commercial venture may perceive the early traces of the pattern but cannot account for the accidents and the decisions of others that will shape the eventual result.

The academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) have not taken a position on the reliability of foresight, treating the question as one that cannot be settled by the evidence currently available. The academies have published several studies that attempt to measure the accuracy of foresight, but the results have been inconclusive, and the dispute continues.

## The Methods of Foresight

The traditions have developed distinct methods of foresight, each shaped by the patterns to which the tradition is most commonly attuned. The methods differ in the detail but share the same underlying structure: the practitioner reads the present configuration of a pattern, attunes to the forces that are shaping the pattern, and reads the traces of the future configuration that are present in the present.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) teaches a method called reading the season, in which the practitioner reads the pattern of the forest in the present and reads the traces of the coming season that are present in the present. A practitioner who reads the forest in the autumn may foresee the shape of the forest in the spring, for the patterns of the winter are present in the autumn in the form of the seeds, the hibernating creatures, and the bare branches that will endure through the cold months.

The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) teaches a method called the deep reading, in which the practitioner attunes to the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) and reads the patterns of the forest over a longer span than the single season. The Fellowship holds that the Heartwood itself carries the traces of the future within its present pattern, and a practitioner who has attuned deeply to the Heartwood may read these traces with a clarity that the practitioner who reads only the surface patterns cannot achieve.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) teaches a method called reading the trend, in which the practitioner reads the present movement of trade, population, or other patterns of human activity and extrapolates the future movement from the present direction. The Council's method is more systematic than the methods of the forest traditions and is based on the observation that the patterns of human activity tend to continue in the same direction until some force deflects them.

The [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) teaches a method called reading the weather, in which the practitioner reads the present patterns of wind, water, and sky and foresees the weather that will follow. The Compact's practitioners are among the most skilled in this method, for the patterns of the weather are more regular than many other patterns, and the practitioner who has attuned to the water and the wind may read the traces of the coming weather with considerable accuracy.

## The Reading of Signs

Many traditions also practise the reading of signs, in which the practitioner reads the patterns of the world as indications of the shape of things to come. The reading of signs is distinct from the methods of foresight described above in that the practitioner does not attune to a particular pattern but rather reads the configuration of the world as a whole as a sign of what is to come.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) teaches the reading of signs in the forest. The flight of birds, the behaviour of animals, and the growth of plants are all signs that the practitioner may read as indications of the coming season or of events that will affect the forest. The Court holds that the signs are reliable to the extent that the practitioner understands the patterns of the forest and the meaning of the signs within those patterns.

The [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) teaches the reading of signs in the sea and the sky. The colour of the water, the direction of the wind, and the behaviour of the creatures of the sea are all signs that the practitioner may read as indications of the coming weather or of events that will affect the vessel. The Compact's practitioners are among the most skilled in the reading of signs, and the navigators who cross the seas rely upon their readings for the safety of their vessels.

The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) does not practise the reading of signs, holding that the practice is too unreliable to be of practical use. The Council observes that a sign that is read one way may be read differently by another practitioner and that the interpretation of signs is more a matter of the practitioner's imagination than of the discipline of the Art.

## The Limits of Foresight

Foresight is bounded by the same limits that govern the Art as a whole. The practitioner who would foresee the future must attune to the present pattern of the subject and read the traces of the future within it, but the reading is limited by the extent of the practitioner's attunement and the complexity of the pattern.

The principal limit on foresight is the uncertainty that is inherent in the formation of patterns. A pattern that is not yet fully configured may take any of several shapes, and the practitioner who reads the traces of the future may perceive the most likely shape but cannot be certain that the pattern will take that shape. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) holds that the uncertainty is the reason that foresight should not be relied upon for decisions of consequence, and the Council's practitioners are trained to treat their foresight as a possibility rather than as a certainty.

The second limit is the influence of the practitioner's own will upon the pattern that is read. A practitioner who foresees a particular future may unconsciously shape the present pattern toward that future, and the foresight becomes a self-fulfilling reading. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the practitioner who would read the future must first still the will, for the will that is not stilled will shape the pattern that it reads.

## The Risks of Foresight

The principal risk of foresight is that the practitioner may become attached to the future that has been read and may attempt to shape the present toward that future, violating the ethical constraints of the Art. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has recorded several cases in which a practitioner who foresaw an unfavourable outcome attempted to reshape the present to avoid the outcome and caused harm in the attempt.

The second risk is that the practitioner may read a future that is harmful and may be harmed by the reading. The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) has recorded cases in which a practitioner who read the future of a person was so affected by what was read that the practitioner could not continue in the practice. The Fellowship holds that the practitioner who would read the future must be prepared to bear the knowledge of what is read, for the knowledge cannot be unread.

The practice of foresight is therefore approached with caution by all the traditions. The practitioner who would foresee the future must be trained in the discipline of reading, must be skilled in the methods of attunement, and must be prepared to bear the uncertainty and the risk that the practice entails.
