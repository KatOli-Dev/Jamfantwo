## The Principles That Govern Every Working

The Art is governed by four principles that hold across every tradition, however much the traditions disagree about their meaning. The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) first set these principles down in their present form, though the principles themselves are older than any court. They are the [Source](/content/magic/law/source), from which the Art draws; the [Medium](/content/magic/law/medium), upon which the Art operates; the [Limit](/content/magic/law/limit), which bounds what the Art may do; and the [Cost](/content/magic/law/cost), which the Art exacts from those who practise it. Each law is treated in its own page in the present collection, but a detailed treatment of the four laws together, with their interrelations and the disputes that surround them, is necessary to understand the Art as a whole.

This page sets down the four laws as they are commonly stated, with the principal variants and disputes among the traditions, and with an account of the ways in which the laws are interrelated. The disputes are not adjudicated here.

## Source: The Principle of Resonance

The first law states that the Art draws from resonance, a property of the world by which like things act upon like. Every thing in the world has its likeness, and every likeness is a path by which the thing may be reached. Resonance is not a force that flows between things nor a substance that can be gathered and spent. It is the principle of sympathy itself, and the practitioner of the Art is one who has learned to walk the paths of sympathy without losing the way.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) states the first law in the most widely cited form: resonance is the source of the Art, and the practitioner who would work the Art must find the paths of resonance that connect the pattern to the subject. The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) prefers the older term of sympathy, but the meaning is the same in practice.

The traditions agree that resonance is not measurable in the manner of weight or length and cannot be stored in a vessel. The [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) has been particularly insistent on this point and has published several accounts of failed experiments in which an attempt was made to gather resonance into a containment and release it at need. The accounts agree that the containment gathered nothing and the release produced nothing, and they are taken as the strongest evidence that resonance is not a substance.

The traditions also agree that resonance is not the gift of any god. The priests of various temples have on occasion claimed that the Art is a divine favour, a claim the practitioners receive with courtesy and polite refusal to engage. The temples have not pressed the claim, contenting themselves with the observation that the Art is part of the order of the world, a matter upon which the temples and the practitioners may agree.

The traditions disagree about whether sympathy may be deepened by practice. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) maintains that sympathy is fixed in the order of the world and that the practitioner may only follow paths that already exist. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) maintains that sympathy may be deepened by long practice and that a practitioner of sufficient skill may reach patterns an untutored practitioner cannot. The two positions are not far apart in practice: both agree that the practitioner must work within the sympathy that is given and cannot create a sympathy that does not exist.

## Medium: The Substance of Pattern

The second law states that the medium of the Art is pattern, the shape of a thing considered as a whole. A practitioner learns first to read the pattern of a thing, then to reshape it within the limits that the order of the world imposes. Reading is a discipline of attention; reshaping is a discipline of will. The medium is not a substance or a force but the configuration of a thing in all of its aspects, and the practitioner is one who has learned to perceive this configuration and to alter it.

The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) define the pattern of a thing as its configuration in all of its aspects, taken together. The configuration is not a list of properties or a sum of parts but the shape of the thing as a whole, including the relations of the parts to one another as well as the parts themselves. A stone has a pattern, and a tree has a pattern, and a storm has a pattern, and a moment has a pattern. The patterns differ in complexity and in coherence but are all of the same kind in the sense the traditions require.

The pattern of a thing is not the thing itself, and practitioners are at pains to mark the distinction. The stone endures, and its pattern endures with it. The stone is broken, and its pattern is broken with it. The stone is ground to powder, and its pattern is dispersed among the grains. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has likened pattern to the footprint relative to the foot, an analogy taken up by several academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach).

The patterns of the world vary widely in character, and the traditions distinguish several kinds of particular interest to the practitioner. The patterns of dead matter are the simplest and the most easily read and reshaped. The patterns of living matter are more complex and resist reading and reshaping with a vigour the practitioner must overcome. The patterns of persons are the most complex and the most resistant, for the pattern of a person is bound up with the will of the person, and the will is itself part of the pattern. The patterns of places are of a different order and are the subject of much of the practice of the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) and the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court).

## Limit: The Boundaries of the Art

The third law states that the Art is bounded by limits that no tradition has been able to exceed. The limits are not imposed by any authority nor are they the consequence of any failure of skill. They are properties of the order of the world, and the practitioner of the Art is one who has learned to work within them.

The first limit is the principle that resonance acts only between like things. A practitioner may only reach a subject through a path of sympathy, and the path exists only between the pattern of the practitioner and the pattern of the subject. The limit is observed in every working of the Art and is the foundation of the disciplines of attunement. A practitioner who wishes to reshape a pattern must first bring the pattern into sympathy with the subject through a discipline of attention and will. A practitioner who has not first attuned to the subject will accomplish nothing, and an attempt to reshape a pattern without attunement is a common cause of harm to the practitioner.

The second limit is the principle that the total of pattern in the world is conserved. A practitioner may reshape a pattern but may not create a pattern from nothing nor destroy a pattern entirely. Reshaping is a redistribution constrained by the conservation of the total. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) has likened the total of pattern to the weight in a balance, an analogy taken up by several of the academies. This limit is the source of the costs of the Art, for a practitioner who reshapes a pattern takes the substance of the pattern from their own pattern or from some other pattern in sympathy with them.

The third limit is the principle that the patterns of living things resist reading and reshaping with a vigour that the patterns of dead matter do not. A practitioner may read a stone with comparative ease and may reshape a stone within the bounds of the first two limits with little difficulty. A practitioner who attempts to read or reshape the pattern of a living thing encounters a resistance not found in dead matter, greater the more complex the living thing and the more vigorous its will.

The traditions agree on the three limits as stated and agree that the limits are not to be exceeded. They disagree about the edges of the limits and about the cases in which the limits are said to have been approached. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) holds that the first limit may be deepened by long practice, while the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) holds that it is fixed.

## Cost: The Price of Reshaping

The fourth law states that the Art exacts a price, recorded in the body and mind of the practitioner, in the pattern of the subject, and in the world that lies between them. The cost is not a penalty or a punishment but the price of reshaping the medium, and the practitioner who would practise the Art must be prepared to pay it.

The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) state the cost under five headings. The first is the effort of reading and reshaping, which manifests as bodily fatigue and a mental weariness that cannot long be maintained. The second is the strain that reshaping places upon the practitioner, a disturbance of the practitioner's pattern felt as stiffness of the joints, heaviness of the limbs, and slowness of the mind. The third is the backflow, the sympathetic effect of reshaping upon the practitioner, for the path of resonance that connects practitioner to subject is two-way, and the reshaping that travels from practitioner to subject is matched by a smaller reshaping travelling from subject to practitioner. The fourth is the drift, the cumulative marks a practitioner bears from a career of practice. The fifth is the reverberation, the tendency of a reshaped pattern to settle back toward its old shape.

The cost is borne in the body and mind over the years of a practitioner's career. The traditions teach the practitioner to bear the cost and to mitigate it through attunement, long apprenticeship, and the required period of rest. The cost is the measure of the Art and the reason the Art is taught only to those prepared to bear it.

## The Interrelation of the Laws

The four laws are interrelated in a manner that the traditions have long recognised. The source provides the means by which the Art operates; the medium is what the Art operates upon; the limits define the boundaries of possible operation; and the costs are the prices exacted by any operation. No tradition disputes the existence of these four categories, though the relative weight assigned to each varies considerably.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and the [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) treat the source and the medium as the foundations of the Art, with the limit and the cost following from them. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) treats the limit and the cost as the primary considerations, holding that the source and the medium are best understood through the boundaries they impose. The [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) treats the four laws as equal in standing, while the academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) treat all four as subjects of ongoing investigation.

The disputes about the relative weight of the laws are of greater interest to the traditions themselves than to the practitioner at work, who is occupied with reading and reshaping and has little time for theoretical questions. The questions are not without importance, however, for the answers the traditions give shape the disciplines they teach, and the disciplines in turn shape the workings they make their own.
