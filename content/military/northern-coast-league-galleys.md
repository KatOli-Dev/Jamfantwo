## The Galley Tradition

The [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league)
maintains a fleet of galleys that patrol the sheltered waters of its coastline
and the southern shores of the [Greater Inland
Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/greater-inland-sea). Unlike the
deep-water navies of [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and
[Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven), the League's fleet is
designed for operations in shallow coastal waters, where the seabed rises
quickly and where a deep-keeled ship would run aground. The galley, with its
shallow draught and its ability to move independently of the wind, is the ideal
vessel for these conditions.

The League was founded by a collection of coastal towns and small cities that
recognised the need for mutual defence against pirates and against the
expansionist ambitions of larger neighbours. Each member of the League
contributes ships, crews, or funds to the common fleet, and the fleet is
commanded by an Admiral elected by the League Council. The fleet has no
permanent home port; its squadrons rotate among the member harbours, spending
a month in each before moving on to the next.

## Ship Design

The standard galley of the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) is smaller than the triremes
of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league),
typically carrying sixty to eighty rowers in a single bank on each side. The
ships are built low to the water, with a shallow draught that allows them to
operate in waters where a deeper vessel would be unable to navigate. The hull
is carvel-built, with flush planking that reduces drag and improves speed.

The ram is the primary weapon of the League galley. It is fitted at the
waterline on the bow, projecting forward from the stem and sheathed in bronze.
The ram is designed to punch through the planking of an enemy hull, creating a
breach that will cause the enemy ship to take on water and sink. The League's
galley crews are trained in ramming tactics, and they practise the manoeuvre
against floating targets in sheltered bays.

## Organisation and Squadrons

The League fleet is organised into five squadrons, each assigned to a section
of the coastline. The Eastern Squadron covers the approaches from the [Auric
Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), where the waters are
deeper and where larger enemy ships might appear. The Western Squadron patrols
the straits that lead to the open sea, watching for pirates who use the
islands as bases. The Central Squadron protects the heart of the League, the
stretch of coast where the richest member cities are located. The Northern
Squadron operates along the coast toward the lands of the [Southern
Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach), and the Southern Squadron
covers the waters near the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic).

Each squadron is commanded by a Captain of the Coast, who serves for a term of
one year and who reports to the elected Admiral. The Captains of the Coast are
experienced seafarers who know the waters of their sector intimately, and they
are given considerable autonomy in the conduct of their patrols.

## The Crews

The crews of the League galleys are drawn from the coastal population, men who
have grown up on the water and who know the handling of small boats before they
ever step aboard a warship. Rowers are volunteers who serve for a share of the
prize money from captured pirate vessels, and the promise of plunder attracts
more men than the League can use during the summer raiding season. In winter,
when galleys are laid up and the crews are paid off, many rowers find work on
fishing boats or in the shipyards.

Training is conducted in the off-season, when the galleys are hauled ashore for
maintenance and the crews are given instruction in tactics and weapons handling.
The League maintains training grounds at several locations along the coast,
where rowers practise the stroke and marines practise boarding drills. The
training is practical and demanding, and those who fail to meet the standards
are dismissed from the fleet.

## Tactics

The Northern Coast League galleys rely on speed and surprise. A typical action
begins with the galleys approaching the enemy in line abreast, using their
oars to close rapidly while the marines on deck provide covering fire with
bows and javelins. As the galleys near the enemy, they form a line ahead and
attempt to ram the enemy vessels one by one. Once the ram has struck and the
enemy hull is breached, the marines board to finish the fight.

The League also employs fire as a weapon. Specially prepared pots of oil and
pitch are carried on the deck, ready to be ignited and thrown onto enemy ships.
Fire ships, old hulks filled with combustibles and set alight, are sometimes
used against anchored fleets or harbour installations. The use of fire is
carefully controlled, for the League's own ships are as vulnerable to flame as
any other.

## The Coast Watch

In addition to its galley squadrons, the League maintains a network of coastal
lookout stations that report the movement of ships to the nearest squadron
commander. The lookouts are positioned on headlands and hilltops along the
entire length of the League's coastline, and they communicate using signal
fires and semaphore flags. A ship can be spotted as soon as it appears on the
horizon, and the alarm can be passed along the coast at speed that far
outpaces any vessel.

The Coast Watch is manned by volunteers from the coastal settlements, men and
women who serve for a small stipend and for the right to claim salvage from any
wreck that occurs within their sector. The watch is particularly active during
the summer months, when pirate raids are most common, and it has proved its
worth many times over.

## Maintenance and Support

Galleys require constant maintenance to remain effective. The hulls must be
kept free of marine growth, the rigging must be inspected and replaced, and the
oars must be checked for cracks and warping. The League maintains repair yards
at three locations along the coast, each equipped with a slipway and a covered
shed where a galley can be hauled out of the water for inspection.

The repair yards are staffed by shipwrights who are skilled in the
construction and maintenance of galleys. They keep stocks of seasoned timber,
bronze for ram sheathing, and the pitch and tar needed to keep the hulls
watertight. A galley that is brought into a repair yard for maintenance can
expect to be returned to service within a week, assuming that no major damage
is discovered.
