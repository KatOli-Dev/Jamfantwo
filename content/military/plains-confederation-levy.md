## The Horse and the Rider

The levy of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation)
is the largest mounted force in the known world, a host of riders that can be
called together from across the vast grasslands that stretch between the [Crown
Range](/content/location/natural/feature/crown-range) and the eastern coasts.
The levy is not a standing army in the manner of the Vael infantry or the
Aldreth fleet. It is a gathering of free riders who answer the call of the
Confederation Council when the plains are threatened, and who disperse to their
homes and herds when the danger has passed. This system has served the
Confederation for centuries and has defeated invading armies that outnumbered
the levy on paper.

The bond between the Plainsfolk and their horses is the foundation of their
military strength. Children learn to ride before they learn to walk any
distance, and horses are treated as partners rather than mere transport. The
ponies and horses of the plains are hardy animals, capable of travelling fifty
miles in a day and surviving on the coarse grass that covers the prairie.
Every adult Plainsman owns at least two horses, and a prosperous rider may own
five or six, allowing the levy to maintain fresh mounts during a campaign.

## Centaur Riders

The [centaur](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) population of the plains forms
an integral part of the levy, contributing some of the most formidable warriors
in the known world. Centaur riders do not mount horses — they are their own
mounts, combining the strength and speed of a horse with the reach and
dexterity of a human upper body. A centaur warrior carries a composite bow of
horn and sinew and a long spear that can be used either overhand or couched
under the arm. In combat, centaurs are capable of manoeuvres that no human
rider can match, including rapid changes of direction and the ability to shoot
accurately at a full gallop.

Centaur clans maintain their own organisation within the levy, contributing
warriors under their own chieftains who coordinate with the human commanders of
the Confederation. The relationship between centaur and human within the levy
is one of mutual respect, forged through centuries of shared warfare. Centaur
warriors are often employed as the spearhead of an attack, breaking enemy
formations with the shock of their charge before the human riders sweep in to
exploit the disruption.

## The Calling of the Levy

When the Confederation Council determines that a military response is
necessary, the Speaker of the Council issues a call to the clans and
settlements. Runners and riders carry the message across the plains, and within
a week the first contingents begin to arrive at the designated gathering point.
Speed is of the essence, and the levy has been known to assemble in force
within a fortnight of the initial call, a feat that no infantry-based army can
match.

Each clan and settlement is assigned a quota of riders based on its population
and the extent of its grazing lands. A large clan may be required to provide
five hundred riders; a small settlement may contribute as few as twenty. The
riders bring their own horses, weapons, and provisions for the first thirty
days of the campaign. After that point, the Confederation treasury assumes
responsibility for supply, purchasing grain, fodder, and other necessities from
the merchants who follow the army.

## Tactics and Doctrine

The levy fights in a manner that exploits its mobility and avoids prolonged
close combat with heavier infantry. The standard tactic is to approach the
enemy in a loose formation, showering them with arrows from beyond the range of
their own missile weapons, then withdrawing before they can close. If the enemy
formation breaks or becomes disordered, the levy closes in for the kill,
riding down fleeing soldiers and cutting them apart with sabres and long knives.

Against heavily armoured infantry, the levy employs a different approach.
Riders target the flanks and rear of the formation, looking for weak points
where the armour is thinner or the soldiers are less experienced. They also
target the enemy's supply train and camp followers, disrupting the logistics
that keep an army in the field. A force that cannot feed itself cannot fight
for long, and the levy excels at making supply impossible for an invader.

## The Long March

One of the most remarkable capabilities of the Plains Confederation Levy is its
strategic mobility. A riding column can cover sixty miles in a day on good
terrain, and seventy or eighty if the need is urgent. This speed allows the
Confederation to concentrate its forces faster than any potential invader can
react, and it allows the levy to appear where it is least expected. The
strategic history of the plains is full of accounts of invaders who thought
they were facing a small force, only to discover that the main levy had ridden
around their flank and was threatening their line of retreat.

The levy is less effective in rough or forested terrain, where its mobility is
negated and its horses cannot manoeuvre. The commanders of the Confederation
are aware of this limitation and plan their campaigns accordingly, luring
enemies onto the open plains where the advantages of the levy can be brought to
bear. Defence of the plains is the primary mission, and the levy has little
interest in offensive operations beyond the borders of the grassland.

## Discipline and Leadership

Discipline in the levy is looser than in a standing army, but it is not absent.
Riders are expected to obey their clan chieftains and the appointed war leaders
of the Confederation, and those who break ranks or flee in the face of the
enemy face severe penalties, including exile from their clan. The social
pressure of the clan system is a powerful motivator, and riders fight hard to
avoid bringing shame upon their families.

War leaders are chosen by the Council from among the most experienced and
respected riders in the Confederation. They serve for the duration of the
campaign and are granted authority to make tactical decisions, negotiate
surrenders, and dispose of captured property. The best war leaders are those
who understand the strengths and weaknesses of the levy and who know when to
press an advantage and when to withdraw.
