## The Art of Defence

The fortifications of the known world reflect the resources, the geography, and
the military traditions of the realms that built them. From the dwarf-hewn
bastions of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) to the
river forts of [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), from the hilltop
citadels of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach)
to the wooden palisades of the [Northern
Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches), the variety of
defensive architecture is as wide as the variety of the realms themselves.
Every fortification is a compromise between the ideal of impregnability and the
practical limits of cost, labour, and available materials.

The purpose of a fortress is not merely to resist attack but to delay an enemy
long enough for a relief force to arrive. A garrison that holds out for a month
may give the defending realm time to raise an army that it could not have
assembled in a week. The great fortresses of the known world are positioned at
strategic points where a small force can block the advance of a much larger
army, buying time at the cost of the garrison's safety.

## Stone and Mortar

The finest stone fortifications in the known world are found in the [Stoneheim
Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), where dwarven masons have
raised walls that are the envy of every human realm. Dwarven stonework is
characterised by the precision of its fitting; the blocks are cut so carefully
that no mortar is needed to hold them in place, and the joints are so tight
that a knife blade cannot be inserted between them. Walls built in this manner
can withstand the pounding of siege engines for months without losing their
structural integrity.

Human fortifications vary in quality according to the wealth and skill of the
builders. The great citadels of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth)
and [Vaelmouth](/content/location/settlement/city/vaelmouth) are built of
dressed stone and are comparable in strength to the dwarven fortresses, though
they lack the refinement of dwarven work. Lesser fortifications are built of
rubble and mortar, faced with dressed stone on the outer surface where the
defenders expect the attack to fall.

## Earthwork and Timber

Not every realm can afford stone fortifications. The [Plains
Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) relies on
earthworks and timber palisades to defend its settlements, for stone is scarce
on the grasslands and the cost of transporting it would be prohibitive. An
earth rampart, faced with turf and topped with a timber palisade, can be raised
in a matter of weeks by a determined workforce, and it provides credible
protection against all but the most determined assault.

Timber fortifications are also common in the [Northern
Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches) and the [Frostwall
Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) territories, where the
abundance of wood compensates for the lack of suitable stone. The timber forts
of the north are built of logs laid horizontally and interlocked at the
corners, a technique that produces walls that are resistant to battering and
that can be repaired easily when damaged.

## Siege Engines

The siege engines of the known world can be divided into three broad categories.
Tension engines, such as the ballista and the scorpion, use the stored energy
of twisted skeins of sinew or hair to propel bolts or stones at flat
trajectories. These engines are used primarily against personnel, clearing the
walls of defenders and suppressing the fire of enemy engines. Ballistae can be
mounted on towers and on ships, and they are accurate enough to hit a man at
two hundred paces.

Torsion engines, including the mangonel and the onager, use a different
principle. A single arm, held under tension by a twisted bundle of ropes, is
released to swing forward and hurl a stone in a high arc. These engines are
less accurate than tension engines but can throw heavier projectiles, and they
are used to batter the walls and roofs of fortifications rather than to target
individual soldiers.

The trebuchet is the largest and most powerful siege engine in common use. A
counterweight is dropped, swinging a long arm that hurls a projectile from a
sling at the far end. Trebuchets can throw stones weighing three hundred pounds
or more, and they can demolish even the strongest walls if given enough time.
The largest trebuchets require crews of fifty or more men to operate and must
be assembled on site, a process that can take several weeks.

## Mining and Countermining

The siege technique most feared by defenders is mining. Sappers dig a tunnel
beneath the walls, supporting the roof with timbers as they go. When the tunnel
reaches the foundations of the wall, the timbers are set alight or removed, and
the wall collapses into the void. A successful mine can bring down a section of
wall that would have resisted bombardment for weeks.

Defenders counter mining by digging their own tunnels to intercept the
attackers. The counterminers listen for the sound of enemy digging, using
drums of water or simply pressing their ears to the ground to detect the
vibrations. When a countermine meets the enemy tunnel, a fierce underground
battle ensues, fought in darkness and confined spaces with short swords and
daggers.

## The Conduct of a Siege

A siege begins with the arrival of the attacking army before the walls of the
fortress. The first task is to surround the position, cutting off all routes by
which supplies or reinforcements could reach the garrison. The attackers then
establish their camp, digging trenches and building palisades to protect
themselves from sorties by the garrison.

The next phase is the approach. The attackers dig approach trenches, zigzagging
toward the walls to provide cover for the soldiers who will eventually assault
the breach. Siege engines are assembled and brought into position, and the
bombardment begins. The defenders respond with their own engines, with archers,
and with sorties that attempt to destroy the attacking engines.

The final phase is the assault. When a breach has been made in the walls, the
attackers send in their infantry to storm the position. The assault is the most
dangerous phase of the siege, for the defenders will concentrate all their
remaining strength at the breach. Successful assaults are rare against a
determined garrison, and most sieges end with the garrison surrendering when
food or water runs out.

## The Cost of Siege

Sieges are expensive in lives and resources. The attackers must feed a large
army for weeks or months, must manufacture or transport the materials for siege
engines, and must endure the casualties inflicted by the defenders. The
defenders suffer from hunger, disease, and the constant strain of standing
watch. Both sides know that a siege is a desperate business, and both sides
prefer to avoid one if possible.
