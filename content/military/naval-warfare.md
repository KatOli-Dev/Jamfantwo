## The Sea as a Battleground

Naval warfare in the known world is shaped by the same forces that shape the
societies that fight it. The [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea),
the [Greater Inland Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/greater-inland-sea),
and the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) are the
great highways along which trade flows and armies move, and control of these
waters is a prize worth fighting for. The realms that have invested in naval
power — [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth),
[Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven), the [Thessaran
League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league), and the [Northern Coast
League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) — have reaped
benefits in trade, security, and influence that far exceed the cost of their
fleets.

Naval warfare takes different forms in different waters. The open ocean demands
ships that can withstand storms and remain at sea for weeks. The inland sea
favours galleys that can manoeuvre in confined waters and exploit the calmer
conditions. The coastal waters require shallow-draft vessels that can pursue
pirates into the estuaries and creeks where larger ships cannot follow. Each
environment has produced its own tactics, its own ship designs, and its own
traditions.

## Ship Types

The warships of the known world can be divided into two broad categories: sail-
powered ships and oar-powered galleys. Sail-powered ships, such as those used
by [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and [Nordhaven](/content/location/settlement/city/nordhaven), are built for the open ocean and rely on the wind for
propulsion. They carry a crew of sailors to handle the rigging and a
complement of marines for combat. Their advantages are range and endurance; a
well-found sailing ship can remain at sea for months and can cross the widest
stretches of ocean.

Galleys, such as those used by the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) and the Northern Coast
League, rely primarily on oars, with sails used as auxiliary power. Galleys are
faster in calm conditions and can manoeuvre more nimbly than sailing ships, but
they are limited in range and endurance by the need to carry a large crew of
rowers. Galleys are most effective in sheltered waters where the wind is
unreliable and where the distances are short enough that the rowers can reach
their destination before exhaustion sets in.

## Ramming

The ram is the oldest and most decisive weapon in naval warfare. A galley,
driven by its oars at speed, can strike an enemy ship with its reinforced prow
and punch a hole in the enemy's hull. The ram is most effective when it strikes
at a right angle to the enemy's course, driving through the planking and
opening a breach that will quickly fill the enemy ship with water.

Ramming requires precise timing and judgement. The attacking ship must approach
at the right angle and the right speed, and the crew must be ready to reverse
oars immediately after the impact to pull the ram free. A ram that becomes
stuck in the enemy's hull will leave the attacking ship vulnerable to boarding
or to attack by other enemy vessels. The Thessaran League trireme crews
practise ramming constantly, and they have developed techniques that maximise
the damage while minimising the risk.

## Boarding

When ramming is not possible or not desired, victory at sea is achieved through
boarding. The attacking ship pulls alongside the enemy, grapnels are thrown to
hold the two vessels together, and the marines storm across to take the enemy
ship by force. Boarding is a brutal business, fought on a narrow, heaving
platform where there is no room to retreat and no cover from the enemy's
weapons.

Marines trained for boarding carry short swords, axes, and boarding pikes,
weapons that are effective in the confined space of a ship's deck. They are
armoured in mail or leather, with helmets that protect the head and face. The
objective is not merely to kill the enemy crew but to capture the ship intact,
for a prize ship is worth a significant sum in prize money.

## Catapults at Sea

Some warships carry light catapults mounted on the forecastle or aftercastle.
These engines hurl stones, pots of burning oil, or iron bolts at the enemy
ship, aiming to damage the rigging, kill the crew, or set the ship on fire.
Ship-mounted catapults are smaller than the siege engines used on land, and
they are less accurate, but they can still inflict serious damage.

The use of fire at sea is a terrifying prospect. A ship that catches fire is
difficult to save, for the timber of the hull burns readily and the pitch that
seals the planking provides fuel for the flames. Fire pots are thrown by
catapult or by hand, and fire ships — old hulks filled with combustible
material and set alight — are sometimes sent drifting into an anchored fleet.

## Fleet Tactics

Naval battles are won by concentration of force, by manoeuvre, and by the
quality of the crews. The ideal formation is the line of battle, with ships
arranged one behind the other so that each ship supports its neighbour and
prevents the enemy from breaking through. The line of battle allows the
commander to control the engagement, bringing the full weight of the fleet to
bear on a selected part of the enemy formation.

Against a line of battle, the enemy may attempt to break through at a weak
point, to outflank the formation, or to use fireships to force the line to
open. The commander who can anticipate the enemy's manoeuvres and respond
quickly holds the advantage. The quality of the signalling system — flags,
horns, or lamps — and the discipline of the captains determine whether the
fleet can execute the commander's plan.

## Logistics of Naval Warfare

A fleet at sea consumes supplies at a prodigious rate. Every sailor and marine
requires food and water, and the rowers of a galley require more than a
passenger, for their labour is exhausting. The ships themselves require
maintenance: ropes must be replaced, sails must be repaired, and hulls must be
cleaned of the marine growth that slows them down.

The logistic demands of naval warfare limit the range and duration of
operations. A fleet operating far from its bases must rely on supply ships that
are vulnerable to attack. The great navies of the known world maintain networks
of supply depots and friendly ports where their ships can replenish, and they
plan their campaigns around the availability of these facilities.

## The Prize System

Privateering — the use of privately owned ships to attack enemy commerce — is a
common feature of naval warfare. A privateer carries a letter of marque from
its government, authorising it to capture enemy merchant ships and to sell them
as prizes. Privateering provides a means of waging economic warfare without the
expense of maintaining a large fleet, and it has been employed by every
maritime realm at one time or another.

The prize system has its abuses. Privateers have been known to attack neutral
ships, to exceed the terms of their letters of marque, and to treat captured
crews with unnecessary cruelty. The [Greywash
Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) attempted to regulate privateering,
establishing rules for the inspection of prizes and the treatment of prisoners,
but enforcement has always been difficult.
