## A Trade Built on Observation and Text

The physician is a practitioner of the healing arts who treats the sick and injured through observation, diagnosis, and the application of remedies drawn from accumulated written knowledge, without recourse to magic. The distinction between the physician and the practitioner of magic is fundamental to the standing of the profession in every settled realm of the known world: the physician's authority rests on years of study, the memorisation of texts, and the accumulated case histories of the trade, not on the reading and reshaping of pattern that a practitioner of magic performs. In a city such as [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) or in the towns of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) valley, where a resonance practitioner may be scarce, expensive, or altogether absent, the physician is often the only trained healer that an ordinary household will ever consult, and the great majority of the sick and injured throughout the known world are treated by physicians, surgeons, midwives, bonesetters, and herbalists rather than by any tradition of magic.

The physician's trade is distinguished from the related trades of the surgeon, the midwife, the bonesetter, and the herbalist by its emphasis on internal complaints, on the diagnosis of disease from its outward signs, and on the prescription of remedies rather than the direct manual treatment of the body. A physician diagnoses a fever, a flux, a wasting sickness, or a disorder of the humours and prescribes a regimen of diet, rest, and medicine; the physician does not customarily set a bone, deliver a child, or cut into the flesh of a patient, tasks that fall to the surgeon, the midwife, and the bonesetter respectively. This division of labour is not absolute and varies considerably between regions, but it is recognised throughout the known world as the basic structure of the medical trades, and it shapes the training, the guild organisation, and the social standing of each branch.

## Education and the Long Course of Study

The training of a physician is a matter of years rather than months, and the physician's claim to authority rests substantially on the length and rigour of that training. In Aldreth, a young man or woman who aspires to the physician's trade is customarily apprenticed to a licensed physician for a term of seven years, during which the apprentice copies out and memorises the standard texts of the trade, accompanies the master on his rounds, and gradually assumes responsibility for the diagnosis and treatment of minor complaints under the master's supervision. The apprentice who completes the term and passes an examination before the assembled masters of the city's physicians is admitted to practice independently; the apprentice who fails may repeat the examination after a further year of study.

The academies of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach), situated at [Vereth](/content/location/settlement/town/vereth) and the lesser academies of [Ashaar](/content/location/settlement/town/ashaar) and [Torem](/content/location/settlement/town/torem), maintain a faculty of medicine alongside their faculties of natural philosophy and astronomy, and a physician who has studied at the Great Academy is regarded throughout the northern coast as having received the most thorough grounding available in the theory of the humours and the properties of remedies, even where the academy's practical instruction in the treatment of patients is less extensive than that offered by an apprenticeship under a busy city physician. A number of the wealthier physicians of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Amurra](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) send their sons and daughters to study at the Southern Reach for several years before returning to complete a shorter apprenticeship at home, a practice that combines the prestige of academic learning with the practical training that the academies do not fully provide.

The river valley of Vael presents a different pattern of training, for the valley supports few resident physicians of any standing and the household head of a farming family more commonly relies upon a herbalist, a traveling physician who circuits between market towns, or the counsel of the local priest of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) trained in the basic treatment of common ailments. The itinerant physician of the Vael valley typically trains under a master in one of the towns before adopting a circuit of villages that he visits on a fixed schedule tied to the market days, a pattern of practice unknown in the denser cities of the coast where a physician's clientele is concentrated within a single district.

## Guilds, Charters, and the Regulation of Practice

The physicians of the larger cities are organised into guilds on the same basic pattern as the trades described in the general treatment of [guilds and crafts](/content/economy/guilds): a charter granted by the sovereign or council of the city establishes the guild's authority to examine and license physicians, to discipline members who violate the guild's standards, and to represent the interests of the trade before the governing council. The College of Physicians of Aldreth, chartered by the crown some generations past, examines every candidate who wishes to practise within the city's jurisdiction and maintains a register of licensed physicians that the city's magistrates consult when a dispute arises over a physician's competence or conduct. A physician who practises within the city without having passed the college's examination is liable to a fine and to the confiscation of his instruments and stock of remedies, though the college's authority does not extend beyond the city's own jurisdiction, and a physician licensed in Aldreth must seek separate recognition, or rely on informal reciprocity, before practising in another city.

The physicians of the coastal cities of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) organise themselves somewhat differently, reflecting the council-based governance common to that coast. In Khalisar, physicians are examined and licensed by a committee of the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) itself rather than by an independent guild, a practice that gives the city's magistrates a more direct hand in medical regulation than is customary in Aldreth but that also ties the standards of practice to the shifting composition of the council from one term to the next. The merchant families of Khalisar who maintain trading relationships across the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) and the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) often retain a household physician on a fixed annual fee, a practice that provides the physician with a stable income in exchange for priority attendance upon the household's members.

In the river realm of [Valdmark](/content/government/national/valdmark), where settlement is more thinly spread along the interior valley than in the coastal cities, physicians are too few in number to sustain an independent guild in most towns, and the trade is instead regulated through the holding courts described in the general treatment of Valdmark governance, which examine candidates for a licence to practise within the holding's jurisdiction and record the licence in the holding's own registers. A physician who moves between holdings must ordinarily be re-examined by the court of the new holding, though a physician of established reputation is sometimes admitted on the strength of a letter of recommendation from his previous court without a further formal examination.

The dwarven holds of [Stoneheim](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) treat the physician's trade according to the same principle that governs the smiths' guild described in the general treatment of dwarven guilds: authority derives from the accumulated tradition of the hold rather than from a charter granted by a sovereign, and a physician of the holds is recognised not through an examination at a fixed point in training but through years of service and the gradual accumulation of reputation among the hold's elders. The dwarven physician does not hold a licence in the sense familiar to the human cities, but a dwarven physician whose remedies repeatedly fail or whose diagnoses prove unreliable is simply given fewer patients and less standing within the hold, a form of regulation that operates through communal judgment rather than formal sanction.

## Social Standing among the Medical Trades

Among the trades of the healing arts, the physician generally enjoys the highest social standing, a position that rests on the length of the physician's training, the physician's command of written learning, and the physician's customary clientele among the wealthier households of a city or realm. The surgeon, who works directly upon the body with knife and needle, is typically ranked below the physician in social esteem despite the manual skill the surgeon's trade demands, for the handling of blood and flesh is regarded in most cities as a lesser undertaking than the physician's diagnosis of disease from its outward signs and the physician's prescription of remedies according to written authority. Bonesetters, who commonly learn their trade through direct apprenticeship rather than through the physician's course of texts, occupy a standing closer to that of the ordinary craftsman than to that of the physician, though a skilled bonesetter is valued highly in any community that depends on manual labour and is vulnerable to the falls and fractures that such labour produces.

Midwives, who attend the great majority of births throughout the known world, occupy a position apart from the guild structures that govern the physicians and surgeons of the cities, for the midwife's trade is passed customarily from woman to woman within a household or a neighbourhood rather than regulated through apprenticeship and examination, and a physician is rarely called to attend a birth except where the midwife judges the case to exceed her own skill. Herbalists, who prepare and sell the remedies that physicians prescribe and that ordinary households use without consulting a physician at all, occupy a standing that varies considerably with the herbalist's reputation and the rarity of the substances in which she deals; an herbalist who supplies the physicians of a wealthy city with imported remedies from Sahrana or the southern lands may command a respect close to that of the physicians she supplies, while a village herbalist who gathers common plants from the surrounding countryside is regarded as a useful but modest tradeswoman.

## Physicians and the Practitioners of Magic

The relationship between the physician's trade and the traditions of magical [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration) is one of clear separation rather than rivalry in most of the known world, for the two arts operate on entirely different principles and are consulted for different reasons. A [practitioner](/content/magic/practitioners) of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) or the Khalisar Council reads and reshapes the pattern of an injured or diseased body, working within the limits and at the cost that the order of magic imposes, while the physician diagnoses the same complaint through observation of its outward signs and treats it through diet, rest, and prescribed remedies drawn from written authority. A magical practitioner able and willing to heal a person is, in most cities, rare and costly to engage compared with a physician, and the households that can afford to summon a practitioner for a serious injury or illness are commonly the same wealthy merchant and noble families who also retain a physician on permanent call; the ordinary household of a farmer, a labourer, or a small tradesman relies on the physician, the surgeon, the bonesetter, and the midwife as a matter of course; and consults a magical practitioner, when one is to be found nearby at all, only in the direst extremity.

The physicians of the larger cities do not, as a rule, regard the practitioners of magic as competitors, for the clientele and the cost of the two trades rarely overlap, and a physician who exhausts the remedies available to his own trade will sometimes recommend that a wealthy patient seek out a practitioner of the Khalisar Council, whose practical approach to the restoration of function is regarded by many city physicians as more compatible with their own diagnostic habits than the more contemplative methods of the forest traditions. The reverse recommendation is less common, for a practitioner of magic who has attuned to a patient's pattern and undertaken a working of healing has little further need of the physician's remedies, though a practitioner will sometimes advise a patient to consult a physician for the management of a chronic condition, such as a wasting sickness or a disorder of long standing, that magic can ease but not resolve outright. The two trades thus coexist in most cities as parallel and largely separate institutions, each drawing on a distinct body of knowledge and each serving a clientele defined more by circumstance, wealth, and the availability of a practitioner than by any settled preference between the reshaping of pattern and the physician's remedies.
