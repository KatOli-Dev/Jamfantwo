## The Mundane Study of Poison

The study of poison outside the practice of magic is a discipline shared among several trades rather than the possession of any single one. Herbalists who gather and cultivate plants for the treatment of illness, apothecaries who compound and sell remedies, and physicians who attend the sick and injured all acquire, in the ordinary course of their training, a working knowledge of which substances harm the body and how that harm may be resisted or reversed. No formal boundary separates the herbalist's knowledge of a toxic root from the physician's knowledge of the same root's antidote, and a household that keeps a well-stocked stillroom is as likely to hold the remedy for a poisoning as the poison itself. This overlap is deliberate in the training of all three trades, for the gatherer who cannot distinguish the wholesome herb from its poisonous look-alike is a hazard to the community, and the healer who cannot recognise the symptoms of poisoning is unable to treat the ordinary illnesses that share those symptoms.

The mundane treatment of poisoning is distinct from the magical treatment practised by the orders described in the discipline of [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration), which relies upon the reading and reshaping of a living pattern and is available only where a trained practitioner of magic can be summoned in time. The greater part of the known world's population, in the countryside and in the poorer quarters of the cities, has no such recourse, and the mundane knowledge of poison and antidote described here is the only defence available to them. Even where magical healing can be summoned, the delay between the onset of poisoning and the arrival of a practitioner often means that mundane first response determines whether the patient survives to receive it.

## Sources of Poison Known to the Herbalist

The plant poisons best documented by the herbalists and apothecaries of the known world are those that resemble food or medicine closely enough to be taken by accident. The [death cap](/content/species/flora/death-cap) of the Aethelar forests is the most notorious of these, its toxins producing a deceptive recovery between the first violent illness and the organ failure that follows days later, and its resemblance to edible fungi accounts for the greater number of accidental poisonings recorded in the forested regions of Aethelar and Morvind. Herbalists teach their apprentices to distinguish the death cap from its edible neighbours by the volva at the base of the stem and the colour of the spore print, the same tests that foragers among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the Aethelar Heartwood have used for generations. Other plants are dangerous not because they resemble food but because they are themselves medicines whose margin between remedy and poison is narrow. The [arnica](/content/species/flora/arnica) of the Morvind Uplands relieves bruising and sprains when applied as an oil to unbroken skin but is toxic if it enters the body through a wound or is taken internally in more than the most minute quantity, and healers who prepare arnica are trained from the outset in the discipline of dosage that separates its beneficial and harmful uses. The apothecary's stores therefore contain, side by side, the herb that heals and the herb that kills, distinguished only by preparation, dose, and the route by which it enters the body.

## Venomous Creatures and Their Bites

Beyond the poisons of plants, the mundane physician must also reckon with the venoms delivered by living creatures, a hazard more common to travellers, herders, and soldiers than to the settled population of the cities. The bite of the [Kharesh glass spider](/content/species/fauna/kharesh-glass-spider) is among the most feared hazards of desert travel, its venom capable of causing swelling, paralysis, and death if untreated, and the desert peoples respond with traditional remedies that include the crushed leaves of the yarrow plant, the binding of the limb above the wound to slow the spread of the venom, and the drawing of poison from the bite by the application of a heated stone. The [cockatrice](/content/species/fauna/cockatrice) of the rocky hill country delivers a venomous bite whose precise action remains debated among the naturalists of the academies, though its potency is not disputed, and the venom itself has been put to a controlled medical use as a paralytic agent in certain surgical procedures when collected under the licence of the apothecary guilds. The [basilisk](/content/species/fauna/basilisk) of the deep forest presents the gravest case, for its venom is of a potency the apothecaries have not been able to reproduce or counter in any preparation, and a basilisk bite is treated by the physicians who attend it as very often fatal regardless of the response. Where the venom of a creature is not lethal in the ordinary run of cases, the physician's task is chiefly to slow its spread through the body and to support the patient through the period of illness that follows, using many of the same techniques employed against plant poisons: the binding of the affected limb, the induction of vomiting where the poison has been swallowed rather than injected, and the application of remedies believed to draw or neutralise the toxin.

## Mundane Antidotes and the Limits of Treatment

The antidotes available to the physician who works without magic are for the most part supportive rather than curative, aimed at relieving the symptoms of poisoning and sustaining the patient's strength while the body clears the toxin on its own. Willow bark is used to relieve the pain that accompanies poisoning, and preparations of milk [thistle](/content/species/flora/thistle) seed are given in the belief that they protect the liver from the damage that certain poisons, including the death cap's, inflict upon it. Where a poison has been swallowed and not yet absorbed, the induction of vomiting is the most direct mundane intervention, and physicians of every tradition are taught to attempt it in the early hours after a suspected poisoning, before the point at which the toxin has passed into the blood and the opportunity for this simple remedy has closed. A body of lore, more persistent than well founded, holds that the horn of the [unicorn](/content/species/fauna/unicorn) possesses a particular virtue in countering poison, and apothecaries of the woodland regions continue to prize fragments of horn obtained through lawful means for this reputed property, though naturalists who have studied the claim have reached no settled conclusion as to its truth. The honest physician tells a poisoned patient plainly that many poisons, once fully absorbed, cannot be drawn out or neutralised by any mundane means known, and that treatment beyond that point consists of supporting the body's own functions and hoping that the dose taken was not lethal.

## Detecting Poisoning Without Magic

The detection of poisoning before it can be treated is complicated by the fact that many poisons produce symptoms resembling ordinary illness, a resemblance that is often the reason a poisoner chooses one substance over another. The death cap's initial symptoms of vomiting and abdominal pain are frequently mistaken for severe food sickness, and the false recovery that follows can lull both patient and physician into believing the crisis has passed before the underlying damage becomes apparent. Physicians and apothecaries who suspect poisoning look first to the circumstances, questioning what the patient has eaten or drunk in the hours before the onset of illness and, where possible, examining any remaining food, vomit, or waste for identifiable fragments of a poisonous plant or fungus. The pattern and timing of symptoms are themselves diagnostic, for physicians who have attended many cases of poisoning learn to recognise the particular sequence associated with each common poison, distinguishing it from the more haphazard course of a natural fever or digestive complaint. No mundane method exists to detect a poison's presence in food or drink before it is consumed, save for the ordinary caution of tasting or smelling a suspect dish, and this limitation is the reason the practice of poisoning is difficult to prevent and, when carried out with care, difficult to prove.

## Poison in the Politics of Courts and Councils

The knowledge of poison and its concealment has an ordinary, if unwelcome, place in the political life of the courts and councils of the known world, where the wealth and power concentrated around a throne, a senate seat, or a council chair have long made poisoning a recognised hazard of high office. The delayed and ambiguous symptoms of poisons such as the death cap's have made them, according to the chronicles of several Aethelaran kingdoms, the instrument of choice for those who wish to remove a rival while avoiding detection, and the keeping of food tasters at the tables of the nobility is a long-established precaution among ruling houses that can afford it, one intended less to guarantee safety than to signal to would-be poisoners that the risk of discovery has been raised. The councils of merchant republics and the senates of trading cities, where high office is contested among rival houses and factions rather than inherited, are not exempt from this hazard, for the same concentration of wealth and influence that makes a throne worth seizing makes a senate seat or a council chair worth removing a rival to obtain. The precaution against poison is treated by the households of the great as an ordinary cost of power, comparable to the guarding of a treasury or the maintenance of a private guard, rather than as evidence of any particular scandal, and the physicians and apothecaries retained by such households are valued as much for their vigilance against poison as for their skill in treating the ordinary illnesses of their patrons.

## Regulation of Poisonous Substances

The trade in substances capable of poisoning, whether sold as medicine, as a component of tanning or dyeing, or as a pest control for granaries and stores, is regulated in most cities through the same guild structures that govern the practice of the apothecary and the herbalist. The [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) that certify a master apothecary's competence typically require a demonstrated knowledge of the poisonous properties of the substances the apothecary compounds, and the sale of the more dangerous preparations, such as concentrated arnica oil or the dried root of certain toxic plants, is restricted to buyers who can show a legitimate medical purpose. The criminal codes of the settled kingdoms treat poisoning as murder or attempted murder when it can be proven, and the difficulty of proof described above is precisely why prosecutions for poisoning are rare even where the suspicion is widespread, a gap between suspicion and proof that the [criminal law](/content/law/criminal-law-and-punishment) of every major jurisdiction acknowledges without having found a reliable remedy for it. The regulation of substances capable of altering the mind or body for purposes other than poisoning, such as the [dream leaf](/content/species/flora/dream-leaf) of the Kharesh oases, follows a comparable pattern of guild licensing and criminal penalty, reflecting a broader recognition among the lawmakers of the known world that a substance valuable in careful hands is dangerous in careless or malicious ones.

## The Physician's Understanding of Poison

Physicians who study poison without recourse to magic understand their knowledge as incomplete and are generally candid about its limits with patients and students alike. The mechanisms by which a given poison harms the body are understood only in broad outline in most cases, described in terms of the organs affected and the sequence of symptoms rather than in terms of any deeper explanation of cause, and the treatments available are for the most part the product of generations of accumulated observation rather than a settled theory of how poison and antidote interact within the body. This state of knowledge is neither static nor complete, and physicians continue to record and compare cases of poisoning across the cities and courts of the known world in the hope of improving both the detection of poison and the treatment of those who have suffered it, work that proceeds slowly and without the certainty that any single case will yield a remedy applicable to the next.
