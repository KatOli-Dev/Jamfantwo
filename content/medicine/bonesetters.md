## A Craft Apart from the Medical Guilds

The bonesetter is a specialist in the treatment of broken and dislocated bones who works outside the formal structures that govern physicians and surgeons in most cities of the known world. Where a physician trains through years of study in a chartered college and a surgeon serves an apprenticeship registered with a barber-surgeons' guild, the bonesetter typically learns the craft through a household lineage, a private apprenticeship arranged outside any guild register, or, in rural districts, through informal instruction by an established practitioner who takes on a promising helper without any contract of indenture. This distinction in training is the foundation of the bonesetter's separate standing in every region where the trade is practised, and it accounts for the frequent friction between bonesetters and the more formally credentialed orders of healers.

The origins of bonesetting as a distinct trade are not recorded in any single chronicle, but the practice appears to have arisen independently in agricultural and pastoral communities where broken bones were a common consequence of labour with animals, falls from height, and accidents with tools, and where no physician or surgeon was within convenient reach. In such communities, a household or family that developed skill in reducing fractures and dislocations retained and transmitted that skill across generations, and the resulting trade persisted even as towns grew large enough to support chartered medical guilds. The [Guilds and Trades](/content/economy/guilds) of the cities generally did not absorb the bonesetters into their structures, since the bonesetter's methods rested on manual skill and inherited judgment rather than the theoretical instruction that the physicians' colleges required for admission, and since the bonesetters themselves frequently resisted the fees and restrictions that guild membership would impose.

## Hereditary Lines and Informal Apprenticeship

In many rural districts of [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and the interior kingdoms, bonesetting is practised by families who have held the trade for generations, the skill passing from parent to child along with the accumulated knowledge of which manipulations succeed for which injuries. A family of bonesetters typically maintains a modest reputation across several neighbouring villages, and the household is consulted whenever a labourer falls from a hayloft, a rider is thrown from a horse, or a child breaks a limb in play. The family does not maintain written records of its methods in most cases, the knowledge being retained instead in the practised hands of the elder members and transmitted to the younger through direct demonstration and correction over years of assisting at the working.

Where no hereditary line exists, a young person with an aptitude for the trade may be taken on by an established bonesetter as a helper, in an arrangement that resembles the apprenticeship of the guild trades but lacks any formal contract of indenture or registration with a civic authority. The helper assists at reductions, learns to prepare splints and dressings, and gradually takes on simple cases under supervision before being permitted to treat patients independently. This informal apprenticeship may last anywhere from three to eight years depending on the aptitude of the learner and the willingness of the master to release control of the practice, and it produces practitioners whose skill varies considerably from one district to the next, since there is no common examination or standard against which the finished bonesetter is measured.

The nomadic peoples of the deserts and the grasslands maintain their own traditions of bonesetting adapted to the conditions of travel. Among the [Kharesh](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) and [Vashari](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) tribes of the sand seas, the treatment of fractures sustained in falls from camels or in accidents with the herds is the responsibility of an elder of the camp who has learned the skill through long observation and practice, often a woman of the tribe who also oversees the birthing of children and the treatment of common ailments. The centaur kindreds of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) treat fractures among their own kind through a tradition of manipulation adapted to the particular anatomy of the centaur leg and spine, a body of knowledge that human bonesetters who have studied among the plains peoples regard as considerably more sophisticated than anything practised in the settled cities, owing to the frequency of leg injury among a people who travel constantly at speed across uneven ground.

## Techniques of Reduction

The reduction of a fracture, by which the bonesetter returns the broken ends of a bone to their proper alignment, is accomplished chiefly through traction and manipulation applied by hand. The bonesetter grasps the limb above and below the break and applies steady pulling force to draw the bone ends apart before guiding them back into alignment, a procedure that causes considerable pain to the patient and that is frequently performed with the assistance of one or two helpers who hold the patient still or apply counter-traction from the opposite direction. Bonesetters of experience learn to judge the degree of traction required for different bones and different patients by the resistance felt in the muscle and by the sound and feel of the bone ends engaging, a judgment that the trade's practitioners hold cannot be taught from any description and must be acquired through the handling of many cases.

The reduction of a dislocated joint follows different principles from the reduction of a fracture, since the bonesetter must work the joint back through the same path by which it was displaced rather than simply realigning two broken surfaces. The shoulder, the most commonly dislocated joint among labourers and riders, is reduced through several methods that vary by region and by the individual bonesetter's training, some favouring traction applied with the patient's arm raised overhead, others favouring a method in which the bonesetter braces a foot or knee against the patient's ribs while pulling the arm outward and downward. The [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) has recorded that its healers regard certain traditional bonesetter methods of shoulder reduction as effective but crude compared to the gentler techniques available through the reshaping of pattern, though the Council acknowledges that the bonesetter's methods succeed in the great majority of cases and require no aptitude for magic whatsoever, which makes them the only recourse available to most of the population.

Dislocations of the fingers, the jaw, and the smaller joints are generally regarded as the simplest cases and are often treated by bonesetters with only a few years of training, while dislocations of the hip are regarded as the most difficult, requiring substantial force and carrying a significant risk of injury to the surrounding vessels and nerves if the manipulation is performed incorrectly. A bonesetter's reputation in a district frequently rests on a small number of difficult cases successfully treated, word of which travels between villages and establishes the practitioner's standing more effectively than any credential could.

## Splinting Materials and Aftercare

Once a fracture has been reduced, the bonesetter immobilises the limb to allow the bone to knit in proper alignment, using materials that vary by region and by the resources available to the practitioner. In the forested regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), splints are commonly fashioned from thin strips of willow or ash wood, padded with wool or linen wadding and bound with linen strips soaked in a mixture of egg white and flour that stiffens as it dries, producing a rigid casing similar in function to more elaborate plaster methods used in the larger cities. In the desert regions, where wood is scarce, bonesetters among the Kharesh and Vashari more often use hardened leather, softened in water and shaped around the limb before being allowed to dry stiff, or bundles of reed bound tightly with cord. The dwarven halls of [Stoneheim](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and [Karhold](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) favour splints of thin beaten metal, padded with cloth, a method that dwarven bonesetters hold provides superior rigidity for the treatment of the heavier dwarven bone structure, though the metal splints are costly to produce and are reserved for serious fractures.

The bonesetter typically instructs the patient or the patient's household in the care of the splinted limb, including the signs of poor circulation that indicate the splint has been bound too tightly and must be loosened, and the approximate period for which the splint must remain in place, which the trade's practitioners judge by the location and severity of the fracture and by the age of the patient, younger patients being expected to knit more quickly than the old. The bonesetter generally returns to inspect the limb at intervals during the healing period, adjusting the splint as any swelling subsides and testing the limb's stability before finally removing the splint and instructing the patient in the exercises thought to restore strength and movement to a limb that has been immobilised for a period of weeks.

Complications such as a fracture that fails to knit properly, a limb that shortens or twists as it heals, or an infection arising from a fracture that has broken through the skin are beyond what most bonesetters undertake to treat, and a bonesetter confronted with such a case will commonly refer the patient to a surgeon or a physician if one is available within reasonable travel, an acknowledgment of the limits of the bonesetter's training that is one of the few points of regular cooperation between the bonesetter's trade and the formally credentialed orders of healers.

## Standing Relative to Physicians and Surgeons

The social standing of the bonesetter varies considerably by region but is almost everywhere lower than that of the physician and generally somewhat below that of the surgeon, reflecting the absence of formal education and guild credential rather than any general judgment on the bonesetter's competence. In the cities, where physicians hold standing as learned men admitted to a chartered college and surgeons hold standing as guild craftsmen who have completed a recognised apprenticeship, the bonesetter is regarded as a tradesman of uncertain and unverifiable training, consulted by those who cannot afford a physician's fee or who live too far from a chartered surgeon to summon one promptly. Physicians in several cities have argued before the councils that the practice of bonesetting by untrained persons endangers the public and have sought to bring the trade under guild regulation or to prohibit its practice outright, though these efforts have generally failed, since the bonesetters treat a volume of patients, particularly among the labouring population, that the physicians and surgeons of most cities are neither numerous enough nor willing enough to serve at an affordable price.

In the countryside and among the nomadic peoples, the bonesetter's standing is considerably higher, since the bonesetter is frequently the only healer of any kind within reach and since the trade's reputation rests entirely on the visible success or failure of its treatments rather than on any credential. A skilled bonesetter in a rural district may be as respected as the local healer of any other kind, consulted for fractures and dislocations before any physician would even be sent for, and a family that has held the trade for generations may carry a standing in the community comparable to that of the smith or the miller, trades likewise founded on inherited and demonstrated skill rather than formal schooling. The tension between the rural regard for the bonesetter and the urban physician's suspicion of the trade is a recurring feature of disputes over the regulation of healing across the known world, and it has not been resolved uniformly in any kingdom, each city and each region continuing to strike its own balance between the convenience and cost of the bonesetter's services and the more thorough, and more expensive, training of the guild-credentialed physician and surgeon.
