## The Wounded of the Campaign

Every army that takes the field in the known world travels with a body of practitioners whose task is to keep as many soldiers alive as the resources of the camp allow. These field surgeons and their attendants are distinct from the physicians who serve the wealthy in the cities and from the healers of the magical orders described elsewhere; the great majority of soldiers who fall in battle are treated, if they are treated at all, by hands that carry no magic and by methods developed through generations of experience with the wounds that spears, arrows, and blades inflict. The practice of battlefield medicine varies with the wealth of the army that maintains it, the terrain over which the campaign is fought, and the customs of the realm that raised the soldiers, but certain common problems recur in every conflict: the sorting of the wounded by the severity of their injuries, the closing and binding of wounds under field conditions, the decision to remove a shattered limb, and the movement of the injured away from danger and toward a place where they might recover.

The record of these practices survives unevenly. The chronicles of the [Three-Banners War](/content/history/three-banners-war) note the hardship suffered by the wounded of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) during the retreat to the river valleys in the first phase of that conflict, and the accounts of the [Stoneheim Pass War](/content/history/stoneheim-pass-war) describe the treatment of the garrisons that held the watch-towers under siege. The [Northern Marches Frontier Wars](/content/history/the-northern-marches-frontier-wars) produced a body of practical knowledge among the militia surgeons who treated wounds inflicted in raids and skirmishes across several generations, knowledge that was passed down informally from one generation of camp attendants to the next rather than recorded in any single treatise.

## Sorting the Wounded

When an engagement produces more casualties than the attendants of a camp can treat at once, a system of sorting determines the order in which the wounded receive attention. The soldier whose wound is minor and who can be returned to the ranks quickly is treated first, so that the strength of the army is restored with the least delay. The soldier whose wound is severe but treatable is set aside for careful attention once the minor cases have been cleared, on the reasoning that his survival depends on time and skill rather than on speed. The soldier whose wound is judged beyond the skill of the attendants to treat, whether from the depth of a spear-thrust to the body or the loss of blood from a severed limb, is made as comfortable as the camp allows and is not given the attention that might save a soldier with a lesser injury. This sorting is harsh in practice and is the subject of dispute among the surgeons themselves, some holding that a soldier should never be abandoned while breath remains in him, others holding that scarce bandages, water, and hands are better spent where they will do the most good. The [Amurra Republic legion](/content/military/amurra-republic-legion), whose camp is built anew each night of the march and whose prefect of the camp is responsible for the ordering of every function within it, assigns a fixed number of attendants to each cohort and trains the centurions to recognise which of their wounded should be carried to the attendants first and which can wait.

The sorting of the wounded is complicated further by the confusion of the field itself. An engagement fought at close quarters, of the kind favoured by the pike formations of the Amurra legion or the long spears of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), tends to produce wounds concentrated in a narrow strip of ground where the lines met, and the attendants can move among the fallen with some order. An engagement of mounted archers, of the kind fought by the Vashari Pact and the cavalry of the Plains Confederation in the great engagements of the Three-Banners War, scatters the wounded across open ground, and the attendants who follow such an army must ride out after the fighting has moved on to gather the injured before exposure or a returning enemy patrol finishes what the arrow began.

## Wound Care and Bandaging on Campaign

The wounds inflicted by the weapons in common use are well understood by the attendants who treat them, even where the causes of infection are not. The broad head of the Stoneheim spear, described among the [Weaponry and Armour](/content/military/weaponry-and-armour) of the known world, makes a wound that tears rather than punctures cleanly and that is accordingly difficult to close, while the narrow thrust of the vaelspatha or the gladius carried by the legionaries of Amurra produces a deep wound with a small opening that is prone to festering beneath the skin if it is closed too quickly. Attendants who serve with armies that fight in close formation learn to distinguish these wounds and to treat the tearing wound by packing and loose binding rather than by an attempt to draw the edges together, on the understanding, arrived at through long observation rather than through any account of how corruption spreads, that a wound left open to drain is less likely to fester than one closed too soon.

Bandaging on campaign is performed with whatever clean cloth the camp can supply, boiled where time and firewood allow, and bound with a firmness intended to slow bleeding without stopping the flow of blood to the limb entirely. Arrow wounds present a particular difficulty, for the head of the arrow frequently detaches from the shaft when the shaft is withdrawn, leaving the head buried in the flesh and requiring the attendant to cut down to it or to work it free with a hooked instrument. The composite bows favoured by the horse archers of the Plains Confederation and the Vashari Pact drive an arrow deep enough that this extraction is a common task after any engagement fought at range, and attendants who accompany armies that face such archery keep a set of extraction tools distinct from those used for blade wounds.

Burns, frostbite, and the wounds of siege warfare require their own treatments. The garrisons that held the watch-towers of [Stonespire Pass](/content/location/natural/feature/stonespire-pass) during the coalition's blockade in the Stoneheim Pass War suffered as much from exposure and frostbite during the weeks of the siege as from the injuries of combat, and the hold chronicles record that the attendants who served the towers treated frostbitten hands and feet with gradual warming, a practice the dwarven surgeons regarded as superior to the sudden application of heat that some lowland attendants preferred and that was more likely to damage the tissue further. Armies that campaign in the cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), including the militia that fought the confederation of Frostwall clans under the chieftain Ulf the Red during the Northern Marches Frontier Wars, carry a proportionally larger stock of dressings for frostbite than armies that fight only in the temperate south.

## Amputation Practice

The removal of a shattered limb is the most drastic treatment available to a field attendant and is undertaken only when the alternative is judged certain to be fatal, as when a limb has been crushed beyond any hope of repair or when a wound has begun to putrefy and threatens to spread corruption through the body. The decision to amputate is not taken lightly, for the operation itself carries a substantial risk of death from blood loss or from the shock of the procedure, and a soldier who survives the amputation still faces a period of vulnerability to infection that many do not survive. Attendants who perform amputations on campaign work quickly, using a sharp blade and, where the resources of the camp allow, a saw for the bone, and they attempt to control the bleeding by binding above the wound before the cut is made and by searing or binding the vessels once the limb is removed. Wine or strong spirits are given where available, both to dull the pain and, though the attendants do not always understand why, because wounds cleaned with spirits appear to fester less often than wounds left untreated.

The Iron Hall Company, the [mercenary company](/content/military/mercenary-companies) founded in the aftermath of the Three-Banners War, is noted for maintaining its own hospitals as a standing feature of its camp, staffed by attendants retained on permanent contract rather than assembled anew for each campaign, and the company's surgeons are consequently more experienced in amputation and in the aftercare of amputees than the improvised attendants of a militia levy raised for a single conflict. A soldier who loses a limb in the service of a well-established company or a standing force such as the Amurra legion has some prospect of a pension or a place in the company's train once he can no longer fight; a soldier who loses a limb serving in a levy raised from freeholders and dismissed at the end of the campaigning season has no such certainty and often returns home to depend upon his family or his village.

## Transporting the Wounded

Once a wounded soldier has been treated as well as the field camp allows, the question of how he is to be moved becomes as important as the treatment itself. An army on the march, such as the Amurra legion with its train of supply wagons and pack animals, can carry its wounded on the wagons that also bear grain and fodder, though the jolting of a wagon over rough ground is itself a hazard to a soldier with a wound that has only begun to close. Armies that fight in open, mounted campaigns, of the kind waged by the Plains Confederation and the Vashari Pact, improvise litters slung between two horses or use travois dragged behind a single mount, methods that are gentler on the wounded than a wagon but slower and more exposed to the risk of a returning enemy patrol. The wounded who cannot be moved at all, whether because the army must retreat quickly or because no transport is available, are left in the care of a small number of attendants who remain behind, a practice that exposes both the wounded and their attendants to considerable danger and that has produced some of the more painful episodes recorded in the chronicles of the frontier wars of the Northern Marches.

Evacuation from a besieged position presents its own difficulties, as the garrisons of the watch-towers at Stonespire Pass discovered during the weeks of blockade in the Stoneheim Pass War, when the wounded of the northern tower could not be moved to safety until the sortie coordinated with the arrival of the relieving levies broke the coalition's lines. In general, the wounded of a besieged garrison must be treated in place until relief arrives or the siege is lifted, and the survival of such wounded depends heavily on the stores of clean water, cloth, and food that the garrison laid in before the investment began.

## The Organisation of Camp Medicine

The quality of the medical provision an army makes for its soldiers depends heavily on how the army itself is organised and funded. The Amurra legion, as a permanent institution supported by the treasury of the Republic, maintains trained attendants as a fixed part of its establishment and equips them with tools and dressings carried in the baggage train alongside the legion's other supplies. The Iron Hall Company, as a long-established mercenary force with a settled headquarters, likewise maintains permanent hospitals and retains experienced surgeons between contracts. Levies raised for a single campaign, such as the mounted forces summoned by the council of the Plains Confederation during the Three-Banners War or the militia called up along the northern frontier of the Marches, rely instead on whatever attendants can be found among the soldiers themselves, on camp followers with some knowledge of wound treatment, and on bonesetters and other practitioners who travel with the army informally rather than as an established establishment. The disparity in provision between a standing force and an improvised levy is one of the clearest advantages that a wealthy, organised realm holds over a confederation of clans or tribes raised for a single conflict, and it is reflected in the survival rates that the chronicles, where they record such figures at all, attribute to the armies of the established realms as against the levies of the frontier.
