## The Herbalist and the Apothecary Trade

The great majority of the population of the known world has no regular access to magical healing, whether because the [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration) practitioners of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) and the other traditions are concentrated in a few forests and courts, or because their services are reserved for those who can pay the price or claim the standing that the practitioners require. For the farmer, the labourer, the sailor, and the townsman, the ordinary treatment of injury and illness rests with the herbalist who gathers the plants of the field and forest and the apothecary who compounds them into remedies. Herbalism is the older of the two occupations, practised in every household to some degree, while the apothecary trade is the commercial and increasingly regulated profession that has grown up around the sale of prepared remedies in the towns and cities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind).

The distinction between the herbalist and the apothecary is one of degree rather than of kind. The herbalist is typically a person of a village or a rural household who has learned from a parent or a neighbour the plants that treat the common complaints of the district, and who gathers and prepares these remedies without formal training or license. The apothecary is a tradesman of the town, trained through an apprenticeship and admitted to a guild, who keeps a shop stocked with dried herbs, oils, tinctures, and compounded preparations, and who sells these goods to the households and the physicians of the city. The apothecary's trade depends on the same plants that the herbalist gathers, and in many towns the apothecary purchases a portion of the stock from the herb-gatherers of the surrounding countryside rather than growing or collecting the material directly.

## Gathering and Preparation of Remedies

The gathering of herbal remedies is governed by the season and the part of the plant that is required, a body of practical knowledge that is transmitted from the experienced gatherer to the apprentice through years of accompanied harvest rather than through written instruction, though the larger apothecary shops of the cities keep ledgers recording the yield and the quality of each season's gathering. The leaves of a plant such as [yarrow](/content/species/flora/yarrow) or [feverfew](/content/species/flora/feverfew) are harvested when the plant is in flower and the medicinal compounds are believed to be most concentrated, while the roots of a plant such as [angelica](/content/species/flora/angelica) are dug in the autumn of the second year of growth, after the plant has stored its reserves below ground. Bark, as with the [willow](/content/species/flora/willow) that supplies the most widely used remedy for pain, is stripped in the spring when the sap rises, and flowers such as the [arnica](/content/species/flora/arnica) of the high pastures of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) are gathered in the brief weeks of their blooming.

Once gathered, the raw material must be dried, ground, or otherwise reduced to a form that will keep. Leaves and flowers are dried in the shade, for direct sun degrades many of the compounds that give a remedy its effect, and the dried material is stored in sealed pots or wrapped bundles that keep out moisture and light. Roots and bark are washed, cut into manageable pieces, and dried more slowly, after which they are ground to a powder as needed rather than in advance, since the powdered form loses its potency faster than the whole root or bark. The apothecary's workshop, whether attached to a shop in [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) or a household in a Morvindi hold, typically includes a drying room or rack, a set of scales for measuring doses, mortars and pestles for grinding, and a store of jars and bottles for the finished preparations. Infusions, made by steeping the dried material in hot water, are the simplest of the preparations and the ones that any household can make; tinctures, made by dissolving the active material in wine or distilled spirit, keep longer and are preferred for remedies such as the poppy resin that must be dosed with precision; and oils, made by steeping fresh material in oil over a period of weeks, are used chiefly for the preparations that are applied to the skin rather than taken internally.

## The Apothecary Shop as a Commercial Institution

The apothecary shop is a fixture of every town of consequence in the known world, typically located near the market square or the harbour where the trade in goods and the movement of travellers bring the custom that the shop depends on. The shop itself is both a retail establishment and a workshop, with a public counter where the customer states the complaint and receives the remedy, and a back room where the drying, grinding, and compounding take place. The apothecary keeps a stock of the common remedies prepared in advance, such as the feverfew infusion for the autumn fevers or the willow bark powder for pain, but many preparations are compounded to order, particularly the more potent or dangerous remedies such as the [poppy](/content/species/flora/poppy) resin, which the apothecary measures and dispenses personally rather than leaving pre-measured on the shelf.

The apothecary's income depends on a combination of retail sale to households, standing arrangements with physicians and healers who prescribe remedies that the patient then purchases from the shop, and supply contracts with institutions such as the [inns and hospices](/content/economy/inns-and-hospices) of the larger cities, which maintain their own gardens of common herbs such as feverfew but rely on the apothecary for preparations that require specialised compounding or ingredients that cannot be grown locally. The apothecaries of the coastal cities of Sahrana also deal in imported aromatics and spices such as [cardamom](/content/species/flora/cardamom), which are used both in cookery and in the spiced tonics and warming preparations that the southern apothecary trade favours, giving the southern shops a closer relationship to the spice trade than their northern counterparts.

## Guild Regulation of the Apothecary Trade

The apothecary trade is organised into [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) in every city where the trade is of any size, following the same pattern of apprentice, journeyman, and master that governs the other trades of the settled peoples. An apprentice apothecary is bound to a master for a term of years, during which the apprentice learns the identification of plants, the methods of preparation, and the dosages of the remedies that the trade requires, a body of knowledge that carries considerably more risk of fatal error than most other trades, since a mistake in the dose of a preparation such as poppy resin can kill the patient rather than merely spoil the goods. The guild's chief function in the apothecary trade is therefore the certification of competence: a guild that admits a master to practice is vouching that the master can be trusted with substances whose margin between remedy and poison is narrow.

The guilds of the apothecaries also regulate the trade in dangerous substances more strictly than they regulate the common herbs. The cultivation of the poppy in the gardens of the [Green Crescent](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/green-crescent) is licensed by the authorities of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) and the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic), and the resulting resin may be sold only by apothecaries who hold the guild's license for its handling, with the fields registered and the yield accounted at every stage from harvest to sale. In the northern regions, the guilds of the apothecaries of the cities that trade in the [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) uplands maintain similar standards for the sale of arnica oil, given the toxicity of the plant if it is taken internally or applied to broken skin, and a master who sells an adulterated or mislabelled preparation risks fine, suspension, or expulsion from the guild. The guild also negotiates, on behalf of its members, the terms under which herbs may be gathered from lands under the stewardship of others, as in the arrangement by which the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) permit the apothecaries of the neighbouring settlements to gather feverfew from the forest margin in quantities limited to local need, while withholding permission for commercial harvesting on a larger scale.

## Regional Traditions

The apothecary trade of the northern regions, centred on the settlements of the [Northern Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches) and the [Valdmark](/content/government/national/valdmark), draws heavily on the plants of the alpine pastures and sheltered valleys of the Morvind uplands, and its remedies are weighted toward the treatment of the chest complaints, joint pains, and injuries of a population of miners, herders, and labourers working in a cold climate. The angelica root imported from the Valdmark valleys and the arnica oil of the high pastures are staples of every northern apothecary's shelf, alongside the willow bark that is gathered along the rivers and traded from the marsh communities of the coast.

The apothecary trade of the Sahrani cities, by contrast, is shaped by the proximity of the [Green Crescent](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/green-crescent) gardens and the caravan trade that brings aromatics and dried plant material from across the interior. The apothecaries of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) deal more heavily in the regulated poppy resin, in imported spices used for warming tonics, and in remedies suited to the illnesses of a warmer climate, and their guilds have developed the most elaborate licensing system for dangerous substances found anywhere in the known world, a consequence of the scale of the poppy cultivation on which the southern trade depends. Between these regional traditions there is a steady exchange of goods and knowledge, carried along the same trade routes that carry cloth, spice, and metal, so that a preparation developed by the apothecaries of one region may become common stock in the shops of another within a generation.

## Overlap and Tension with Magical Healing

The relationship between the apothecary trade and the practitioners of [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration) is one of partial overlap and occasional friction rather than outright rivalry, since the two forms of treatment serve populations that only sometimes coincide. A patient who can reach a practitioner of the Sylvan Court or the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) and can meet whatever price or condition the practitioner sets may prefer the speed and thoroughness of magical healing, particularly for an injury as severe as a shattered bone. For the greater number of patients, who have no such access, the apothecary's remedies are not a second choice but the only choice, and the apothecary trade has developed independently of magical practice for this reason, with its own guilds, its own body of accumulated knowledge, and its own standards of training.

Where the two forms of treatment meet most directly is in the treatment of poisoning and severe illness, where the magical practitioner and the apothecary sometimes work from the same case with different tools. The treatment of a poisoning by a fungus as dangerous as the [death cap](/content/species/flora/death-cap) may combine the willow bark tea that the apothecary prepares for the relief of pain with a magical working intended to bind or neutralise the toxin in the blood, and the healers of both traditions have recorded, in their separate literatures, the outcomes of such combined treatments without always agreeing on which element of the treatment was responsible for the patient's survival. Some practitioners of magical healing regard the apothecary's remedies as a useful adjunct that eases a patient's suffering while the deeper working proceeds; others, particularly among the more traditional lodges, hold that a plant remedy administered alongside a magical working can interfere with the reading of the patient's pattern, and decline to treat a patient who has recently taken a strong preparation such as poppy resin until its effects have passed. The apothecaries, for their part, are generally content to leave the more demanding cases to the practitioners of magic when such practitioners are available, reserving their own trade for the steady business of the common complaints, the injuries of daily labour, and the illnesses of the great majority of the population who will never see a magical healer in the whole course of their lives.
