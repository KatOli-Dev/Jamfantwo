## The Balance of the Four Substances

Humoral theory is the framework by which physicians, herbalists, and the greater part of the ordinary population of the three continents understand the causes of illness and the reasons that particular treatments are chosen over others. The theory holds that the body contains four substances, called humours, whose proportions determine the health of the person who carries them. The four humours are blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile, and each is associated with a pair of qualities drawn from the older reckoning of the elements: blood is warm and moist, phlegm is cold and moist, yellow bile is warm and dry, and black bile is cold and dry. Health, in this account, is not the absence of a single cause of harm but a condition of proportion among the four, and illness is a disturbance of that proportion, whether by excess, by deficiency, or by the corruption of one humour into a form that no longer serves its proper function in the body.

The theory is taught in every apprenticeship of medicine known to the physicians of [Sahran](/content/location/settlement/city/sahran), [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar), and the cities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and it is the common inheritance of physicians, surgeons, and herbalists alike, whatever their disagreements over particular treatments. A physician who diagnoses an illness begins from the assumption that some humour has departed from its proper measure, and the treatments that follow are directed at restoring the measure rather than at any single organ or wound. The theory does not distinguish sharply between disease and injury in its account of recovery, for a wound that fails to heal cleanly is explained, like a fever that will not break, as a disturbance of the humours at the site of the injury.

## The Origins and Standing of the Theory

The theory is old enough that no single author is credited with its founding, and the physicians who teach it treat it as an inheritance refined over generations of observation rather than a doctrine handed down whole. The chronicles of the physicians of the Sahrani coast record generations of commentary and adjustment to the theory, particularly following the [Sahrani Plague](/content/history/the-sahrani-plague), an epidemic whose course could not be fully explained by the ordinary account of humoral imbalance and which prompted physicians to add categories of contagion and bad air to their diagnostic vocabulary without abandoning the humoral framework itself. The commission of physicians established in [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) during the plague kept detailed records of the humoral state each patient was judged to be in upon admission to the hospitals established for their care, and these records remain among the most complete sources for the practice of humoral diagnosis available to later scholars.

The theory is taught as a body of accumulated observation rather than as a settled science, and physicians of different cities and different lineages of training disagree over particular details even while accepting the broad framework. A physician trained in the coastal cities tends to place greater weight on the qualities of moisture and dryness, given the humid climate of the coast and its association with an excess of phlegm among the population, while a physician trained in the drier country of the interior places greater weight on the heat and dryness that produce an excess of yellow bile. These regional variations are a matter of ordinary professional disagreement rather than a fracture in the theory itself, and a physician moving between cities is expected to master the local variant without difficulty.

## Diagnosis by the Reading of Signs

A physician who examines a patient looks first to a set of signs that are held to indicate the state of the humours: the colour and temperature of the skin, the quality of the pulse, the appearance of the tongue, the colour and consistency of the urine, and the patient's own account of appetite, thirst, sleep, and temperament. A flushed and feverish patient with a rapid pulse is judged to suffer an excess of blood or of yellow bile, depending on whether the fever is accompanied by lethargy or by agitation. A patient with pale skin, a slow pulse, and a persistent cough productive of clear or white matter is judged to suffer an excess of phlegm. A patient given to melancholy, poor digestion, and a sallow complexion is judged to suffer an excess of black bile, a humour associated with the autumn season and with the qualities of cold and dryness.

The examination of urine is a particularly developed branch of humoral diagnosis, and physicians of every city keep glass vessels calibrated for the inspection of urine by colour, clarity, sediment, and odour. A physician who examines urine is not merely observing an excretion but reading a report on the state of the humours as they have been processed by the body, and a skilled physician is expected to distinguish dozens of shades and qualities, each associated with a particular imbalance. The pulse is read with comparable care, the physician noting its speed, strength, and rhythm at several points on the wrist and neck, and comparing the findings against the season, the age of the patient, and the time of day, since the humours are held to shift in their proportion according to all three.

Diagnosis by humoral sign is distinguished from diagnosis by cause, and physicians are trained to distinguish an immediate cause, such as a wound, a poison, or exposure to cold, from the underlying humoral disturbance that the cause has produced. A wound is treated first as a wound, by cleaning and closing, but the fever and inflammation that may follow are treated as a humoral disturbance requiring its own remedies, and a physician who treats only the wound while neglecting the fever is regarded as incompletely trained.

## Treatment: Restoring the Balance

The treatments prescribed under humoral theory are directed at reducing an excess, restoring a deficiency, or correcting a corruption of one of the four humours, and the physician's task is to select the treatment appropriate to the diagnosed imbalance. Bloodletting, the deliberate removal of a measured quantity of blood, is prescribed for an excess of blood or for a fever judged to arise from an overheated and overabundant blood, and physicians are trained in the sites of the body from which blood may be drawn for the treatment of particular complaints, a leg vein for a disorder of the lower body and an arm vein for a disorder of the chest or the head. Purging, by which the bowels are induced to empty through prescribed preparations, is the treatment of choice for an excess of black bile or yellow bile, the two humours associated with the processes of digestion, and physicians distinguish gentle purgatives suitable for the frail from stronger preparations reserved for patients of robust constitution.

Dietary regimen is the treatment most commonly prescribed and the one physicians regard as foundational to the maintenance of health even in the absence of illness. Since each humour is associated with a pair of qualities, foods are classified by the same qualities and prescribed or forbidden according to the patient's constitution and season. A patient judged to run cold and moist, with an excess of phlegm, is directed toward foods that are warm and dry in their effect, spiced meats and aged wines rather than fresh fruit and unspiced bread, while a patient with an excess of yellow bile is directed toward cooling and moistening foods and away from strong spices and roasted meats. Physicians extend the same reasoning to the choice of climate, exercise, sleep, and even the emotional life of the patient, holding that anger and excitement warm the body and encourage an excess of yellow bile while sorrow and inactivity cool it and encourage an excess of black bile.

Herbal remedies are chosen by the same classification of qualities, and the herbalists who supply physicians and treat patients directly work from the same framework of warm, cold, moist, and dry properties assigned to each plant. [Feverfew](/content/species/flora/feverfew), classed as cooling and drying, is prescribed for the fevers associated with an excess of blood or yellow bile, while preparations drawn from the [poppy](/content/species/flora/poppy), classed as cooling and profoundly moistening in its effect on the humours, are reserved for the relief of pain and for the quieting of an overheated and agitated constitution. The herbalist's classification of a plant's qualities is not always identical to the physician's, and disputes over whether a given preparation is properly warming or cooling are a recurring feature of the correspondence between the two professions.

## Constitution, Season, and the Course of a Life

Humoral theory holds that every person is born with a natural constitution, a baseline proportion of the four humours that determines temperament as well as physical health, and that the treatment of illness must take this constitution into account rather than aiming at some single ideal balance common to all patients. A person of naturally warm and moist constitution is expected to run a higher fever than a person of naturally cold and dry constitution without alarm, and a physician who does not first establish the patient's baseline constitution is liable to prescribe a treatment suited to a different sort of body altogether. The theory further holds that the proportion of the humours shifts across the seasons of the year and across the stages of a life, infancy being warm and moist, old age cold and dry, and the physician is expected to weigh the patient's age and the season of the year alongside the immediate signs of illness.

## Tension with the Magical Account of the Body

Humoral theory developed and continues to be practised entirely apart from magic, and physicians and herbalists who work within it do not appeal to any practitioner's discipline in their diagnosis or treatment. The tension between the two accounts becomes explicit chiefly at the point where a magical healer and a physician are called to treat the same patient, an occurrence common enough in the larger cities that both professions have developed views on the other's competence. The practice of [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration) as performed by magical traditions addresses the living body directly, by a discipline proper to magic alone, and a magical healer who examines a patient looks past the humours entirely to the condition that discipline is trained to perceive. Physicians who have observed magical healing report that it may resolve a fever or close a wound without any of the purging, bleeding, or dietary correction that humoral treatment would prescribe, and some physicians take this as evidence that the humours are themselves only a partial or provisional account of a deeper reality that magic addresses more directly.

Other physicians resist this conclusion and argue that a magical healing which appears to bypass the humours has in fact restored their balance by a means the physician cannot observe, so that the two accounts describe the same recovery from different vantage points rather than competing explanations of it. This latter position is more comfortable for physicians who wish to preserve the authority of their own training, and it is the position generally taught to apprentices, though private correspondence among senior physicians shows considerably more uncertainty on the point than public teaching admits. Magical practitioners, for their part, generally decline to engage with humoral theory at all, regarding it as a system developed by those without access to their own discipline, though a few practitioners of the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council), whose tradition favours practical results over theoretical purity, have taken an interest in humoral diagnosis as a supplementary means of assessing a patient before treatment is attempted.

The disagreement has practical consequences for patients able to afford a choice of treatment, since the two traditions sometimes recommend contrary courses for the same complaint, a physician urging rest, warmth, and a moistening diet where a magical healer proposes an immediate treatment, or a magical healer declining to treat a chronic complaint that responds slowly to their discipline where a physician proposes a sustained regimen over a season. Wealthy patients in the larger cities sometimes retain both a physician and a magical healer and receive treatment from whichever is judged more suited to the complaint at hand, while the greater part of the population, lacking access to trained magical practitioners in any case, relies upon humoral medicine as practised by physicians, surgeons, and herbalists as the only medicine available to them.
