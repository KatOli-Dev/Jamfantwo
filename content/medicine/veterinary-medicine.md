## The Mundane Care of Working Animals

Most livestock and draught animals in the known world are treated, when they sicken or are injured, by the people who work alongside them rather than by any trained physician or magical practitioner. The [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration) worked by the orders of magic, including the animal healing practised by the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) upon the horses and camels of the merchant caravans and by the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) upon the birds and creatures of the coast, reaches only a small fraction of the working animals of the known world. The ordinary farmer, herder, and carter cannot summon a magical healer for a lamed plough ox or a sick milking goat, and the animal is instead treated by the farrier, the herdsman, or the drover using methods that have been developed and passed down through generations of practical experience rather than through the reading and reshaping of pattern.

This mundane veterinary practice is not a single discipline with a common body of doctrine, as the healing traditions of magic are, but a scattered collection of trades and household knowledge that varies by region, by animal, and by the wealth of the owner. A wealthy horse breeder of the Great Grass Plains may retain a farrier who does nothing else but tend the feet and general health of horses, while a smallholder with a single ox and a handful of goats relies upon knowledge handed down within the family and upon the occasional visit of a travelling animal doctor who serves an entire district.

## The Farrier and the Horse

The farrier is the tradesman most consistently associated with the mundane treatment of animals, for the shoeing of horses requires a skill that most owners do not possess and that has therefore become a distinct trade in most towns of any size. The farrier trims and shoes the hooves of the [horse](/content/species/fauna/horse), a task that must be repeated every few weeks as the hoof grows, and in the course of this regular attention the farrier is generally the first to notice lameness, cracked hooves, and the early signs of disease in the feet and legs. Farriers in the towns of Aethelar and the horse-breeding regions of the Great Grass Plains have therefore extended their trade beyond the shoeing of hooves into the broader treatment of lameness, strains, and the common ailments of horses, and a skilled farrier is often the closest thing to a horse doctor that an ordinary owner can obtain.

The farrier's methods rely on close observation of the animal's gait, the condition of the hoof wall, and the swelling or heat in a leg, together with a repertoire of poultices, bandaging, and rest that has proven effective over long practice rather than through any formal theory of disease. A horse with a strained tendon is rested and its leg is wrapped and cooled; a hoof abscess is opened and drained; a horse that has gone lame from a stone bruise is shod with a leather pad to protect the sole. These treatments are learned through apprenticeship to an established farrier rather than through study, and the quality of care available to a horse therefore depends heavily on the skill of the particular farrier the owner can reach, which in remote districts may mean no farrier at all and only the owner's own limited knowledge.

## Oxen, Cattle, and the Herdsman's Knowledge

[Oxen](/content/species/fauna/oxen), the principal draught animals of the agricultural regions of Aethelar and Morvind, receive a different quality of care than the horse, for they are generally kept by farmers of modest means who cannot afford a specialist and must instead rely on their own accumulated experience and that of their neighbours. The herdsman who has spent decades among cattle learns to recognise the signs of foot rot, of bloat from rich pasture, and of the various fevers and infections that afflict a herd, and treats these conditions with remedies that are common knowledge within farming communities rather than the property of any trade. A bloated ox may be walked to relieve the pressure in its gut or, in severe cases, pierced with a hollow tube to release the trapped gas, a crude but often effective remedy that requires no training beyond what is passed from an experienced farmer to an apprentice or a son.

The herding communities of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), whose economy depends heavily on the movement of large herds of [cattle](/content/species/fauna/cattle) along the drove roads described in the account of [livestock and herding](/content/economy/livestock-and-herding), have developed a body of practical knowledge concerning the diseases that spread through a herd on the trail, including the recognition of animals too weak to continue the drive and the isolation of beasts showing signs of contagious illness. The drovers who accompany these herds are not physicians in any formal sense, but the successful completion of a drive of several weeks' duration depends on their ability to recognise and manage illness and injury without any recourse to a trained healer, since no farrier or animal doctor accompanies the herd on the trail itself.

## Camels and the Caravans of the Kharesh

The [camel](/content/species/fauna/camel) that carries the caravans of the Kharesh desert is tended by drovers and camel handlers whose knowledge of the animal's ailments is essential to the survival of a caravan crossing that may last for weeks without access to any settlement. The salawa handlers who accompany the caravans described in the account of [livestock and herding](/content/economy/livestock-and-herding) are skilled in recognising the early signs of exhaustion, dehydration, and the sores caused by ill-fitting harness or overloading, conditions that if left untreated can kill a camel and strand a caravan far from water. The treatment available on the trail is limited to rest, the adjustment of loads, and the application of salves to sores and cracked skin, for the caravan cannot carry a magical healer of the kind that the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) maintains in the cities, and even a caravan wealthy enough to retain such a healer for the journey would find few practitioners willing to endure the crossing.

The camel handlers of the Kharesh have also developed methods for treating the parasites and skin conditions common to the animal in desert conditions, using preparations of oils and minerals gathered locally rather than any formal apothecary's stock. This knowledge, like that of the farrier and the herdsman, is transmitted through apprenticeship within families and trading communities rather than through any school or guild, and its quality varies considerably from one caravan company to another.

## Sheep, Goats, and the Smallholder's Remedies

The keeper of [sheep](/content/species/fauna/sheep) and [goats](/content/species/fauna/goats) in the upland and desert-margin regions generally has the least access to any specialised animal treatment, for these animals are typically kept in smaller numbers by farmers and herders of modest means who cannot support a dedicated tradesman. The shepherd of the Aethelaran highlands treats foot rot, a common ailment of sheep kept on wet ground, by trimming the affected hoof and applying preparations of tar or other astringent substances, a remedy known throughout the wool-producing regions and taught within shepherding families rather than through any formal instruction. The goatherd of the Kharesh margins and the mountain regions of Morvind similarly relies on inherited knowledge to treat the parasites, skin conditions, and occasional injuries suffered by a herd that ranges over difficult terrain.

The scale of sheep and goat keeping, in which a single herder may be responsible for hundreds of animals spread across open pasture, makes individual treatment of every sick or injured animal impractical in any case. The herder's attention in such circumstances is directed chiefly toward preventing the spread of contagious illness through the culling or isolation of affected animals, a practice that resembles the drovers' management of cattle on the trail more than it resembles the individual care that a farrier might give to a single valued horse.

## The Absence of Formal Instruction

None of these mundane veterinary practices are taught through any institution comparable to the training that a physician who treats persons might receive, nor is there any guild in most regions that certifies or regulates the treatment of animals as distinct from the trades, such as farriery, that happen to include such treatment among their functions. The knowledge is instead transmitted through apprenticeship, through the oral tradition of farming and herding communities, and through the practical experience that any person who works closely with animals over a long career accumulates. This absence of formal instruction means that the quality of mundane animal care varies enormously between regions and between individual practitioners, in marked contrast to the more consistent, if narrower, standard of care that the animal healing of the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) and the [Velden Compact](/content/government/local/velden-compact) provides to the animals fortunate enough to come within their reach.

The overwhelming majority of the working and farm animals of the known world never receive the attention of any magical healer in the whole course of their lives, whether because their owners cannot afford such attention, because no practitioner is available within reach of the farm or the drove road, or because the animal's owner does not consider the expense justified for a beast whose economic value is modest. For these animals, the mundane treatment offered by the farrier, the herdsman, and the drover, uneven and unsystematic as it may be, represents the whole of the medical attention they will ever receive.
