## Institutions for the Sick Without Recourse to Magic

The greater part of the population of the known world has no regular access to the healing arts of the magical traditions, whether because no practitioner resides within reach or because the cost of a working exceeds what an ordinary household can pay. For these people, the institutions that matter are the infirmary, the almshouse, and the sickhouse maintained by a temple or a religious order — places where the sick and the destitute are sheltered, fed, and tended by hand rather than by working. These institutions vary considerably from one region of the known world to another, reflecting differences in governance, in religious practice, and in the wealth of the cities that support them, but they share a common purpose: the care of those who cannot care for themselves and who have no other recourse.

The terms are not always used consistently across the continents. An almshouse, in the usage of the eastern coast of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), is a permanent shelter for the aged, the destitute, and the chronically infirm, distinct from an infirmary, which admits the acutely sick for a period of days or weeks and discharges them upon recovery or death. In the cities of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast the distinction is less sharply drawn, and a single foundation may serve both functions under one roof. The sickhouses maintained by religious orders occupy a position between the two, offering shelter to travellers and the local poor alike without the more formal admission and discharge that the civic infirmaries observe.

## Crown Patronage on the Eastern Coast

In [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth), the almshouses of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) and the lesser port towns are founded and maintained chiefly through crown patronage, a pattern that reflects the concentration of authority in the Aldreth crown more broadly. The king grants a charter and an endowment of land or rents to an almshouse, appoints a warden to administer it, and reserves the right to inspect its accounts through the royal officials who oversee the local councils. The almshouses of Eldreth admit the aged poor of the city on a fixed number of places, determined by the endowment's income, and a waiting list is kept by the warden for places that fall vacant through death. The crown's motive in this patronage is acknowledged by the chroniclers of the realm to be as much a matter of public order as of charity, for a city with no provision for its destitute is held to invite disorder among the poor, and the king's almshouses are represented in official records as an instrument of the peace of the realm no less than of mercy.

The rival kingdom of [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) follows a different pattern, in which the great merchant families endow almshouses individually, each bearing the founder's name and administered by trustees drawn from the founder's descendants. The Vael almshouses compete with one another for reputation, and a family that maintains its foundation generously is honoured accordingly in the city's opinion, while a family whose almshouse falls into neglect suffers a corresponding loss of standing. This competitive endowment has produced a larger number of smaller institutions in the cities of Vael than the single large crown foundations of Aldreth, and the quality of care varies more widely from one Vael almshouse to another than it does among the almshouses of the Aldreth crown.

## Temple and Guild Funding on the Sahrani Coast

The infirmaries of the Sahrani coastal cities are funded through an arrangement of temple endowment and guild contribution that has no close parallel on the eastern coast of Aethelar. The [Temple of the Sea Mother](/content/religion/temple-of-the-sea-mother) in [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) distributes a portion of the offerings of the first catch to the infirmary that stands adjacent to the temple precinct, and the guild of the fishing fleets contributes a fixed share of each season's catch toward the maintenance of the same institution, in recognition that the sailors and porters who fall sick or are injured in the service of the fleet are the infirmary's principal charges. This dual funding, from religious offering and guild levy, is characteristic of the coastal cities generally and reflects the close relationship between the maritime trades and the temple that watches over them.

The infirmaries of [Qassir](/content/location/settlement/city/qassir) draw their support from a wider circle of guilds beyond the fishing trade, including the porters, the caravan drivers, and the salt-workers, each of which maintains a modest levy paid into a common fund administered by a council of guild elders rather than by priests. This guild-administered model gives the Qassir infirmaries a more secular character than those of Khalisar, though the physicians and attendants who staff them are drawn from the same schools of training as their counterparts elsewhere on the coast.

## Staffing: Physicians, Attendants, and Novices

The staffing of an infirmary or almshouse reflects the same hierarchy of practical medical training found among physicians more broadly, though the great majority of the daily labour in these institutions is performed by attendants who have not undergone the extended training of a physician. A senior physician typically visits an infirmary on a fixed schedule, examining the newly admitted, prescribing treatment, and reviewing the more difficult cases, while the daily nursing — the changing of dressings, the administration of prescribed remedies, the feeding of patients too weak to feed themselves, and the washing of the sick — is carried out by attendants who have learned their duties through apprenticeship within the institution itself rather than through formal instruction. Many of these attendants are drawn from the ranks of novices attached to whichever temple or order maintains the foundation, and in the almshouses of Aldreth the warden frequently employs widows of the parish, whose labour is compensated with lodging and a small wage in addition to their keep.

The [hearth-halls](/content/religion/hearth-halls) of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame), scattered through the river valleys of Aethelar and the eastern Sahrani coast, occupy a position distinct from the formal infirmaries in that they extend shelter to the sick and the destitute as an extension of the broader duty of hospitality the faith imposes upon its hearth-keepers, rather than as a specialised medical function. A traveller taken ill on the road, or a labourer with nowhere else to turn, may find shelter and the ordinary comforts of food and warmth at a hearth-hall, though the hearth-keepers do not claim the specialised knowledge of a trained physician and will refer serious cases to an infirmary or a physician's household where one is within reach. This informal capacity has made the hearth-halls an important supplement to the formal infirmaries in the smaller river towns, where no dedicated sickhouse exists.

## Quarantine Practice After the Two Great Pestilences

The institutions described here owe much of their present form to the public health measures adopted during the two great pestilences of the age, the [Stone Plague](/content/history/stone-plague) that swept the three continents in the generations following the Long Peace, and the [Sahrani Plague](/content/history/the-sahrani-plague) that struck the coastal cities of Sahrana in the middle period of the present age. The commission of physicians and magistrates that the council of Khalisar established during the Sahrani Plague built a system of hospitals for the isolation and care of the sick that became, in modified form, the ancestor of the infirmaries that stand in the city today; the quarantine stations established at the principal ports during that epidemic likewise remain in occasional use whenever a vessel arrives bearing the signs of contagious illness.

The commissioners of public health that the magistrates of the principal cities appointed during the Stone Plague performed a comparable function on the northern continent, overseeing the separation of the sick from the well and the disposal of the dead in every district of the affected cities. Though the office of commissioner did not survive as a standing institution in most cities once the plague had passed, the practice of separating the sick into a dedicated ward or building, rather than tending them within the general population of an almshouse, is traced by the physicians of the present age to the measures the commissioners devised. An infirmary that admits a patient suspected of a contagious sickness will, in most of the coastal cities and in the larger towns of Aethelar, house that patient apart from the other inmates for a period of observation before admitting them to the general ward, a precaution the wardens and physicians alike attribute to the hard lessons of the two pestilences.

## Charitable Motive and Practical Necessity

The almshouses and infirmaries of the known world are sustained by a combination of religious charity, guild self-interest, and civic order that varies in its proportions from one city to the next but that nowhere rests on a single motive alone. The temples that endow infirmaries do so from a genuine conviction that the care of the sick is a religious duty, but the guilds that contribute to the same foundations do so chiefly because the sailors, porters, and labourers who fall ill are members of their own trade, and the crown that patronises an almshouse does so with an eye to the peace of the city as well as to the welfare of the poor. This convergence of motives has produced an uneven but durable network of institutions across the settled regions of the known world, one that stands apart from the healing practised by the magical traditions and serves the great majority of the population who have no other recourse when illness or injury strikes.
