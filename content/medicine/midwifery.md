## The Attendant at Birth

Childbirth across the known world is managed, in the overwhelming majority of households, without recourse to the practitioners of magic described in the account of [healing and restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration). The orders and courts that maintain that discipline are few, their practitioners fewer still, and their attention is directed toward the injuries of war, the diseases of the great households, and the maintenance of the forests and waters under their care. The ordinary household, whether of the farming valleys, the port cities, the desert camps, or the mountain halls, relies instead on the midwife, a woman of the community trained in the practical management of labour and delivery. The midwife is not a physician in the formal sense that the guild towns of the coast are beginning to recognise, nor is she a surgeon; her competence is confined to the single, recurring event of birth, but within that competence she is the most experienced person that most households will ever call upon.

The social standing of the midwife varies with the wealth and organisation of the community that employs her, but certain features recur widely. She is almost always a woman who has herself borne children, for the customary law of most regions holds that a woman who has not experienced labour cannot properly judge its progress in another. She is typically older, past the years in which she would be bearing children of her own, and she is paid in a mixture of coin, produce, and standing obligation rather than a fixed wage, since a birth may occupy a single evening or several days and no household enters the arrangement knowing which it will be. She is called upon not only at the birth itself but in the weeks before it, to advise the mother on diet and rest, and in the weeks after, to check the healing of the mother and the thriving of the infant.

## Training and Succession

The transmission of midwifery knowledge follows patterns that mirror the broader systems of education described in the account of [childhood and education](/content/culture/childhood-and-education), though the craft itself is rarely taught to children. In the villages of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) river valley, midwifery passes most commonly from mother to daughter within a small number of households in each parish, the daughter attending her mother at successive births from adolescence and taking over the mother's clientele only after a decade or more of assisting. The parish council recognises no formal licence for this practice, but a woman's reputation is established by the record of the births she has attended, and a midwife whose deliveries have gone badly will find the households of the parish turning to a rival or sending to the neighbouring village. Some parishes maintain no dedicated midwife at all, and childbirth there is managed by whichever older woman of the household or the neighbouring households has the most experience, a practice the priests of the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) tolerate without formally sanctioning.

In [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) the trade is organised more formally, after the pattern of the crafts described in the account of life in that city. The midwives of the Lower City and the River Ward have formed an association that is not chartered as a guild in the manner of the shipwrights or the smiths but that nonetheless maintains a roll of recognised practitioners, examines candidates before admitting them to the roll, and arbitrates disputes between a midwife and a dissatisfied household. Entry to the roll requires a period of apprenticeship of three to five years under an established midwife, shorter than the seven to ten years required of the chartered crafts, on the reasoning that the essential skills of the trade can be observed and practised more quickly than those of the shipwright or the weaver, though the association's senior members privately concede that judgment in the more difficult cases takes considerably longer to acquire than the roll's minimum term allows. The poor house maintained by the temple of the Still Flame in the Upper City employs two midwives on a standing basis to attend women who cannot pay, and their services are counted among the charitable functions that the temple provides to the labouring population of the city.

In the courtyard households of the [Sahrani city-states](/content/culture/sahrani-city-life), the attendance of birth is a function that typically falls to the senior women of the extended household, most commonly a grandmother or great-aunt who has attended many deliveries within the family and its close connections over the decades, though the wealthier merchant households of Ashaar and Vereth increasingly retain a midwife who serves several households on a standing fee, in a manner resembling the retained physicians and scribes that the merchant families employ for other purposes. The Sahrani midwife's standing is bound up with the reputation of the households she serves, and a midwife who has attended the births of several prominent merchant families is sought out by others seeking to establish the same connection.

## Desert and Plains Practice

Among the [Kharesh](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) and the [Vashari](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the attendance of childbirth commonly falls within the broader competence of the tribal shaman described in the account of desert life, though the shaman's direct involvement is usually limited to the more difficult labours and to the spiritual observances that accompany the birth; the routine management of an uncomplicated delivery is left to the senior women of the camp, who possess through repeated experience a working knowledge of the stages of labour and the signs of an approaching birth. Because the camp is mobile and a woman in the final weeks of pregnancy travels with difficulty, camps commonly delay the movement of a household whose birth is imminent, and the customary law of the desert obliges neighbouring camps to render assistance to a household whose delivery has begun unexpectedly during a journey between wells.

The peoples of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) manage childbirth within the mobile household described in the account of grassland nomad life, attended by the older women of the tribe in a manner similar to the desert camps. Among the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs), whose foals are capable of independent movement within hours of birth, the management of delivery falls to the herd's most experienced mares, and the practice differs in particulars from that applied to the human herders of the plains, though both traditions share the general expectation that birth is a matter for the women and the female elders of the community rather than for the tribe's shaman, who is called upon only when the labour has gone seriously wrong.

## The Dwarven Halls and the Northern Coast

The halls of [Stoneheim](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and [Karhold](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) manage childbirth within the lineage structures described in the account of dwarven hall life. Because the dwarven lineage extends across several households, a dwarven woman in labour is commonly attended by the senior women of the entire lineage rather than of her immediate household alone, and the largest halls maintain a chamber near the hall of feasts set aside for deliveries, staffed by lineage members recognised for their experience in the craft. The naming of the dwarven child, conducted at the binding ceremony described in the account of [naming traditions](/content/culture/naming-traditions), follows the birth by a period of days sufficient to confirm the infant's survival of the first, most dangerous interval of its life.

On the northern coast, within the longhouse households described in the account of coastal life in [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven), childbirth is attended by the senior women of the household and, in the larger settlements, by a woman recognised by the port council as competent in the craft, though no formal roll or association exists comparable to that of Aldreth. The isolation of the smaller settlements, particularly during the winter confinement, means that many northern households have no choice but to rely on whatever experience is available within the longhouse itself, and the [Nordhaven Sea Code](/content/law/nordhaven-sea-code) makes no specific provision for the practice, treating it as a domestic matter outside the code's concern with the sea and the harbour.

## Complications and Their Management

The midwife's practical repertoire, across every region, centres on positioning the labouring woman, judging the progress of labour by the intervals and strength of contractions, and managing the delivery of the afterbirth. Breech presentation, prolonged labour, and haemorrhage following delivery are the complications most consistently recorded across the traditions, and the responses to them vary in sophistication rather than in kind. A midwife confronted with a breech presentation will typically attempt to turn the infant by manipulation of the mother's abdomen before labour is far advanced, a technique practised with local variation from the Vael valley to the Sahrani coast. Prolonged labour is managed through changes of position, the administration of preparations known to the local herbal tradition, and, in the households that can call upon one, consultation with a bonesetter or physician when the midwife's own methods have failed to bring the labour to a conclusion. Haemorrhage is the complication most feared by midwives of every tradition, and the mortality that follows a severe haemorrhage remains high in every region for which records survive, a fact that the priests of the Still Flame and the shamans of the desert and plains alike acknowledge in the observances that accompany a difficult birth.

The midwife's relationship to the wider practice of medicine is one of overlap rather than subordination. She is rarely trained in the theory that guides the physicians of the guild towns, and she does not ordinarily perform the surgery that a difficult birth sometimes requires, referring such cases, where a suitable practitioner exists nearby, to those with the relevant competence. Yet in the great majority of households across the known world, the midwife remains the only attendant that childbirth will ever see, and the survival of mother and infant alike depends, in the overwhelming number of cases every year, on her judgment and her hands rather than on any other resource that the community can bring to bear.
