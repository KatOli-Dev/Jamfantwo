## A Craft Distinct from the Reshaping of Pattern

Surgery, as practised in the known world outside the traditions of magic, is the treatment of injury and disease through direct manual intervention on the body: cutting, cauterising, setting, stitching, and removing. It is a craft learned by apprenticeship, exercised with the hands and with instruments of steel, and bounded by the physical limits of the practitioner rather than by the [limits](/content/magic/law/limit) that govern the working of pattern. This distinguishes it sharply from the practice described in [Healing and Restoration](/content/magic/healing-and-restoration), in which a trained reader of pattern attunes to a living subject and removes the obstacles that prevent a natural configuration from returning. The surgeon does not read pattern and does not attune to the patient in the sense that a practitioner of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) or the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) would understand the term. The surgeon works on the body as it is presented, with the senses and the instruments available to any craftsman, and the great majority of the population of the known world, lacking access to a magical practitioner or the means to pay for one, depends entirely on this mundane craft for the treatment of wounds, growths, and the complications of childbirth and disease that fall within a surgeon's competence.

The distinction between the two practices is understood plainly by both patients and practitioners. A household in [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) that can afford the fee of a practitioner of the [Khalisar Council](/content/government/local/khalisar-council) will often prefer the magical treatment for a serious injury, since it carries a lower risk of the complications that attend a mundane operation. But such practitioners are rare outside the cities where the traditions maintain a presence, and even within those cities the cost places magical healing beyond the reach of most labourers, farmers, and soldiers. The mundane surgeon, by contrast, is a fixture of every town of any size and of every army that takes the field, and the craft has developed its own body of technique, its own tools, and its own understanding of the risks it cannot fully master.

## Barber-Surgeons and Battlefield Surgeons

Two broad classes of surgeon are recognised across the realms of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), though the terms and the precise division of labour vary from one realm to another. The barber-surgeon is the surgeon of settled life, a tradesman who combines the cutting of hair and the shaving of beards with the lancing of boils, the drawing of teeth, the letting of blood, and the setting of simple fractures. In cities such as [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and [Vaelmouth](/content/location/settlement/city/vaelmouth), the barber-surgeons are organised into a guild distinct from the university-trained physicians who diagnose illness through the study of humoral balance but who traditionally decline to perform manual operations themselves, regarding cutting as beneath the dignity of their calling. The barber-surgeon's guild sets its own standards of practice, examines candidates before admitting them to mastery, and maintains a hall where disputes among members are settled, much as the guilds described in the general study of [guilds and trades](/content/economy/guilds) regulate other crafts under the terms of their [charters](/content/law/guild-charters).

The battlefield surgeon is a different figure, attached to an army rather than to a town, and trained by necessity in a narrower but more urgent set of skills: the stanching of bleeding, the amputation of a shattered limb, and the extraction of arrowheads and other foreign matter from a wound, all performed under time pressure and often within sound of continuing combat. The Iron Hall Company, among the [mercenary companies](/content/military/mercenary-companies) of Aethelar, is noted for maintaining its own permanent surgeons and hospital tents as part of its standing establishment, a provision that other companies and standing forces do not always match. Armies raised by levy, such as those called up by the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) or the clans of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), more commonly rely on surgeons who travel with the baggage train under contract for the duration of a single campaign, and the quality of care available to a common soldier depends heavily on whether his commander has thought to engage such a surgeon at all. Battlefield surgeons in the armies of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) hold a formal rank within the legion's structure and are assigned to units rather than to individual commanders, a system that the Republic regards as more reliable than the private arrangements common elsewhere.

## Common Procedures

The operations performed by a mundane surgeon are limited to those that can be accomplished with a blade, a needle, and manual force, without any capacity to reshape the tissue beneath the skin. Amputation is the most drastic and the most frequently required of these procedures, undertaken when a limb has been crushed beyond repair or when a wound has begun to mortify and threatens the life of the patient if the corrupted tissue is not removed. The surgeon works quickly, using a sharp blade to sever the soft tissue and a saw for the bone, and the speed of the operation is itself a form of mercy, since no reliable means exists to render the patient insensible for its full duration. Trepanation, the opening of the skull to relieve pressure from a blow to the head, is practised by surgeons trained to a higher standard and is regarded as one of the more hazardous operations attempted, given the difficulty of judging the depth of the cut. The removal of bladder stones, known in the trade as the operation for the stone, is performed by a small number of surgeons who specialise in it, since the procedure carries a substantial risk of injury to surrounding organs. Less drastic procedures make up the greater part of a surgeon's ordinary practice: the drawing of decayed teeth, the lancing of abscesses, the closure of wounds with needle and thread, the letting of blood as a treatment believed to restore humoral balance, and the application of cautery, whether by hot iron or by caustic paste, to seal a wound and check bleeding. Bonesetters, who specialise in the reduction and splinting of fractures and dislocations, are frequently barber-surgeons who have developed a particular reputation for that branch of the work, though in some towns the setting of bones is left to practitioners who do not otherwise cut or bleed their patients.

## Tools and Instruments

The surgeon's instruments are forged by smiths who specialise in fine work, and a master surgeon's kit represents a substantial investment maintained and passed down within a household or a guild. The kit typically includes a set of knives of varying size and curvature for incision, a bone saw with a replaceable blade, forceps and probes for the extraction of foreign objects and arrowheads, needles and thread of waxed linen or silk for closing wounds, and cautery irons that are heated in a brazier before use. Surgeons who travel with an army carry a reduced kit suited to speed and portability, favouring instruments that can be cleaned and reused quickly between patients. The dwarven surgeons of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) are noted for instruments of unusually fine tolerance, produced by the same smiths who supply the Holds' renowned metalwork, though the dwarven surgical tradition itself does not differ substantially in method from that practised among humans.

## Infection and Pain

The mundane surgeon has no reliable means to prevent the infection that follows many operations, and a wound that closes cleanly at first may still mortify in the days afterward. Cautery is valued as much for its supposed cleansing effect as for its ability to stop bleeding, and wine or vinegar is commonly applied to wounds in the belief that it discourages corruption, though practitioners differ on how effective this is in practice. Cleanliness of the surgeon's hands and instruments is understood as a matter of professional care rather than as a matter of germ theory, and the standards observed vary considerably between a careful master and a hurried battlefield surgeon working through a queue of wounded men. Pain during an operation is managed as well as circumstances allow but is rarely eliminated. Strong drink is given before a major procedure where it is available, and preparations of poppy are used by surgeons who can obtain them, though the dosage is judged by experience rather than by any precise measure and the risk of giving too much is well understood among practitioners who have lost a patient to the remedy meant to ease their suffering. Restraint by assistants, speed of execution, and the patient's own fortitude remain the principal tools against pain for the surgeon who lacks access to the poppy or to a magical alternative.

## Training and Apprenticeship

A surgeon is trained through apprenticeship in the manner common to the guilds, beginning as a boy bound to a master for a term of years during which he assists at operations, learns to maintain the instruments, and gradually takes on simple procedures such as bloodletting and tooth-drawing under supervision before being permitted to attempt an amputation or a trepanation on his own. Advancement to master status requires the approval of the guild, typically demonstrated through an examination before senior members and, in some cities, the successful completion of a specified operation observed by the examiners. Battlefield surgeons often follow a less formal path, having learned their trade through direct experience in successive campaigns rather than through a guild apprenticeship, and a surgeon who has served through several wars is valued for practical skill even where he lacks the formal credentials that a town guild would require. Some surgeons pursue further study of the humoral theory taught to physicians, seeking to combine an understanding of the body's internal balance with their manual craft, though the two traditions remain institutionally separate in most cities.

## Risk and Mortality

Surgery in the known world carries substantial risk under the best of circumstances, and the outcome of a given operation depends heavily on the skill of the surgeon, the speed with which the patient is treated, and factors that no surgeon can control. Amputation performed promptly and by an experienced hand carries a meaningfully better chance of survival than one delayed until mortification has set in, but even a well-executed amputation exposes the patient to the risk of bleeding, shock, and the infection that follows in the days after the wound is closed. The operation for the stone and trepanation are understood by surgeons themselves to carry the highest risk of the procedures commonly attempted, and a surgeon who has performed either successfully several times gains a reputation that travels well beyond his own town. Soldiers wounded in battle face particularly poor odds, since the delay between injury and treatment, the frequently unsanitary conditions of a field hospital, and the sheer volume of wounded requiring attention after a major engagement all work against recovery. The surgeons of the known world are candid, among themselves and often with their patients, that a mundane operation is a last resort undertaken because no better alternative is available, and that the choice to operate is frequently a choice between a serious risk and a certain decline.
