## Origins

Dwarven tradition traces the origin of the species to the mountains themselves. The oldest dwarven songs speak of ancestors who emerged from the deep places of the world, shaped by stone and fire, and who thereafter made their homes in the heights and hollows of the great ranges. These songs are ancient, passed from one generation to the next with a fidelity that dwarven memory demands, and they form the foundation of dwarven identity across every continent where the people have settled.

Scholars of other species have noted that the dwarven origin tradition, unlike those of [elves](/content/elves) or [humans](/content/humans), is remarkably consistent across widely separated communities. Dwarven settlements in Morvind and Aethelar preserve versions of the same songs with only minor variation, suggesting either a common origin or a shared cultural memory of unusual strength. Whether this reflects a historical migration from a single ancestral home or the parallel development of similar traditions in separate mountain ranges is a question that remains open.

## Characteristics

Dwarves are a short and broadly built people, standing several inches below the average human but comparable in weight owing to their compact and muscular frames. They are long-lived, though not so long-lived as elves, with a typical lifespan of one hundred and fifty to two hundred years. Their senses are sharp, particularly in darkness, and their eyes adapt quickly to the low light of underground halls and mine workings. Dwarven hands are noted for their strength and precision, qualities that serve them well in their renowned crafts.

The dwarven temperament is often described by other peoples as reserved or stubborn, and there is some truth in both characterisations. Dwarves are slow to offer friendship but steadfast once it is given, and they regard commitments and promises as binding beyond mere convenience. This earnestness can make dwarves seem dour to those who do not know them well, but among their own kin and acknowledged friends, dwarves are generous, merry, and fond of song, story, and good fellowship. The contrast between the public reserve and the private warmth of dwarven life is one that surprises many a visitor to a dwarven hall.

## Distribution and Settlement

The greatest concentration of dwarven population is found in the mountain ranges of Morvind, where vast underground halls have been carved from the living rock over centuries of patient labour. These halls are the ancestral heart of dwarven civilisation, and their architecture, engineering, and sheer scale are regarded as among the wonders of the known world. The Morvindian halls house tens of thousands of dwarves in some cases, with districts organised by craft and lineage, and their forges and workshops are the source of the finest metalwork in the world.

In Aethelar, dwarven communities are established in the interior highlands, where the mountain spine of the continent provides suitable ground for their particular style of settlement. These Aethelaran halls are smaller than those of Morvind but no less accomplished, and they serve as important centres of trade between the eastern and western coasts. The [geography](/content/geography) of the mountain ranges has determined where dwarven settlement could take root, and the dwarves have in turn shaped those ranges through centuries of careful excavation and construction.

## Society and Governance

Dwarven society is organised around clans, each tracing its lineage to a known ancestor and maintaining its own traditions, craft specialisations, and internal customs. A clan is both a family and a guild, and membership carries both privileges and obligations. Clans are governed by hereditary elders who hold authority over matters of lineage, marriage, and inheritance, while matters of craft and trade are typically decided by councils of masters within each specialisation.

At the settlement level, clans co-operate through councils of elders that govern the hall as a whole. These councils manage the shared infrastructure of the settlement, the allocation of mining and building rights, and relations with other communities. Dwarven governance is deliberate and conservative, with changes introduced slowly and only after broad agreement among the affected clans. This can make dwarven institutions slow to adapt, but it also gives them a stability that has carried dwarven halls through crises that would have overwhelmed a less cohesive society. The continuity of dwarven governance is one of its proudest features, and a hall governed well for ten generations is considered a measure of success.

## Relations with Other Peoples

Dwarves are among the most active trading peoples in the known world, and their networks extend to every continent. Dwarven metalwork, stonework, and engineered goods are prized everywhere, and in exchange dwarves import foodstuffs, textiles, and materials that the mountains cannot provide. This trade brings dwarves into regular contact with humans and gnomes, and the relationships built through commerce are generally stable and mutually beneficial. Dwarven merchants are known for their fairness and their insistence on clear terms, and other peoples have largely come to appreciate this directness.

Relations with elves have historically been more reserved. The two peoples differ in temperament, in preferred environment, and in the pace at which they conduct their affairs, and these differences have produced a certain distance between them. Yet the two species have also found common ground in scholarship, in craft, and in the defence of shared interests, and the relationship is better characterised as cautious than as hostile. Dwarves and humans co-operate widely, particularly in the cities of Aethelar where mixed communities are common, and the combination of dwarven craft and human enterprise has been a productive one for both peoples.
