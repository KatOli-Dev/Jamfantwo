## Overview

The Northern Coast Bell Codes are the system of bell signals used by the fishing and trading communities of the northern coast of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) to communicate between the boats that work the coastal waters and the towns that line the shore. They are not a language in the ordinary sense, for they have no grammar and no vocabulary of the kind that the surface peoples recognise as speech. They are instead a code of bell strokes, struck upon the bells that the boats and the town towers carry, that conveys information across the distances that separate the fishing grounds from the harbour and the harbour from the open water. The system is used by the fishermen, the harbormasters, and the merchants of the [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast), and its signals are understood by every coastal community of the northern shore from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait.

## The Nature of the Bells

The bells that the system employs are of two principal kinds, distinguished one from another by their size and by the place where they are mounted. The boat bells are small bells of bronze or of polished iron, mounted upon a wooden frame at the bow or the stern of the vessel, and the boat bells are sounded by the striking of a customary clapper that the crew carries for the purpose. The town bells are large bells of cast bronze, mounted in the towers that the coastal towns maintain for the purpose, and the town bells are sounded by the ringing mechanism that the tower keeper operates. The two kinds of bells are tuned to different pitches, the boat bells to a higher and a clearer note and the town bells to a deeper and a more resonant note, and the difference in pitch is the means by which the listeners may distinguish the source of a signal that they hear upon the water.

The bells are sounded in sequences that the customs of the coast have established, and the sequences are distinguished one from another by the number of the strokes, the interval between them, and the rhythm with which they are struck. A single stroke, clear and sustained, is a signal of presence, indicating that the boat or the town from which the signal comes is at the customary place and is attending to the customary business of the day. A pair of strokes, struck in quick succession, is a signal of acknowledgement, indicating that the signal has been heard and that the customary response will be given. A rapid series of strokes, increasing in strength and frequency, is a signal of alarm, warning the hearer of the presence of danger and summoning the customary response of the coastal communities.

## The Calls of the Fishing Boats

The calls of the fishing boats are the first of the principal elements of the code, and the calls are sounded by the crews of the boats at the customary moments of the fishing day. The call to the nets is sounded when the boats have reached the customary fishing ground and the nets are to be set, and the call is a single stroke of the bell followed by a pause and a second stroke. The call to the catch is sounded when the nets are hauled and the customary abundance of the catch has been determined, and the call is a pair of strokes followed by a pause and a third stroke. The call to the return is sounded when the boats are making for the harbour at the close of the day's work, and the call is three strokes in succession followed by a pause and a fourth stroke.

The calls are heard by the harbormasters of the coastal towns and by the other boats that are working the grounds in the vicinity, and the calls are the customary means by which the boats coordinate their movements upon the fishing grounds. The calls also serve a commercial purpose, for the calls inform the merchants of the towns of the size and the quality of the catch that is being brought in, and the merchants may make the customary preparations of the market before the boats arrive at the quay. The chronicler notes that the calls of the fishing boats are among the most familiar of the sounds of the northern coast, and the calls are a measure of the activity that the fishing grounds sustain through the season.

## The Calls of the Town Towers

The calls of the town towers are the second of the principal elements of the code, and the calls are sounded by the tower keepers at the customary hours of the day and at the customary moments of danger. The call to the morning is sounded at the dawn of the customary fishing day, and the call is a single stroke of the great bell followed by a pause and a pair of strokes. The call to the evening is sounded at the close of the day when the boats are expected to return, and the call is a pair of strokes followed by a pause and a single stroke. The call to the storm is sounded when the customary signs of the approaching weather are observed, and the call is a rapid and a sustained ringing that continues until the danger has passed.

The town tower also sounds the calls that regulate the movement of the larger vessels that use the harbour, and the calls inform the masters of the vessels of the state of the tide and the state of the wind that the harbour master has determined. The call to the flood is sounded when the tide is at the flood and the larger vessels may safely enter the harbour, and the call is three strokes in quick succession followed by a pause and a single stroke. The call to the ebb is sounded when the tide is at the ebb and the larger vessels must wait for the next flood, and the call is a single stroke followed by a pause and three strokes in succession. The chronicler notes that the calls of the town tower are the foundation of the orderly conduct of the harbour, and the order is one of the principal assurances of the safety of the vessels that use the port.

## The Signals of Alarm

The signals of alarm are a third principal element of the code, and the signals are sounded at the moments when the customary dangers of the coast require the immediate response of the communities. The signal of the storm is sounded when the winds of the coast rise to a violence that may endanger the boats that are still upon the water, and the signal is a sustained and a rapid ringing that continues until the boats have reached the safety of the harbour or until the storm has passed. The signal of the raiders is sounded when armed vessels are observed approaching the coast, and the signal is a series of short and a rapid strokes that the customary discipline of the tower keeper requires.

The signal of the wreck is sounded when a vessel is observed to be in distress upon the coast, and the signal is a series of long and a sustained strokes that alternate with the customary silence. The signal summons the customary rescue parties of the coastal communities, and the parties put out from the shore with the customary boats and the customary equipment that the coast maintains for the purpose. The chronicler notes that the signals of alarm are observed with great strictness in every town of the [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast), and the strictness is a measure of the importance that the communities attach to the safety of the boats and the crews that work the waters of the coast.

## The Customs of the Bell Keepers

The customs of the bell keepers are the customary forms in which the bells are sounded, and the customs are observed by every bell keeper of the coast. The bell keepers are appointed by the councils of the towns, and the appointment is a position of considerable honour that is conferred upon persons of recognised standing in the community. The bell keepers are instructed in the customary use of the bells during a long apprenticeship that the coastal communities require, and the apprenticeship is the foundation of the reliability that the system depends upon.

The bell keepers maintain a log of the signals that they sound and of the signals that they hear from the boats and from the neighbouring towers, and the log is consulted by the councils of the towns when questions arise concerning the conduct of the harbour or the safety of the boats. The log is also the means by which the bell keepers may verify the accuracy of the signals that they have sounded, and the verification is a customary precaution that the prudent bell keeper observes. The chronicler notes that the customs of the bell keepers are a sophisticated code of conduct that the coastal communities have developed over the centuries, and the code is one of the principal assurances of the reliability that the system provides.

## Centres of Use

The use of the bell codes is most extensive along the northern coast of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), from the western headlands to the eastern approaches of the strait. The principal towns of the coast maintain the great bells in their towers, and the principal fishing fleets maintain the small bells in their boats, and the two are coordinated by the customary relationship that the league has established. The cities of [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara) and [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) are the principal centres of the system, and the bell keepers of the two cities are among the most experienced of the coast.

The system is also used at the smaller towns and the fishing villages of the coast, though the bells of the smaller communities are typically of more modest construction than the bells of the principal cities. The chronicler notes that the system is a distinctive feature of the northern coast, and the system is one of the principal assurances of the safety and the productivity of the fishing communities that depend upon the waters of the coast. The system is recognised by the [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast) as one of the customary institutions of the member cities, and the league has established the customary standards by which the bells of the member cities are tuned and the signals of the bell keepers are coordinated.

## Relation to the Maritime Systems

The Northern Coast Bell Codes are distinct from the [Khareshi](/content/language/pseudo/khareshi) whistled signals of the desert interior, and they are also distinct from the [Trade Tongue](/content/language/pseudo/the-trade-tongue) of the maritime commerce. The bell codes are a system of auditory signals that operates at the customary distances of the coastal waters, while the Trade Tongue is a system of hand signals that operates at the customary distances of the crowded marketplace. The two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes, and the merchant who is skilled in both may coordinate the conduct of the boats at the quay and the conduct of the trade in the marketplace.

The bell codes are also distinct from the spoken languages of the coastal communities, for the spoken tongues are true languages with grammar and vocabulary, while the bell codes are a code of limited scope, capable of conveying only a fixed set of meanings. The two systems are used in combination at the harbours and at the markets, and the combination is the foundation of the orderly conduct of the maritime commerce of the coast. The chronicler notes that the combination of the systems is one of the principal attainments of the coastal craft, and the craft is a measure of the ingenuity by which the maritime communities have adapted the auditory signals of the bells to the necessities of the coastal trade.

## Significance

The Northern Coast Bell Codes are the voice of the coast in the maritime communities of the northern shore of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), the means by which the fishing and trading communities maintain the coordination of their boats upon the waters and the orderly conduct of their harbours upon the shore. Without the bell codes, the boats that work the fishing grounds would have no reliable means of signalling their presence and their catch to the towns that depend upon them, and the harbours that receive the boats would have no reliable means of regulating the movement of the vessels that they serve. The system is a practical invention, born of the necessities of the coastal life, and the invention is a measure of the ingenuity by which the maritime communities of the known world have mastered the waters that sustain them. In the noise of the harbour where the traveller hears only the confusion of many voices and the creaking of many cables, the bell keeper sounds the customary signals, and the signals carry the news of the coast from the towers of the towns to the boats upon the water.
