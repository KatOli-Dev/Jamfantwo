## Overview

The Frostwall Fire Signs are the system of signal fires maintained by the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) of the deep interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) to communicate across the broken country of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches), the high borderlands where the escarpments, glaciated plateaus, and narrow passes of the Morvind interior meet the cold steppes of the far side. They are not a language in the ordinary sense, for they have no grammar and no vocabulary of the kind that the surface peoples recognise as speech. They are instead a code of fires, kindled upon the high points of the escarpment in a prescribed sequence and arrangement, that conveys information across distances that the voice cannot span and the messengers cannot reach in time. The system is used by the clan chiefs, the watchkeepers, and the garrison commanders of the march, and its signals are understood by every dwarf of the clans who serves in the high country.

## The Nature of the Fires

The signal fires of the Frostwall are kindled in pits that the clans have constructed upon the high points of the escarpment, and the pits are positioned to command the principal sightlines that the watchkeepers maintain along the length of the march. The pits are filled with the wood that the clans have gathered for the purpose, and the wood is of a quality that produces a flame of a particular colour and intensity when it is kindled. The customary woods include the pitch-pine of the lower slopes, which burns with a bright yellow flame, and the mountain spruce of the high country, which burns with a deeper red flame. The contrast between the two colours is the foundation of the signal system, and the contrast may be distinguished at a considerable distance in the still air of the mountain evenings.

The fires are kindled upon the customary nights and at the customary hours that the clans have established for the routine signals of the watch, and the routine signals are the principal means by which the garrisons of the march communicate their continued vigilance to one another across the length of the escarpment. The routine signals are repeated at intervals through the hours of darkness, and the repetition is the customary assurance that the watch is maintained and that the way is held. The chronicler notes that the routine signals are observed with great strictness in every clan of the march, for the failure to maintain the signals would be taken as an indication that the garrison had been overwhelmed and that the watch had fallen.

## The Numbers of Fires

The numbers of fires that are kindled at a single station is the first of the principal elements of the code, and the numbers convey the principal classifications of the information that the system is designed to communicate. A single fire, kindled alone at the customary pit, is a signal of routine, indicating that the watch is maintained and that no unusual event has been observed. Two fires, kindled side by side at the customary distance, are a signal of movement, indicating that persons or animals have been observed upon the slopes and that the garrison is observing the movement. Three fires, arranged in a triangle with the customary spacing, are a signal of danger, warning the neighbouring garrisons that the march is threatened and that the garrisons must prepare for action.

Four fires, arranged in a square at the customary spacing, are a signal of assembly, summoning the clansmen of the neighbouring garrisons to a gathering at the place that the signal indicates. Five fires, arranged in a line with the customary spacing, are a signal of victory, announcing that an engagement has been concluded successfully and that the march has been defended. The numbers are the foundation of the system, and the numbers are understood by every dwarf of the clans who has served a term of duty in the watch. The chronicler notes that the numbers are not used in combinations beyond the five that the chronicler has described, for the code is of limited scope and the limits of the code are understood as part of its reliability.

## The Colours of the Flames

The colours of the flames are the second of the principal elements of the code, and the colours refine the information that the numbers convey. The pitch-pine fire, with its bright yellow flame, is the customary fire of the routine signals, and the yellow flame indicates that the matter at hand is one of the ordinary concerns of the watch. The mountain spruce fire, with its deep red flame, is the fire of the warning signals, and the red flame indicates that the matter at hand is one of the dangers that the march defends against. The two colours may be combined at a single station, with one pit burning the pitch-pine and the other burning the spruce, and the combination indicates that the matter at hand lies between the ordinary and the dangerous.

The clans have also developed a third colour for the most urgent of the signals, and the third colour is produced by the burning of a particular lichen that the gatherers of the clans collect from the high cliffs of the escarpment. The lichen burns with a pale and a ghostly flame that is visible at a greater distance than the yellow or the red, and the lichen is reserved for the signals of the gravest moment. The lichen fire is kindled only upon the direct order of the chief of the clan, and the order is given only in circumstances that the customary law of the clans defines as the gravest that the watch may face. The chronicler notes that the lichen fire is rarely kindled, and the rarity of the lichen fire is a measure of the gravity of the occasions that require it.

## The Sequences

The sequences in which the fires are kindled are the third of the principal elements of the code, and the sequences convey the additional information that the numbers and the colours alone cannot express. The fires are kindled in a prescribed order, and the order indicates the clan or the garrison that is the source of the signal. The order is determined by the customary precedence of the clans of the march, and the precedence is established by the seniority of the clans and the customary order of the council. The watchkeeper who observes the sequence may identify the source of the signal by the order in which the fires are kindled, and the identification is the foundation of the coordination that the system provides.

The sequences are also used to indicate the direction of the matter that the signal concerns, and the direction is conveyed by the position of the principal fire in the sequence. The first fire of a sequence indicates that the matter lies to the north of the station, the second fire indicates that the matter lies to the east, the third fire indicates that the matter lies to the south, and the fourth fire indicates that the matter lies to the west. The watchkeeper who reads the sequence may determine the direction of the matter that the signal announces, and the determination is the foundation of the response that the garrison may mount. The chronicler notes that the sequences are a sophisticated refinement of the simpler code of the numbers and the colours, and the refinement is a measure of the experience that the clans have accumulated in the defence of the march.

## The Response

The response that the signal requires is determined by the customary regulations that the council of the clans has established, and the regulations are observed by every garrison of the march. The response to a routine signal is the kindling of the customary reply at the next station along the escarpment, and the reply is the formal acknowledgement that the signal has been observed and that the watch is maintained. The response to a signal of movement is the dispatch of a patrol to the place that the signal indicates, and the patrol is conducted by the customary party of observation that the garrisons maintain for the purpose.

The response to a signal of danger is the muster of the garrison at the customary place of assembly, and the muster is conducted with the customary arms and the customary provisions that the regulations require. The response to a signal of assembly is the march of the clansmen of the neighbouring garrisons to the place that the signal indicates, and the march is conducted with the customary speed and the customary order that the clans have established. The chronicler notes that the response is the foundation of the effectiveness of the system, and the effectiveness is a measure of the discipline that the clans impose upon the watchkeepers of the march.

## Centres of Use

The use of the fire signs is most extensive along the length of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches), where the escarpment of the Frostwall divides the central uplands of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) from the remote valleys of the deep interior. The principal stations of the system are situated upon the high points of the escarpment at intervals that the watchkeepers have determined to be within the reliable sightline of the customary flames, and the stations are maintained by the garrisons of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) that hold the march. The fortress of [Grimhold](/content/location/settlement/city/grimhold) is the principal station of the system, and the watchkeepers of Grimhold are responsible for the coordination of the signals along the length of the march.

The system is also used at the more remote stations that the clans maintain in the deep interior, including the station at [Ravenscrag](/content/location/settlement/village/ravenscrag) and the smaller stations that the patrol parties occupy in the high country. The stations of the deep interior are less elaborately equipped than the stations of the escarpment, but the stations employ the same code of numbers and colours and the same system of sequences that the chronicler has described. The chronicler notes that the system is a distinctive feature of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans), and the system is one of the principal assurances of the security of the deep interior of Morvind.

## Relation to Other Systems

The Frostwall Fire Signs are distinct from the [Stoneheim Bench Marks](/content/language/pseudo/stoneheim-bench-marks) that the dwarves of the holds employ in the underground galleries, though the two systems share the dwarven commitment to the practical communication of essential information. The bench marks are the code of the underground world, and the fire signs are the code of the high country, and the two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes. The bench marks are cut in stone and endure from one generation to the next, while the fire signs are kindled for the moment and must be renewed each night that the watch is maintained.

The fire signs are also distinct from the [Deep Speech](/content/language/pseudo/deep-speech) that the dwarves employ for the long-distance communication of the underground galleries, for the Deep Speech is a code of percussive signals struck upon the stone, while the fire signs are a code of light and colour displayed upon the high points of the mountain. The two systems are complementary, and the dwarf who serves in both the high country and the underground workings may pass from one code to the other as his duties require. The chronicler notes that the fire signs are among the most distinctive of the dwarven customs of the high country, and the signs are a reminder that the defences of the mountain are not confined to the underground world but extend to the high places that the watchkeepers hold against the dangers of the interior.

## Significance

The Frostwall Fire Signs are the voice of the high country in the defence of the deep interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), the means by which the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) maintain the watch upon the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) and coordinate the response to the dangers that the march defends against. Without the fire signs, the garrisons of the march would have no rapid means of warning the neighbouring garrisons of the dangers that they observe, and the coordination of the defence of the interior would be a far slower and a far more uncertain undertaking. The system is a practical invention, born of the necessities of the high country, and the invention is a measure of the dwarven capacity to maintain the watch upon one of the most demanding frontiers of the known world. In the stillness of the mountain evenings where the surface traveller sees only the darkness and the cold, the watchkeeper reads the fires of the neighbouring stations, and the fires carry the news of the march from one end of the escarpment to the other.
