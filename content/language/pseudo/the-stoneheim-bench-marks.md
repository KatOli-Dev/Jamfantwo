## Overview

The Stoneheim Bench Marks are the system of chisel-cut signs by which the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) of the eastern ranges of the [The Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/the-stoneheim-highlands) record distance, direction, and warning upon the walls of the galleries and the tunnels of the underground world. They are not a language in the ordinary sense, for they have no grammar and no vocabulary of the kind that the surface peoples recognise as speech. They are instead a code of carved signs, struck into the dressed stone of the gallery walls at the customary intervals, that conveys information to every dwarf who walks the tunnels of the hold. The system is used by the miners, the tunnel wardens, the smiths, and the messengers who travel between the workings, and its signs are understood by every dwarf who works the stone of the mountain, whatever the dialect of his spoken tongue.

## The Nature of the Signs

The signs of the Stoneheim Bench Marks are produced by the chisel and the hammer, and they are cut into the dressed surface of the gallery walls at the customary places that the holds have established for the purpose. The signs are distinguished one from another by the shape of the cut, the depth of the cut, and the arrangement of the cuts that compose a single sign. A single horizontal line, cut cleanly across the wall at the level of a dwarf's shoulder, is a sign of distance, marking the hundredth pace from the last junction. A pair of horizontal lines, set one above the other with a space between them equal to the breadth of a hand, is a sign of junction, indicating that a side gallery opens at the point where the sign is cut.

A vertical line, cut from the level of the shoulder to the level of the knee, is a sign of descent, indicating that the gallery descends at a steep grade from the point where the sign is cut. A vertical line cut in the opposite direction, from the level of the knee to the level of the shoulder, is a sign of ascent, indicating that the gallery rises from the point where the sign is cut. A circle, cut cleanly into the wall at the level of the shoulder, is a sign of warning, indicating that the gallery ahead is hazardous and that the dwarf who reads the sign must proceed with caution. The signs are cut to a standard depth that the customs of the holds have established, and the standardisation ensures that the signs may be read by the touch as well as by the eye, for the dwarf who walks the gallery in the dark may pass his hand over the wall and feel the sign that the chisel has cut.

## Distance and Direction

The principal use of the bench marks is the recording of distance and direction, and the signs that are employed for this purpose are cut at the customary intervals along every gallery of the hold. The distance signs are cut at every hundredth pace from the last junction, and the signs are accompanied by the numerical marks that the dwarven reckoning requires. The dwarves count by twenties rather than by tens, and the numerical marks that accompany the distance signs are cut in the dwarven numerals that the spoken tongue employs. The numerals are a distinctive feature of the bench marks, and the numerals may be read by any dwarf who has been instructed in the customary reckoning of the holds.

The direction signs are cut at every junction of the galleries, and the signs indicate the principal workings that may be reached by way of the gallery that opens at the junction. A sign cut at the right-hand wall of the junction, pointing toward the gallery that opens to the right, is accompanied by the mark of the working that the gallery serves. A sign cut at the left-hand wall, pointing toward the gallery that opens to the left, is accompanied in the same fashion. The marks that accompany the direction signs are the customary abbreviations that the holds have established for the principal workings, and the abbreviations are understood by every dwarf who walks the galleries. The chronicler notes that the system of distance and direction signs is among the most reliable of the dwarven inventions, and the system is the foundation upon which the orderly conduct of the work of the hold depends.

## Warnings and Cautions

The warnings and cautions that the bench marks convey are a second principal use of the system, and the signs that are employed for this purpose are cut at the places where the customary hazards of the galleries require them. The circle that the chronicler has mentioned above is the most common of the warning signs, and the circle is cut at the entrance to galleries that the tunnel wardens have judged to be hazardous. The circle may be accompanied by additional signs that specify the nature of the hazard, including the sign of falling stone, the sign of foul air, and the sign of water, and the additional signs are cut beneath the circle in the customary arrangement that the holds have established.

The sign of falling stone is a small triangle, cut with the apex pointing downward, and the sign indicates that the gallery ahead is liable to the fall of loose stone from the roof. The sign of foul air is a pair of wavy lines, cut one above the other, and the sign indicates that the air of the gallery ahead is not fit to breathe without the customary precautions. The sign of water is a series of short vertical lines, cut side by side, and the sign indicates that the gallery ahead is liable to the gathering of water in quantities that may be dangerous. The signs are cut at a sufficient distance from the hazard to allow the dwarf who reads them to withdraw or to take the customary precautions, and the distance is determined by the customary judgement of the tunnel wardens who maintain the signs.

## The Marking of Property

The marking of property is a third principal use of the bench marks, and the signs that are employed for this purpose are cut at the places where the boundaries of the principal workings of the hold are recorded. The boundaries are determined by the customary law of the federation, and the boundaries are marked by the signs that the customs of the holds have established. A sign cut in the form of a hammer, with the head of the hammer pointing toward the working that it claims, is the mark of the smithing claim, and the mark indicates that the working that lies in the direction of the hammer is the property of the smiths of the hold. A sign cut in the form of a chisel, with the blade of the chisel pointing toward the working that it claims, is the mark of the mining claim, and the mark indicates that the working is the property of the miners.

The signs of property are cut at the junctions and at the principal points along the boundaries, and the signs are renewed by the wardens at the customary intervals to ensure that the signs remain legible. The chronicler notes that the marking of property is a matter of considerable importance in the life of the holds, for the wealth of the mountain is the foundation of the prosperity of the communities and the boundaries must be clearly indicated if disputes are to be avoided. The signs are also the means by which the council of the hold may determine the extent of the workings that each family or each craft may claim, and the records that are kept in the hall of the council are based upon the signs that are cut in the walls of the galleries.

## The Maintenance of the Signs

The maintenance of the signs is the responsibility of the tunnel wardens of the hold, and the wardens conduct the customary inspections of the galleries at the intervals that the customs of the hold require. The inspections are conducted by the wardens in pairs, each pair accompanied by an apprentice who is being instructed in the reading and the cutting of the signs. The wardens carry the customary tools of their office, including the chisel and the hammer with which the signs are cut and renewed, and the wardens are required to renew any sign that has been damaged by the action of the water or the falling of the stone.

The inspection is also the occasion for the wardens to record the observations that they make of the condition of the galleries, and the observations are entered in the customary log that the wardens maintain in the hall of the council. The log is a record of the state of the workings from one inspection to the next, and the log is consulted by the council when questions arise concerning the safety or the productivity of the galleries. The chronicler notes that the maintenance of the signs is a duty that the wardens discharge with great care, and the care is a measure of the importance that the holds attach to the orderly conduct of the work of the mountain.

## Relation to Deep Speech

The Stoneheim Bench Marks are distinct from the [Deep Speech](/content/language/pseudo/deep-speech) that the dwarves employ for the long-distance communication of the galleries, though the two systems are used in combination at the principal junctions and at the places where the customary signals require them. The Deep Speech carries the messages that must travel beyond the range of the voice, and the Bench Marks record the information that the dwarf who walks the gallery may read upon the wall. The two systems are complementary, and the dwarf who is skilled in both may walk the length of the longest gallery of the hold without the need of a guide.

The Bench Marks are also distinct from the spoken language of the dwarves, which the dwarves call Khazdul, for the spoken tongue is a true language with a grammar and a vocabulary, while the bench marks are a code of limited scope, capable of conveying only a fixed set of meanings. The two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes, and a dwarf who converses in the spoken language at the hearth will rely upon the bench marks when he walks the gallery. The chronicler notes that the bench marks are a practical invention, born of the necessities of the underground world, and the invention is a measure of the dwarven capacity to adapt the materials of the mountain to the purposes of the dwarven civilisation.

## Centres of Use

The use of the bench marks is most extensive in the great workings of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim), where the galleries extend for hundreds of miles beneath the eastern ranges of the [The Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/the-stoneheim-highlands) and where the orderly conduct of the work requires that every passage and every junction be marked with the customary signs. The bench marks are maintained in every hold of the federation, from the great central halls of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) to the most remote of the workings in the high country, and the system is one of the principal assurances of the unity of the federation.

The surface peoples who visit the dwarven halls are sometimes permitted to view the bench marks in the galleries that adjoin the places where the holds receive their guests, and the visitors are accustomed to marvel at the care with which the signs are cut and renewed. The deeper marks of the system, the signs that record the boundaries of the workings and the warnings that the wardens maintain, are not shared with the outsiders, and the visitors are conducted only through the galleries where the customary marks of distance and direction are displayed. The chronicler notes that the surface visitors are often impressed by the orderly appearance of the marked galleries, and the impression is a measure of the dwarven commitment to the maintenance of the works of the mountain.

## Significance

The Stoneheim Bench Marks are the voice of the gallery in the underground world, the means by which the dwarven people maintain the orderly conduct of the work of the mountain. Without the bench marks, the galleries of the holds would be a maze of unmarked passages, and the dwarf who walked the tunnels would be dependent upon the memory of his predecessors and the guidance of his companions for every journey that he undertook. The system is a practical invention, born of the necessities of the underground life, and the invention is a measure of the dwarven genius for adapting the materials of the world to the purposes of their civilisation. In the deep places where the surface dweller sees only darkness and stone, the dwarf reads the signs that the chisel has cut, and the signs carry the news of distance and direction and warning from one end of the hold to the other.
