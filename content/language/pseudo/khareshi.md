## Overview

Khareshi is the system of whistle signals used by the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), the nomadic peoples of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert in [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), to communicate across the open wastes where the voice cannot carry and distance defeats the spoken word. It is a code of pitches and sequences, produced by the lips and fingers of a skilled caller, that carries information across distances of a mile or more. Herders, scouts, and caravan guides of the desert interior use the system, and its signals are understood by every nomad who travels the wastes, whatever the dialect of his spoken tongue. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa), whose hearing is sharper than that of humans, can read Khareshi signals at ranges inaudible to the human ear and are valued as sentinels and relay callers.

## The Nature of the Signals

The signals of Khareshi are produced by whistling through the fingers, a technique that sends a high, piercing note across the open desert. They are distinguished by pitch, duration, and pattern. A single long note held for a count of three is a signal of greeting, indicating that the caller is a friend approaching in peace. A pair of short notes rising in pitch signals water, informing the hearer that a spring or well has been found. A series of short notes descending in pitch and repeated at intervals signals danger, warning of raiders, predators, or approaching storms. A steady alternation of high and low notes sustained for a count of ten signals gathering, summoning scattered members of the kindred to a meeting point.

The range of the signals is determined by the openness of the terrain and the stillness of the air. A caller stationed upon a high dune can be heard at a distance of several miles in the quiet of the desert morning. Wind can distort the signals or carry them away, and the practised caller learns to read the behaviour of the air and choose his moment accordingly. The system is thus a subtle art as well as a practical one; the reputation of a scout rests in part upon the clarity and reliability of his calls. The salawa, with their superior hearing, are capable of relaying signals across distances that would defeat human callers and are employed as relay stations across the longest stretches of open desert.

The reliability of Khareshi depends upon an established discipline of repetition. A distant caller answers each signal before acting upon it, and a relay scout repeats the call from the next rise so the message can travel beyond the reach of its originator. Names, detailed directions, and unfamiliar news cannot be carried in this fashion, but the small repertory is sufficient to keep separated travellers aware of water, danger, movement, and assembly. The limits of the code are understood as part of its safety: no prudent guide relies upon a whistle to convey meaning that must instead be delivered by a messenger.

## Centres of Use

Khareshi is most extensively used among the nomadic kindreds of the desert interior, where distances between camps are measured in days rather than hours and the spoken word cannot span the gaps between scattered groups. Herders who tend flocks on the margins of the settled lands are the most frequent users, for their flocks are spread across wide pastures and the herders must remain in contact with one another and with the camp. The herders use Khareshi to signal the discovery of fresh grazing, the presence of predators, and the direction of the day's movement. Caravan guides crossing the desert between coastal cities and interior settlements also make use of Khareshi, sending signals between forward scouts and the main body of the caravan to coordinate passage through difficult terrain.

The settled peoples of the coastal cities who deal with the nomads are generally aware of Khareshi, but few have learned to read its signals, for the pitch and timing of the whistled calls require a trained ear and knowledge of desert conventions. Traders who travel with caravans are taught a limited set of signals for practical purposes, such as the call for water and the call for danger, but the deeper codes carrying news of the kindreds and intelligence of the desert are kept within the nomadic community. An outsider who attempted to learn these restricted calls would be regarded with suspicion.

## Relation to the Spoken Tongues

Khareshi is distinct from the spoken languages of the desert peoples. The spoken tongues are true languages with grammar and vocabulary, while the signal system is a code of limited scope capable of conveying only a fixed set of meanings. The two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes. A nomad who converses in the spoken tongue within the camp will use the Khareshi calls when he moves beyond the hearing of the hearth. The spoken languages of the Kharesh, of which there are several, are languages of words and sentences used for the ordinary commerce of life and for the preservation of the traditions and histories of the kindreds. Khareshi calls, by contrast, are the voice of the desert itself, the means by which the scattered peoples of the wastes maintain their connection across the distances that keep them apart.

## Significance

Khareshi is the voice of the open desert, the means by which the nomadic peoples of the Kharesh maintain the coherence and safety of their scattered communities. Without the whistled signals, the kindreds of the interior would be isolated by distances the voice cannot cross, and the coordination of herds, caravans, and camps would be a far more difficult and dangerous undertaking. The system is a practical invention born of the necessities of the nomadic life, a measure of the ingenuity by which the desert peoples have adapted to the conditions of their world. In the silence of the wastes where the traveller hears only the wind, the nomad hears the whistle of the Khareshi calls, carrying news of water and danger and gathering from one horizon to the next.
