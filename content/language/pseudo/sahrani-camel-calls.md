## Overview

The [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) Camel Calls are the system of vocal and percussive signals used by the camel drivers and the [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) guides of the inland caravan routes of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) to direct the conduct of the camels that carry the goods of the trade across the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). They are not a language in the ordinary sense, for they have no grammar and no vocabulary of the kind that the surface peoples recognise as speech. They are instead a code of sounds, produced by the voice and by the striking of customary implements, that conveys information to the camels and to the other drivers of the caravan. The system is used by the camel drivers, the salawa guides, and the merchants who oversee the caravans at the inland stops, and its signals are understood by every camel and by every driver who travels the desert trade.

## The Nature of the Calls

The calls of the system are produced by the voice and by the striking of the customary stick that every driver carries upon the journey, and the calls are distinguished one from another by the pitch, the duration, and the rhythm of the sound. A single low note, sustained for a count of three and drawn out at the close, is the call to rise, instructing the camel to lift its load from the ground at the commencement of the day's march. A pair of short notes, rising in pitch and repeated at intervals, is the call to kneel, instructing the camel to lower itself to the ground at the customary halt. A rapid series of short notes, descending in pitch and struck with the stick upon the saddle, is the call to halt, instructing the camel to stop in its tracks and to wait for the customary word of the driver.

A long sustained note, unbroken and unwavering, is the call to advance, instructing the camel to move forward at the customary pace and to maintain the pace until the next call is given. A pair of notes, the first low and the second high, is the call to turn, instructing the camel to change direction by the customary angle and to resume the advance upon the new bearing. The chronicler notes that the calls are not loud, for the camels are trained to attend to the voice of the driver that is nearest to them, and the calls are pitched at a level that carries to the customary distance without disturbing the neighbouring animals.

## The Sounds of the Stick

The sounds of the stick are a second element of the system, and the sounds are produced by the striking of the customary implement against the saddle, the ground, or the body of the camel itself. The striking of the stick against the saddle is a signal of attention, calling the camel to attend to the voice of the driver that is about to give the next call. The striking of the stick against the ground is a signal of urgency, indicating that the matter at hand requires the immediate compliance of the camel and that the customary delays are not to be observed. The striking of the stick against the body of the camel is a signal of correction, indicating that the camel has failed to observe the previous call and that the customary discipline must be applied.

The sounds are distinguished one from another by the surface against which the stick is struck and by the force with which the striking is conducted. A light striking of the stick against the saddle is the customary sound of attention, while a heavy striking of the stick against the saddle is the customary sound of correction. The chronicler notes that the driver is instructed in the customary use of the stick during the long apprenticeship that the camel trade requires, and the instruction is one of the principal attainments of the driver's craft. The craft is transmitted from one generation to the next through the service of the caravans, and the service is the foundation of the reliability that the desert trade depends upon.

## The Calls of the Salawa

The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) guides who conduct the principal caravans across the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) employ a supplementary code of calls that is understood by every camel and every driver of the desert interior. The salawa calls are distinguished from the driver's calls by the higher pitch at which they are sounded, and the higher pitch is a means by which the salawa may direct the attention of the entire caravan at the moments when the customary direction must be changed. The salawa calls are not addressed to the camels directly but to the drivers of the camels, and the drivers translate the salawa calls into the driver's calls that the camels understand.

The salawa calls include the call to the water, sounded when the next oasis is at hand and the customary measures of the watering must be prepared. The salawa calls include the call to the storm, sounded when the customary signs of the approaching sand storm are observed and the customary dispositions of the caravan must be made. The salawa calls include the call to the stranger, sounded when a caravan or a party is observed upon the route and the customary measures of identification must be observed. The chronicler notes that the salawa calls are a distinctive feature of the desert trade, and the calls are a measure of the salawa capacity to coordinate the movements of the caravans across the featureless country of the deep desert.

## The Training of the Camels

The training of the camels to attend to the calls is a long process that occupies the first several years of the animal's life, and the training is conducted by the drivers of the caravans in cooperation with the breeders who raise the animals. The training begins with the customary calls that the chronicler has described, and the calls are repeated at the customary intervals until the camel has learned to associate the call with the action that the call requires. The training is reinforced by the customary discipline that the driver applies when the camel fails to observe a call, and the discipline is administered with the customary stick that the driver carries.

The camels that have completed the training are capable of understanding the customary calls without the need for the supplementary signals of the voice or the hand, and the trained camels are the foundation of the reliability that the desert trade depends upon. The camels are also capable of distinguishing the calls of their own driver from the calls of a stranger, and the distinction is a measure of the bond that is established between the camel and the driver through the long training. The chronicler notes that the bond is one of the most distinctive features of the desert trade, and the bond is a reminder that the camels of the caravan are not merely beasts of burden but partners in the work of the crossing.

## The Customs of the Caravan

The customs of the caravan are the customary forms in which the calls are employed, and the customs are observed by every driver and every merchant who travels the desert trade. The customs include the customary order in which the calls are sounded, with the call to rise sounded first in the morning, the call to advance sounded throughout the day's march, and the call to kneel sounded at the customary halt. The customs include the customary intervals at which the calls are repeated, with the calls to advance repeated at the customary periods to ensure that the camels maintain the pace and do not stray from the line of march.

The customs also include the customary responses that the drivers give to the salawa calls, with the drivers acknowledging the salawa calls by the customary sounds of the stick and the customary repetition of the call that the salawa has given. The acknowledgement is the formal signal that the driver has understood the salawa's direction and that the customary measures will be taken. The chronicler notes that the customs are a sophisticated code of conduct that the desert trade has developed over the centuries, and the code is one of the principal assurances of the smooth operation of the caravans that cross the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh).

## Centres of Use

The use of the camel calls is most extensive along the principal caravan routes of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), including the [Kharesh Caravan Way](/content/location/route/trade/kharesh-caravan-way) that connects the inland cities to the coast. The principal routes are travelled by the great caravans of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) and by the smaller caravans of the independent merchants who operate from the inland oases. The drivers who serve upon the routes are the principal users of the system, and the drivers are trained in the customary use of the calls during the long apprenticeship that the camel trade requires.

The system is also used at the inland stops where the caravans gather for the customary night halt, and the calls are employed at the stops for the customary direction of the camels that are being watered or unloaded. The chronicler notes that the system is a distinctive feature of the desert trade, and the system is one of the principal assurances of the safety of the caravans that cross the wastes. The settled peoples of the coastal cities who have dealings with the caravans are generally aware of the existence of the calls, but few have learned to sound them with the customary precision, and the settled traders rely upon the drivers and the salawa for the direction of the camels that they employ.

## Relation to Other Desert Systems

The camel calls are distinct from the [Kharesh Trail Markers](/content/language/pseudo/kharesh-trail-markers) that the nomads employ for the marking of the routes, though the two systems are used in combination at the principal caravan stops and at the places where the customary signals require them. The trail markers are the code of the route, and the camel calls are the code of the caravan, and the two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes. The markers endure from one season to the next, while the calls are sounded for the moment and must be repeated each time that the direction of the camels is required.

The camel calls are also distinct from the [Khareshi](/content/language/pseudo/khareshi) whistled signals that the nomads employ for the short-range communication of the open desert. The whistled signals are a code of human communication that carries across the open wastes, while the camel calls are a code of animal direction that operates at the customary distance between the driver and the camel. The two systems are complementary, and the driver who is skilled in both may coordinate the movements of the camels and the movements of the other drivers of the caravan in a single operation. The chronicler notes that the combination of the systems is one of the principal attainments of the desert craft, and the craft is a measure of the ingenuity by which the desert peoples have adapted to the conditions of their world.

## Significance

The Sahrani Camel Calls are the voice of the driver in the desert caravan, the means by which the camel drivers and the salawa guides of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) direct the conduct of the camels that carry the goods of the trade across the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). Without the camel calls, the direction of the camels would be a far more difficult and a far more uncertain undertaking, dependent upon the uncertain signals of the hand and the unreliable responses of the animals to the unaccustomed voice. The system is a practical invention, born of the necessities of the caravan trade, and the invention is a measure of the ingenuity by which the desert peoples have mastered the animals upon which the trade depends. In the silence of the open desert where the traveller hears only the wind and the pad of the camels' feet, the driver sounds the customary calls, and the calls carry the direction of the caravan from the front of the line to the rear.
