## Overview

The Kharesh Trail Markers are the system of stones, stacked and arranged by the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), by which the nomads of the interior mark the caravan routes that cross the open wastes between the oases. They are not a language in the ordinary sense, for they have no grammar and no vocabulary of the kind that the settled peoples recognise as speech. They are instead a code of stone arrangements, set upon the open desert at the customary intervals, that conveys information to every traveller of the wastes who has been instructed in the customary reading of the signs. The system is used by the herders, the caravan guides, and the scouts of the desert interior, and its signs are understood by every nomad who travels the wastes, whatever the dialect of his spoken tongue.

## The Nature of the Markers

The markers of the system are constructed from the stones that the desert provides, and the stones are gathered from the surface of the erg and from the gravel plains that surround the oases. The markers are of three principal kinds, distinguished one from another by the manner of their construction and the arrangement of the stones that compose them. The cairn is a simple stack of stones, set one upon another to a height of approximately three feet, and the cairn is the most common of the markers. The platform is a low arrangement of stones laid flat upon the ground in a rectangular pattern, and the platform is used at the places where the customary traffic requires a more substantial indication. The spire is a tall stack of stones, narrower at the base and broader at the top, set upon a platform of the customary construction, and the spire is reserved for the places of particular importance along the routes.

The markers are set upon the open desert at the customary intervals that the nomads have determined by long experience of the crossing, and the intervals are typically a day's march for a loaded camel at the customary pace of the caravan. The places where the markers are set are not random, for the markers are placed at the points where the customary route may be reliably found in the featureless country of the deep desert. The points include the occasional rocky outcrop that rises above the level of the sand, the margin of a salt pan that may be recognised by the colour of the ground, and the gravel skirt that surrounds an isolated hill. The chronicler notes that the placement of the markers is a skill that the guides of the desert acquire through a long apprenticeship, and the skill is one of the principal attainments of the nomadic life.

## The Numbers of Stones

The numbers of stones that compose the uppermost layer of a cairn are the first of the principal elements of the code, and the numbers convey the principal classifications of the information that the system is designed to communicate. A single stone at the top of a cairn is a sign that the water ahead is plentiful, indicating that the next oasis is one of the larger and more reliable sources that the desert provides. Two stones at the top are a sign that the water ahead is scanty, warning the traveller that the next oasis is one of the smaller sources and that the customary measures of conservation must be observed upon the arrival. Three stones at the top are a sign of danger, warning the traveller that the route ahead is attended by one of the customary hazards of the desert, whether the hazard be raiders, the drifting of the sand, or the unreliability of the water at the customary wells.

Four stones at the top are a sign of the junction, indicating that the route ahead divides and that the traveller must choose between the principal directions that the junction offers. Five stones at the top are a sign of the shrine, indicating that the place is one of the sacred sites of the tribes and that the customary observances must be performed before the journey may be continued. The numbers are understood by every nomad who has been instructed in the reading of the markers, and the instruction is a part of the customary education of the young of the tribes. The chronicler notes that the numbers are the foundation of the system, and the numbers may be distinguished at a considerable distance by the traveller who has learned to read the silhouette of the cairn against the sky.

## The Arrangement of Stones

The arrangement of stones around the base of a cairn is the second of the principal elements of the code, and the arrangement conveys the additional information that the numbers alone cannot express. The stones around the base may be laid in a circle, in a square, in a triangle, or in a line, and the shape of the arrangement indicates the principal direction of the matter that the marker concerns. A circle of stones around the base indicates that the matter is one that concerns the oasis that lies in any direction from the marker, while a square of stones indicates that the matter concerns the route that leads to the nearest settled place of the coast. A triangle of stones, with the apex of the triangle pointing in the customary direction, indicates that the matter concerns a particular route that diverges from the principal way.

A line of stones, set in the customary direction, indicates that the matter concerns the route that follows the line, and the line is a sign that the traveller must follow the line of stones to the next marker if he would find the customary water. The chronicler notes that the arrangement is a sophisticated refinement of the simpler code of the numbers, and the refinement is a measure of the experience that the tribes have accumulated in the marking of the desert routes. The arrangement may be read by the traveller who dismounts from his camel and walks the circumference of the cairn, and the reading is a customary precaution that the prudent traveller observes at every marker that he encounters.

## The Colour of the Stones

The colour of the stones that compose the uppermost layer is the third of the principal elements of the code, and the colour conveys the reliability of the information that the marker provides. The white stones, gathered from the chalk outcrops of the desert margins, are the customary stones of the routine markers, and the white colour indicates that the information has been confirmed by the customary authorities of the tribes. The black stones, gathered from the basalt fields of the deep desert, are the stones of the verified markers, and the black colour indicates that the information has been verified by a guide of recognised standing and that the marker may be relied upon in the customary circumstances.

The red stones, gathered from the iron-bearing sands of the deep erg, are the stones of the urgent markers, and the red colour indicates that the information concerns a matter of immediate importance to the safety of the traveller. The red stones are set only upon the direct order of the chief of the tribe or the senior guide of the route, and the order is given only in circumstances that the customary law of the tribes defines as urgent. The chronicler notes that the colour of the stones is a distinctive feature of the system, and the colour may be distinguished at a distance by the traveller who knows what to look for upon the surface of the cairn.

## The Reliability of the System

The reliability of the system depends upon an established discipline of renewal. The markers are inspected by the guides of the tribes at the customary intervals, and the markers that have been damaged by the wind or the passage of the animals are rebuilt by the guides who find them. The rebuilding is conducted according to the customary forms that the tribes have established, and the forms ensure that the information that the marker conveys is renewed at the same time as the stones are replaced. The chronicler notes that the renewal is a duty that the guides discharge with great care, and the care is a measure of the importance that the tribes attach to the maintenance of the routes.

The system is least reliable during the season of the sand storms, when the customary winds of the desert may bury the markers beneath the drifting sand or may scatter the stones of which the markers are composed. The guides of the tribes are instructed to mark the customary positions of the markers with the stakes that they drive into the ground at the time of the construction, and the stakes are the means by which the markers may be restored after the storm has passed. The chronicler notes that the storms are a hazard that the system has learned to accommodate, and the accommodation is one of the principal assurances that the customary maintenance provides.

## Centres of Use

The use of the trail markers is most extensive along the principal caravan routes that cross the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), including the route that the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) maintains between the inland cities and the coast. The principal routes are marked at the customary intervals that the chronicler has described, and the routes are the arteries of the desert trade. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) guides who conduct the caravans across the desert are the principal readers of the markers, and the salawa are instructed in the reading of the markers from an early age.

The system is also used at the lesser routes that the nomads maintain between the scattered wells and the seasonal pastures, and the markers of the lesser routes are typically smaller in construction than the markers of the principal routes. The chronicler notes that the system is a distinctive feature of the nomadic life of the desert, and the system is one of the principal assurances of the safety of the travellers who cross the wastes. The settled peoples of the coastal cities are generally aware of the existence of the markers, but few have learned to read the signs, and the settled traders who travel with the caravans rely upon the salawa guides for the interpretation of the markers that they encounter.

## Relation to the Whistled Calls

The trail markers are distinct from the [Khareshi](/content/language/pseudo/khareshi) whistled calls that the nomads employ for the short-range communication of the open desert, though the two systems are used in combination at the principal caravan stops and at the places where the customary signals require them. The trail markers are the code of the route, and the whistled calls are the code of the moment, and the two systems are used in different contexts and for different purposes. The markers endure from one season to the next, while the calls are sounded for the moment and must be repeated each time that the communication is required.

The two systems are complementary, and the salawa guide who is skilled in both may conduct a caravan across the deepest of the desert by the reading of the markers and the sounding of the calls. The chronicler notes that the combination of the two systems is one of the principal attainments of the nomadic art, and the art is a measure of the salawa capacity to maintain the connections that the desert life requires. The art is transmitted from one generation to the next through the long apprenticeship that the salawa conduct, and the apprenticeship is the foundation of the reliability that the desert trade depends upon.

## Significance

The Kharesh Trail Markers are the voice of the open desert, the means by which the nomadic peoples of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) maintain the routes that connect the scattered oases and the seasonal pastures of the wastes. Without the trail markers, the caravans that cross the desert would have no reliable means of finding the water that sustains the crossing, and the trade that flows between the coast and the interior would be a far more dangerous and a far more uncertain undertaking. The system is a practical invention, born of the necessities of the desert life, and the invention is a measure of the ingenuity by which the peoples of the wastes have adapted to the conditions of their world. In the silence of the open desert where the traveller sees only the level sand and the unbroken horizon, the nomad reads the stones that the guides have set, and the stones carry the news of water and danger and direction from one oasis to the next.
