## Stone Signs of the Wastes

The [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) Trail Markers are the system of stones, stacked and arranged by the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), by which the nomads of the interior mark the caravan routes crossing the open wastes between the oases. It is a code of stone arrangements, set upon the open desert at customary intervals, that conveys information to every traveller of the wastes who has been instructed in their reading. Herders, caravan guides, and scouts of the desert interior use the system, and its signs are understood by every nomad who travels the wastes, whatever the dialect of his spoken tongue. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) guides who conduct caravans across the desert are the principal readers of the markers, instructed in their reading from an early age.

## Cairns, Platforms, and Spires

The markers are constructed from stones gathered from the surface of the erg and from the gravel plains surrounding the oases. They are of three principal kinds, distinguished by their construction. The cairn is a simple stack of stones set one upon another to a height of approximately three feet and is the most common marker. The platform is a low arrangement of stones laid flat upon the ground in a rectangular pattern, used where customary traffic requires a more substantial indication. The spire is a tall stack of stones, narrower at the base and broader at the top, set upon a platform of customary construction, reserved for places of particular importance along the routes.

The markers are set at intervals determined by long experience of the crossing, typically a day's march for a loaded camel at the customary pace of the caravan. The places where markers are set are not random; they are placed at points where the customary route may be reliably found in the featureless country of the deep desert, including rocky outcrops rising above the level of the sand, the margin of a salt pan recognisable by the colour of the ground, and the gravel skirt surrounding an isolated hill. The placement of the markers is a skill acquired through a long apprenticeship, one of the principal attainments of the nomadic life.

## Meaning by Number

The number of stones composing the uppermost layer of a cairn is the first principal element of the code, conveying the primary classification of information. A single stone at the top is a sign that the water ahead is plentiful, indicating that the next oasis is one of the larger and more reliable sources. Two stones at the top are a sign that the water ahead is scanty, warning that the next oasis is a smaller source and that conservation measures must be observed upon arrival. Three stones at the top are a sign of danger, warning that the route ahead is attended by one of the customary hazards of the desert, whether raiders, drifting sand, or unreliable water at the customary wells. Four stones at the top are a sign of a junction, indicating that the route ahead divides and the traveller must choose between principal directions. Five stones at the top are a sign of a shrine, indicating a sacred site of the tribes where customary observances must be performed before the journey may continue. The numbers are understood by every nomad instructed in the reading of the markers, a part of the customary education of the young of the tribes. The numbers can be distinguished at considerable distance by the traveller who has learned to read the silhouette of the cairn against the sky.

## Reading Base Patterns

The arrangement of stones around the base of a cairn is the second principal element of the code, conveying additional information the numbers alone cannot express. The stones around the base may be laid in a circle, square, triangle, or line, and the shape indicates the principal direction of the matter the marker concerns. A circle of stones indicates that the matter concerns the oasis that lies in any direction from the marker. A square indicates that the matter concerns the route leading to the nearest settled place of the coast. A triangle with its apex pointing in the customary direction indicates that the matter concerns a particular route diverging from the principal way. A line of stones set in the customary direction indicates that the route follows the line, and the traveller must follow the line to the next marker to find the customary water. The arrangement is a sophisticated refinement of the simpler code of numbers, a measure of the experience the tribes have accumulated in marking the desert routes. The arrangement may be read by the traveller who dismounts and walks the circumference of the cairn, a customary precaution observed at every marker.

## Stone Colour as Code

The colour of the stones composing the uppermost layer is the third principal element of the code, conveying the reliability of the information the marker provides. White stones, gathered from the chalk outcrops of the desert margins, are the customary stones of routine markers, indicating that the information has been confirmed by the customary authorities of the tribes. Black stones, gathered from the basalt fields of the deep desert, are the stones of verified markers, indicating that the information has been verified by a guide of recognised standing and that the marker may be relied upon in customary circumstances. Red stones, gathered from the iron-bearing sands of the deep erg, are the stones of urgent markers, indicating that the information concerns a matter of immediate importance to the safety of the traveller. Red stones are set only upon the direct order of the chief of the tribe or the senior guide of the route, given only in circumstances the customary law defines as urgent.

## Maintenance and Storms

The reliability of the system depends upon an established discipline of renewal. The markers are inspected by guides of the tribes at customary intervals, and those damaged by wind or the passage of animals are rebuilt according to the customary forms the tribes have established, ensuring the information is renewed at the same time as the stones are replaced. The renewal is a duty the guides discharge with great care, a measure of the importance the tribes attach to the maintenance of the routes. The system is least reliable during the season of sand storms, when customary winds may bury the markers beneath drifting sand or scatter the stones. The guides are instructed to mark the customary positions with stakes driven into the ground at the time of construction, the means by which the markers may be restored after the storm has passed. The storms are a hazard the system has learned to accommodate.

## Routes That Bear Markers

The trail markers are most extensively used along the principal caravan routes crossing the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), including the route the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) maintains between inland cities and the coast. These principal routes are marked at the customary intervals and are the arteries of the desert trade. The system is also used at the lesser routes maintained between scattered wells and seasonal pastures, and the markers of the lesser routes are typically smaller in construction. The settled peoples of the coastal cities are generally aware of the markers but few have learned to read the signs, and the settled traders who travel with caravans rely upon the salawa guides for interpretation.

## Trail Markers and Khareshi

The trail markers are distinct from the [Khareshi](/content/language/pseudo/khareshi) whistled calls and the [Sahrani Camel Calls](/content/language/pseudo/sahrani-camel-calls) used for directing camels, though the systems are used in combination at principal caravan stops. The trail markers are the code of the route, enduring from one season to the next; the whistled calls are the code of the moment, sounded and repeated each time communication is required. The two systems are complementary, and a salawa guide skilled in both may conduct a caravan across the deepest desert by reading the markers and sounding the calls. The combination of the two systems is one of the principal attainments of the nomadic art, transmitted from one generation to the next through apprenticeship.

## Marking the Desert Routes

The Kharesh Trail Markers are the voice of the open desert, the means by which the nomadic peoples of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) maintain the routes connecting scattered oases and seasonal pastures. Without the trail markers, the caravans crossing the desert would have no reliable means of finding the water that sustains the crossing, and the trade flowing between coast and interior would be far more dangerous and uncertain. The system is a practical invention born of the necessities of the desert life, a measure of the ingenuity by which the peoples of the wastes have adapted to the conditions of their world. In the silence of the open desert where the traveller sees only the level sand and the unbroken horizon, the nomad reads the stones the guides have set, and the stones carry news of water, danger, and direction from one oasis to the next.
