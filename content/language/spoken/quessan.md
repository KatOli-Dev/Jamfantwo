## Overview

Quessan is the spoken language of the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the smaller elven communities of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the western forests, used for the ordinary commerce of life and for the preservation of the elven traditions and the elven learning. The name is the elves' own, and it is older than any of the elven settlements that the chronicler has catalogued. Quessan is distinct from the [Sylvan](/content/language/pseudo/sylvan) signals, which are a code of limited scope used only for the hunt and the walk and the stealthy approach through the forest. Quessan is a true language, with a grammar and a vocabulary that have been refined over many generations, and it is the language of the hearth and the song of the festival and the long conversation of the elven councils.

The chronicler records that Quessan is treated by the elven communities as a trust rather than as a possession, and the language is transmitted from parent to child with a care that the chronicler finds unusual among the languages of the known world. The elven elders maintain the forms of the language in oral tradition, and the forms are revised only when a generation has had time to weigh the change against the older usage. The result is a language of great stability, and the chronicler has noted that the forms of Quessan recorded in the earliest elven chronicles are recognisable in the speech of the present day, though the elven scholars are quick to point out the differences in pronunciation and in vocabulary that the centuries have introduced.

## The Sounds and the Script

Quessan is spoken in a register that is quieter than the common speech of the surrounding human communities, and the elves are noted for the evenness of their tone and the deliberation of their articulation. The language has a wide range of vowel sounds, and the chronicler records that the vowels are used to carry the rhythm of the sentence in a manner that the consonants of the common human speech do not. The consonants are few in number, and the elves rely upon the vowels to distinguish the words that would otherwise be confused in the rapid speech of the markets and the festivals. The chronicler notes that the elven singers use the vowel range to produce the long sustained notes of the elven song, and the song is one of the most distinctive of the cultural expressions of the elven people.

The script of Quessan is a set of curved marks that are incised into the soft bark of certain trees of the deep forest, and the script is read in the long horizontal lines that run from the base of the trunk to the first branch. The marks are not letters in the sense that the human scripts are letters, for each mark represents a syllable rather than a single sound, and the order of the marks in the line is read in a particular direction that the elves determine by the orientation of the tree. The chronicler has been unable to discover a rule for the orientation that holds across all the inscriptions that the chronicler has seen, and the chronicler records that the elven scholars treat the orientation as a matter of custom rather than of rule. The marks are cut with a small blade of bronze or of stone, and the cut is shallow enough that the bark grows over it in a generation, leaving the inscription to be renewed by the keeper of the tree.

## The Vocabulary and the Compounds

The vocabulary of Quessan is rich in words for the things of the forest and the long observations of the elven scholars. The chronicler records that the elven language has more words for the kinds of tree, the kinds of leaf, the kinds of bird song, and the kinds of shadow than any of the human languages of the known world, and the richness of the vocabulary is a measure of the long attention that the elven people have given to the life of the woodland. The vocabulary is also rich in words for the relations of kinship, for the elven communities place great weight upon the distinctions of descent and of marriage, and the distinctions are preserved in a set of compound terms that the human scholars find difficult to translate. The chronicler notes that the elven compounds are formed by the joining of two or more of the simple words, and the compounds are used in the formal speech of the councils and the festivals.

The vocabulary of Quessan is also notable for what it lacks. The chronicler records that the language has few words for the instruments of war, and the elven scholars are said to remark upon the absence as a feature of the language rather than as a defect. The chronicler has heard it said that the elven people do not require the words because they do not require the things, but the chronicler notes that the elven communities have on occasion taken up arms in defence of their forests, and the elven warriors use the borrowed words of the human speech for the instruments they employ. The observation is taken by the chronicler as a measure of the customs of the elven people, and the customs are the foundation of the elven relationship with the forest and with the wider world.

## The Speech of the Councils and the Festivals

Quessan is the language of the elven councils, and the councils are conducted in a formal register that is preserved by the elders of the community. The chronicler records that the formal register uses a set of fixed phrases for the opening and the closing of the proceedings, and the phrases are inherited from the earlier generations of the elven people. The formal register is also used for the public announcements of the community, and the announcements are spoken from the customary platforms in the market squares of the elven settlements. The chronicler has observed that the formal register is intelligible to the elven children of seven or eight years, and the intelligibility is the measure of the care with which the forms are transmitted.

The festivals of the elven communities are also conducted in Quessan, and the songs of the festivals are sung in the formal register. The songs are long, and the chronicler records that a festival song may last for the better part of a day, with the verses taken up in turn by the singers of the community. The songs are not narrative in the manner of the human songs, for the elven songs are concerned with the recurrence of the seasons and the long memory of the community, and the verses are repeated with small variations from year to year. The chronicler has heard it said that the songs of the festivals are the most complete of the oral records of the elven communities, and the chronicler notes that the songs have done much to preserve the forms of Quessan through the centuries.

## The Relation to the Spoken Tongues of the Other Peoples

Quessan is distinct from the [Sylvan](/content/language/pseudo/sylvan) signals of the forest, but the language has borrowed a small number of words from the human speech of the surrounding communities. The borrowed words are chiefly the names of the new objects and the new practices that the elven communities have taken up through their contact with the human settlements, and the borrowed words are adapted to the phonology of Quessan by the addition of the vowels that the elven speech requires. The chronicler records that the elven communities do not borrow from the human speech in the opposite direction, and the elven scholars treat the absence of borrowing as a point of pride.

The relation of Quessan to the elven speech of the southern forests and the elven speech of the elven communities beyond the mountains is the subject of some scholarly dispute, and the chronicler has been told by the elven scholars that the differences are matters of dialect rather than of language. The chronicler records that a speaker of Quessan from the forests of Aethelar can make himself understood to a speaker from the southern forests after a short period of adjustment, and the adjustment is chiefly a matter of vocabulary rather than of grammar. The chronicler has been unable to determine the extent of the elven speech beyond the known world, and the elven scholars are said to remark that the question is not one that the elven people are inclined to answer.

## Significance

Quessan is the language of the elven people, the medium of the elven councils, the songs of the elven festivals, and the long conversation of the elven hearths. The language is a true language with a grammar and a vocabulary of its own, and the language is transmitted with a care that the chronicler finds exemplary. The elven people have maintained Quessan through the centuries by the patient practice of the elders and the listening of the young, and the language is the foundation of the elven identity and the elven memory. The chronicler finds in Quessan one of the most stable and the most carefully preserved of the languages of the known world, and the language is a measure of the long attention that the elven people have given to the life of the forest and to the relations of the elven communities with one another.
