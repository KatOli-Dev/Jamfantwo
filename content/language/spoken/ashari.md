## Overview

Ashari is the common tongue of [Ashara](/content/location/natural/continent/ashara), the warm continent of river basins, forested escarpments, and long inland waterways that run toward a broken eastern coast. The language is most closely associated with the human population of the river valleys and the eastern ports, but the chronicler records that Ashari is spoken as a first language by the [lacertian](/content/species/sapient/lacertians) communities of the marshlands and the rivers and as a second language by the [halfling](/content/species/sapient/halflings) communities of the lower terraces and by every merchant who requires a working knowledge of the long river trade. The name is taken from the continent itself, and the language is sometimes called the river speech by the highland communities of the western ranges, who treat it as the medium of the valley commerce rather than as a learned accomplishment.

The origins of Ashari lie in the speech of the earliest human settlements of the river basins, and the language has grown over the centuries through contact with the highland speech of the western ranges, the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) of the ports that face [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) across the warm sea, and the trade tongues that pass through the continent on the long river routes. The chronicler has traced at least three distinct waves of borrowing, each of which has left a layer of vocabulary and a small set of grammatical habits that the river-valley speakers no longer recognise as foreign. The result is a language of considerable fluidity, suited to the description of the waters, the boats, the rice, and the patient business of the long river trade, and capable of being adapted to the specialised vocabularies of the boatman, the fisher, and the counting house of the river ports.

## Sounds

Ashari is spoken in a register that is more flowing and more musical than the common speech of the highland ranges, and the chronicler notes that highland visitors often remark upon the music of the river-valley vowels and the ease with which the river-valley speakers link one syllable to the next. The consonants of Ashari are softly distinguished, the vowels are full and open, and the rhythm of the sentence is carried by the regular accent that falls upon the second or third syllable of most words of more than one syllable. The chronicler records that the river-valley singers use the vowel range to produce the long sustained notes of the water songs, and the songs are one of the most distinctive of the cultural expressions of the river-valley communities. The pitch of the voice is treated by the river-valley speakers as a matter of custom rather than of meaning, and the chronicler has observed that the river-valley speakers tend to speak with a higher pitch than the highland speakers, in unconscious imitation of the singing register of the boats and the festivals.

The script of Ashari is alphabetic, with thirty marks that represent the consonants and vowels of the language. The script is read from left to right in horizontal lines, and the lines are arranged from the top of the page to the bottom. The script is descended from the marks used by the earliest human chroniclers of the river basins, and the chronicler has identified a small number of borrowings from the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) script in the marks used for the sounds that the older river-valley script did not distinguish. The marks are painted or printed upon parchment, paper, or fine cloth, and the writing is taught in the schools of the larger river-valley towns to children of every species who require it for the ordinary business of life. The chronicler has noted that the script of the eastern ports is distinguished by a set of ornamental marks that the port scribes add to the headings of the trade tallies, and the marks are treated by the river-valley speakers as a sign of the dignity of the long trade.

## Vocabulary and Borrowing

The vocabulary of Ashari is rich in words for the waters, the boats, the rice, the fish, and the patient business of the long river trade, and the chronicler records that the language has more words for the kinds of water, the kinds of boat, the kinds of rice, and the stages of the boatman's art than any of the languages of the highland ranges. The vocabulary is also rich in words for the village, the council, and the customary relations of the river-valley communities, for the river-valley populations place great weight upon the distinctions of the clans and the long memory of the river. The chronicler notes that the river-valley compounds are formed by the joining of two or more of the simple words, and the compounds are used in the formal speech of the village councils and the songs of the water festivals.

Ashari has borrowed from the highland speech of the western ranges a set of words for the mountains, the metals, the stones, and the tools of the workshop that the river-valley communities have required for the conduct of the upland trade. The borrowings are recognised by the river-valley speakers as belonging to the trade of the highlander rather than to the craft of the boatman, and the chronicler records that the river-valley speakers are careful to use the highland word in the highland context and the river-valley word in the river-valley context. The language has also borrowed a small number of words from the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) trade tongue through the eastern ports that face the warm sea, and the borrowings are chiefly the names of the southern goods, the southern customs, and the southern offices that the river-valley merchants have required for the conduct of the long-distance trade.

## Speech

Ashari is the language of the great rivers of [Ashara](/content/location/natural/continent/ashara), and the rivers are worked in a register that is patient and precise, suited to the long observation of the currents and the careful timing of the sailing seasons. The river register uses a set of fixed phrases for the opening of a voyage, the inspection of a cargo, and the closing of a landing, and the phrases are known to every boatman of the principal rivers. The chronicler has observed that the phrases are intelligible to children of eight or nine years who have been brought up in the boatman families, and the intelligibility is a measure of the care with which the forms are transmitted from parent to child in the river-valley communities.

Ashari is also the language of the village councils of the river-valley communities, and the councils are conducted in a more formal register that is preserved by the elders of the larger villages and the principal river ports of the eastern coast. The formal register is used for the public announcements of the village magistrates, the reading of the customary law, and the debates of the larger assemblies, and the register is intelligible to any speaker of Ashari who has had the benefit of a customary education in the boat or the council. The formal register borrows a small number of phrases from the older speech of the river basins, and the borrowings are treated by the river-valley speakers as marks of dignity and authority. The chronicler has noted that the formal register is used with care in the councils of the principal villages, and the care is a measure of the importance that the river-valley communities attach to the conduct of public business in a language that the villagers understand and trust.

## Relations

Ashari stands in a complex relation to the languages of the regions that surround [Ashara](/content/location/natural/continent/ashara), and the relations are a measure of the long history of contact and exchange that has shaped the river-valley communities. The chronicler records that Ashari is intelligible, with some adjustment, to speakers of the highland speech of the western ranges, and the intelligibility is greater in the upland towns than in the deeper interior of either tongue. The relationship between Ashari and the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) of the northern ports of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) is the subject of scholarly interest, for the two languages have borrowed a small vocabulary from one another through the long sea routes, and the chronicler has been told by the river-valley scholars that the borrowings are a measure of the long coexistence of the river-valley and the Sahrani merchants at the eastern ports.

The relation of Ashari to the speech of the [lacertian](/content/species/sapient/lacertians) communities of the marshlands and the rivers is the subject of some scholarly dispute, for the two languages have borrowed a small vocabulary from one another over the centuries, and the chronicler has been told by the river-valley scholars that the borrowings are a measure of the long coexistence of the human and the lacertian communities. The chronicler records that the river-valley speakers tend to treat the lizardfolk speech as a learned accomplishment rather than as a working language, and the lizardfolk communities of the marshlands tend to treat Ashari as the necessary medium of the river trade. The relation of Ashari to the halfling speech of the lower terraces is closer in vocabulary than in grammar, and the chronicler has been told by the halfling communities that their speech is a separate language, though the speakers of Ashari are commonly able to follow it after a short period of adjustment.

## Significance

Ashari is the common tongue of [Ashara](/content/location/natural/continent/ashara), the language of the great rivers and the village councils, the medium of the long river trade, and the first or second language of nearly every speaker of every species who has occasion to dwell or to trade among the river basins and the eastern ports of the warm continent. The language is a true language with a grammar and a vocabulary of its own, and it has been refined over the centuries by the long practice of the river-valley boatmen and the patient correction of the village councils. The chronicler finds in Ashari one of the most musical of the languages of the known world, and the language is a measure of the central place that the river basins have held in the boat trade and the village customs of the warm continent since the earliest human settlements were established along the long waterways of the interior. The language is the foundation of the river-valley identity, and the river-valley identity is the foundation of the village community, and the two together are the measure of the long attention that the river-valley communities have given to the conduct of the river in a tongue that the villagers understand and trust.
