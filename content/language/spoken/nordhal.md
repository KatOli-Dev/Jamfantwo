## Overview

Nordhal is the common tongue of the southern coast of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), the northernmost of the great settled landmasses, spoken in the fishing villages, the trading towns, and the sheltered bays that line the cold sea of long swells and shifting fog. The language is most closely associated with the human population of the southern belt, but the chronicler records that Nordhal is spoken as a first language by the [dwarf](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) communities of the lowland foothill settlements and as a second language by the [halfling](/content/species/sapient/halflings) communities of the warmer river margins and by every merchant who requires a working knowledge of the northern sea trade. The name is taken from an old coastal word for the northern home, and the language is sometimes called the southern speech by the tundra communities of the interior, who treat it as the medium of the coast commerce rather than as a learned accomplishment.

The origins of Nordhal lie in the speech of the earliest human fishing communities of the southern shore, and the language has grown over the centuries through contact with the dwarven speech of the [Whitewall Range](/content/location/natural/feature/whitewall-range) foothills, the halfling speech of the river margins, and the trade tongues that arrive by sea from [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) across the cold northern waters. The chronicler has traced two distinct waves of borrowing, each of which has left a layer of vocabulary and a small set of grammatical habits that the coastal speakers no longer recognise as foreign. The result is a language of considerable directness, suited to the description of the sea, the weather, the fish, the timber, and the patient business of the northern harbours, and capable of being adapted to the specialised vocabularies of the boat, the net, and the counting house of the southern towns.

## Sounds

Nordhal is spoken in a register that is sharper and more clipped than the common speech of the central continents, and the chronicler notes that southern visitors often remark upon the brevity of the coastal sentences and the willingness of the coastal speakers to leave unsaid what the southern custom would require to be set out at length. The consonants of Nordhal are numerous, and the language distinguishes a number of sounds that the southern speech has merged, particularly in the cluster of unvoiced stops that occur at the beginning of many coastal words. The vowels are fewer than in the southern speech, and the chronicler records that the coastal singers use the consonantal range to produce the striking rhythmic effects that mark the songs of the fishing fleets, and the songs are one of the most distinctive of the cultural expressions of the southern communities. The pitch of the voice is treated by the coastal speakers as a matter of custom rather than of meaning, and the chronicler has observed that the coastal speakers tend to speak with a flatter pitch than the tundra speakers, in unconscious imitation of the working register of the boat and the net.

The script of Nordhal is runic, with twenty-four marks that represent not single sounds but the consonant clusters and vowel groups that the chronicler has identified as the basic units of the spoken language. The script is read from left to right in horizontal lines, and the lines are arranged from the top of the page to the bottom. The script is descended from the marks that the earliest human chroniclers of the southern coast cut into the soft wood of the drift logs that the cold sea casts upon the shore, and the chronicler has identified a small number of borrowings from the dwarven script of the foothills in the marks used for the sounds that the older coastal script did not distinguish. The marks are incised into wood, bone, or stone for the records of the coastal communities, and painted or printed upon parchment for the correspondence of the larger towns, and the writing is taught in the schools of the principal harbours to children of every species who require it for the ordinary business of life.

## Vocabulary and Borrowing

The vocabulary of Nordhal is rich in words for the sea, the weather, the fish, the boats, the timber, and the patient business of the northern harbours, and the chronicler records that the language has more words for the kinds of fish, the kinds of weather, the kinds of timber, and the stages of the boat-builder's art than any of the languages of the central continents. The vocabulary is also rich in words for the family, the household, and the customary relations of the fishing village, for the coastal communities place great weight upon the succession of the boats and the long memory of the harbour. The chronicler notes that the coastal compounds are formed by the joining of two or more of the simple words, and the compounds are used in the formal speech of the harbour councils and the songs of the winter festivals.

Nordhal has borrowed from the dwarven speech of the [Whitewall Range](/content/location/natural/feature/whitewall-range) foothills a set of words for the metals, the stones, and the tools of the workshop that the coastal communities have required for the repair of the boats and the construction of the harbour works. The borrowings are recognised by the coastal speakers as belonging to the trade of the smith rather than to the craft of the fisherman, and the chronicler records that the coastal speakers are careful to use the foothill word in the foothill context and the coastal word in the coastal context. The language has also borrowed a small number of words from the [Morvindi](/content/language/spoken/morvindi) trade tongue through the ships that cross the cold northern waters, and the borrowings are chiefly the names of the southern goods, the southern customs, and the southern offices that the coastal merchants have required for the conduct of the long-distance trade.

## Speech

Nordhal is the language of the great harbours of the southern coast, and the harbours are conducted in a register that is brisk and practical, suited to the rapid discharge of the boats and the negotiation of the prices of the catch. The harbour register uses a set of fixed phrases for the opening of a bargain, the testing of a cargo, and the closing of a sale, and the phrases are known to every merchant of the principal harbours. The chronicler has observed that the phrases are intelligible to children of ten or eleven years who have been brought up in the harbour towns, and the intelligibility is a measure of the care with which the forms are transmitted from parent to child in the fishing families of the coast.

Nordhal is also the language of the long-night councils of the coastal communities, and the councils are conducted in a more formal register that is preserved by the elders of the larger towns and the principal fishing villages. The formal register is used for the public announcements of the village magistrates, the reading of the customary law, and the debates of the larger assemblies, and the register is intelligible to any speaker of Nordhal who has had the benefit of a customary education in the boat or the counting house. The formal register borrows a small number of phrases from the older speech of the southern coast, and the borrowings are treated by the coastal speakers as marks of dignity and authority. The chronicler has noted that the formal register is used with care in the long-night councils of the principal towns, and the care is a measure of the importance that the coastal communities attach to the conduct of public business in a language that the fishermen and the merchants understand and trust.

## Relations

Nordhal stands in a complex relation to the languages of the regions that surround the southern coast of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), and the relations are a measure of the long history of contact and exchange that has shaped the coastal communities. The chronicler records that Nordhal is intelligible, with some adjustment, to speakers of the tundra [Frostic](/content/language/spoken/frostic) of the interior, and the intelligibility is greater in the foothill towns than in the deeper interior of either tongue. The relationship between Nordhal and the [Morvindi](/content/language/spoken/morvindi) of the coast of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) is more distant, for the two languages have had less contact over the centuries, and the speakers of the one are not commonly able to follow the speech of the other without a period of study, though the chronicler has noted that the merchants of the principal harbours are commonly able to make themselves understood after a few weeks at sea.

The relation of Nordhal to the dwarven speech of the foothills is the subject of some scholarly interest, for the two languages have borrowed a small vocabulary from one another over the centuries, and the chronicler has been told by the coastal scholars that the borrowings are a measure of the long coexistence of the coastal and the foothill communities. The chronicler records that the coastal speakers tend to treat the dwarven speech as a learned accomplishment rather than as a working language, and the dwarven communities of the foothills tend to treat Nordhal as the necessary medium of the harbour trade. The relation of Nordhal to the halfling speech of the warmer river margins is closer in vocabulary than in grammar, and the chronicler has been told by the halfling communities that their speech is a separate language, though the speakers of Nordhal are commonly able to follow it after a short period of adjustment.

## Significance

Nordhal is the common tongue of the southern coast of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), the language of the great harbours and the long-night councils, the medium of the northern sea trade, and the first or second language of nearly every speaker of every species who has occasion to dwell or to trade among the fishing villages and the trading towns of the southern belt. The language is a true language with a grammar and a vocabulary of its own, and it has been refined over the centuries by the long practice of the coastal fishermen and the patient correction of the schools. The chronicler finds in Nordhal one of the most direct of the languages of the known world, and the language is a measure of the central place that the southern coast has held in the sea trade and the fishing customs of the northern continent since the earliest human settlements were established along the cold shore. The language is the foundation of the coastal identity, and the coastal identity is the foundation of the harbour community, and the two together are the measure of the long attention that the coastal communities have given to the conduct of the harbour in a tongue that the people understand and trust.
