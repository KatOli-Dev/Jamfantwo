## The Law of the River

The Vael Water Law is the body of customary and written law that governs the management of the [River Vael](/content/location/natural/feature/river-vael) and its tributaries, the construction and maintenance of dykes, channels, sluices, and flood defences, and the rights and obligations of landowners, communities, and the crown in the use and control of water. The water law is the oldest continuous legal institution of the Kingdom of [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), older than the monarchy itself, for its origins lie in the customs of the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale) that were first codified by [Sera](/content/people/historical/sera-ambervale) when she united the river principalities into a single kingdom.

The water law is founded upon a principle that distinguishes it from the legal systems of most other kingdoms: the principle that water is a common resource whose management is a collective responsibility, not a private commodity whose use is a matter of individual right. Every landowner whose property borders the river or a watercourse has the right to draw water for domestic and agricultural use, but no landowner may obstruct the flow of water, divert a watercourse, or alter the system of dykes and channels without the approval of the water commissioners appointed by the crown. The principle of collective management is enforced by the water courts, which have the power to order the removal of obstructions, the repair of dykes, and the payment of compensation for damage caused by the improper management of water.

## The Origins of the Water Law in the Amber Vale

The origins of the Vael Water Law lie in the practical necessities of agriculture in the Amber Vale, where the annual flood of the River Vael deposits the silt that renews the fertility of the soil but also threatens the fields and settlements along the riverbanks. The farmers of the vale learned over many generations that the flood could not be prevented but could be managed, and that the management of the flood required collective action that transcended the boundaries of individual holdings. The first water customs were agreements among neighbouring landowners to maintain shared dykes and drainage channels, and these agreements were recorded in the oral traditions that were the foundation of the legal order of the vale.

Sera, who united the river principalities in the early centuries of the kingdom's history, recognised that the management of the river was the foundation of the prosperity and stability of the realm. She caused the water customs of the Amber Vale to be written down and extended to the whole of the river valley, and she established the system of water commissioners and water courts that continues to administer the law to the present day. The water ordinances of Sera are preserved in the old coronation hall in the Amber Vale, where they are read aloud at the [Crown Harvest](/content/culture/crown-harvest) each year, and they are regarded as the foundation of the legal order of the kingdom.

## The System of Dykes and Channels

The water law establishes a comprehensive system for the construction and maintenance of the dykes, channels, sluices, and other works that manage the flow of the river and protect the agricultural land of the valley. The system is administered by the water commissioners, who are appointed by the crown and who serve for terms specified by royal decree. The commissioners are responsible for the inspection of the water works, the enforcement of the water law, and the resolution of disputes arising from the management of water.

The water law divides the river valley into districts, each of which is responsible for the maintenance of a specified reach of the river and its water works. Each district is governed by a water council composed of representatives of the landowners and communities of the district, and the water council is responsible for the allocation of labour and resources for the maintenance of the dykes and channels. The water councils are the institutions through which the collective responsibility for water management is exercised, and their decisions are binding on all landowners and communities within the district.

The water law specifies the dimensions and construction standards for dykes and channels, the schedules for inspection and maintenance, and the procedures to be followed in the event of a breach or failure. The law requires that each district maintain a store of timber, stone, and other materials sufficient to repair damage to the water works, and that each district maintain a roster of workers who can be called upon in an emergency. The law also requires that the water commissioners be notified immediately of any damage to the water works, and it imposes penalties on those who fail to notify the commissioners or who fail to carry out the repairs ordered by the commissioners.

## Rights of Draw and Riparian Obligations

The water law establishes the rights of landowners and communities to draw water from the river and its tributaries for domestic, agricultural, and industrial use. The right to draw water is governed by the principle of reasonable use: every landowner whose property borders a watercourse has the right to draw water for domestic and agricultural use, but no landowner may draw so much water as to deprive downstream users of the water they need for their own reasonable use. The principle of reasonable use is enforced by the water commissioners, who may limit the amount of water drawn by any landowner if the limitation is necessary to protect the interests of other users.

The water law also establishes the obligations of landowners whose property borders a watercourse. Every riparian landowner is required to maintain the portion of the watercourse that borders the property, including the banks and the bed, and to keep the watercourse free of obstructions that might impede the flow of water. A landowner who fails to maintain the watercourse or who obstructs the flow of water is liable to the water commissioners and to any other landowner or community that suffers damage as a result of the obstruction.

The water law also governs the construction of mills, weirs, and other structures that use the flow of water for industrial purposes. The construction of a mill or weir requires the approval of the water commissioners, who must be satisfied that the structure will not interfere with the flow of water to the detriment of downstream users or obstruct the passage of fish in the river. The approval may be conditional on the construction of fish passes or other measures to mitigate the impact of the structure.

## The Water Courts

The water courts are the institutions through which the water law is enforced and disputes arising from the management of water are resolved. The water courts are distinct from the ordinary courts of the kingdom and are presided over by judges who have specialised knowledge of water management and the water law. The judges of the water courts are appointed by the crown from among the water commissioners and senior officials of the realm, and they serve for terms specified by royal decree.

The water courts have jurisdiction over disputes arising from the management of water, including disputes over the right to draw water, the maintenance of dykes and channels, the construction of mills and weirs, and the liability for damage caused by the improper management of water. The water courts also have jurisdiction over disputes between water districts concerning the allocation of water and the sharing of the costs of maintaining the water works.

The procedure of the water courts is designed to be swift and efficient, for the needs of agriculture require that disputes over water be resolved promptly. The water courts sit at regular intervals in each district of the valley, and they may also sit in special session to address emergencies such as the breach of a dyke or the failure of a sluice. The decisions of the water courts are binding on the parties and may be enforced by the water commissioners, who have the power to order the removal of obstructions, the repair of damage, and the payment of compensation.

## The Crown's Role in Water Management

The crown of Vael plays a central role in the management of water under the water law. The king is the ultimate authority over the water works, and the water commissioners serve at the king's pleasure. The king has the power to issue decrees concerning the management of water, to override the decisions of the water commissioners in matters of urgent necessity, and to allocate resources from the royal treasury for the construction and maintenance of the water works.

The crown's role in water management is exercised through the Office of the Royal Water Commissioner, a department of the royal administration that oversees the water law and the water courts. The Royal Water Commissioner is appointed by the king and is responsible for the inspection of the water works, the supervision of the district water councils, and the enforcement of the water law. The Royal Water Commissioner reports directly to the king and may recommend changes to the water law or the water works that are necessary to protect the interests of the realm.

The crown also maintains stores of grain in the royal granaries at [Vaelmouth](/content/location/settlement/city/vaelmouth) that may be released to support communities affected by flooding or by the failure of the water works. The king may also order the suspension of taxes or the provision of emergency labour to districts that have suffered damage to their water works, and the crown's capacity to respond to emergencies is a significant factor in the stability of the agricultural economy of the valley.

## The Water Law and the Wider Legal Order

The water law of Vael is not a static body of rules but a living tradition that evolves in response to changing conditions and new challenges. The water commissioners and the water courts apply the principles of the water law to new situations as they arise, and their decisions are recorded and preserved as precedents that guide future decisions. The water law is thus a common law, developed through the accumulation of decisions and the application of principles to particular cases, and its flexibility is one of the sources of its enduring effectiveness.

The water law has influenced the development of legal systems beyond the borders of Vael. The principles of collective management and riparian obligation that are the foundation of the water law have been adopted or adapted by other kingdoms that face similar challenges in the management of water resources. The water courts of Vael have served as a model for the establishment of similar institutions in other river valleys, and the decisions of the Vael water commissioners are cited as authority in legal proceedings concerning water rights throughout Aethelar.

The water law is also the foundation of the relationship between the crown and the landowners and communities of the valley. The water law is a constant presence in the life of every farmer and landholder in Vael, governing the most fundamental aspects of the agricultural year: when the fields may be irrigated, how the dykes must be maintained, and what obligations are owed to the downstream neighbours whose land depends upon the same water. The water law is thus not merely a body of legal rules but an expression of the collective identity of the people of the valley, a reminder that their prosperity depends upon their capacity to manage the river that is the source of their wealth.

The enduring significance of the Vael Water Law lies in its demonstration that the management of a shared resource can be achieved through collective action and legal regulation, without resort to the private ownership of water or the unrestricted exercise of individual rights. The water law represents a different conception of the relationship between the individual and the community, one in which the needs of the community take precedence over the claims of the individual in matters that affect the common welfare. This conception has sustained the agricultural prosperity of the Vael valley for centuries, and it continues to guide the management of the river and the water works that depend upon it.
