## The Foundation of Eastern Commerce

The Aldreth Commercial Code is the body of written law that governs contracts, insurance, maritime commerce, and commercial practice within the Kingdom of [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and in those territories that have adopted its provisions by treaty or custom. The code is not a single document but a comprehensive collection of statutes, royal decrees, judicial decisions, and scholarly commentaries that have accumulated over the centuries of Aldreth's existence as a commercial power. It is the most influential body of commercial law in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and its principles have been adopted or adapted by kingdoms, leagues, and city-states across the known world.

The code originated in the customs of the merchant houses of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth), the commercial practices that developed among the traders who made the city the wealthiest port on the continent. These customs were first written down and given the force of law by royal decree in the early centuries of the kingdom, and they have been revised and expanded by successive kings as the needs of commerce have grown and changed. The royal chancery maintains the official text of the code, and additions to the code require the king's assent. The code is divided into books, each dealing with a distinct area of commercial law, and the books are further divided into chapters and sections that organise the provisions by topic.

## Book One: Contracts and Obligations

The first book of the code governs the formation, performance, and enforcement of contracts. The code defines a contract as an agreement between competent parties that creates an obligation enforceable by law. The essential elements of a valid contract are the offer, the acceptance, the consideration, and the intention to create legal relations. The code requires no particular form for the validity of a contract, though certain contracts must be in writing to be enforceable: contracts for the sale of land, contracts for the carriage of goods by sea, contracts of insurance, and contracts for the loan of money above a specified amount.

The code distinguishes between contracts that are performed immediately and contracts that are performed over time. Contracts for the sale of goods are governed by the principle that delivery and payment are concurrent conditions: the seller must deliver the goods when the buyer tenders the price, and the buyer must pay the price when the seller tenders the goods. The code provides detailed rules for determining the time and place of delivery, the passing of risk, and the remedies available to a party whose expectations have been disappointed by the other party's breach.

The code recognises the concept of warranty, an undertaking by the seller that the goods sold are of a specified quality or suitability. A seller who sells goods that do not meet the warranty is liable to the buyer for the difference between the value of the goods as delivered and the value they would have had if they had met the warranty. The code also recognises the concept of force majeure, events beyond the control of the parties that excuse performance of a contract. A party whose performance is prevented by an act of the gods, by war, by the decree of a sovereign, or by a natural disaster that could not reasonably have been foreseen is discharged from the obligation to perform and is not liable for damages.

## Book Two: Insurance and Risk

The second book of the code governs insurance, the practice by which a merchant transfers the risk of loss to an insurer in return for the payment of a premium. Insurance is the foundation of long-distance commerce in the known world, for the dangers of the sea and the road are such that no merchant can bear the risk of a single voyage alone. The code recognises two principal forms of insurance: marine insurance, which covers the loss of a vessel or its cargo during a voyage, and overland insurance, which covers the loss of goods during carriage by road.

The contract of marine insurance is governed by the principle of utmost good faith. The merchant seeking insurance must disclose to the insurer every fact material to the risk, including the condition of the vessel, the nature of the cargo, the route to be taken, and the season of the intended voyage. A failure to disclose a material fact renders the contract voidable at the option of the insurer. The insurer who issues a policy of marine insurance undertakes to indemnify the merchant against losses caused by perils of the sea, including storms, shipwreck, collision, piracy, and fire. Losses caused by the negligence of the master or crew are covered unless the negligence amounts to deliberate wrongdoing.

The code establishes the principle of general average, by which the losses incurred for the common safety of a vessel and its cargo are shared proportionally among all parties with an interest in the voyage. If the master of a vessel orders cargo to be jettisoned to lighten the ship in a storm, the owners of the cargo that is saved contribute to the value of the cargo that is lost, each paying in proportion to the value of their own cargo. The principle of general average is one of the oldest and most widely recognised principles of maritime law in the known world, and its inclusion in the Aldreth Commercial Code has contributed to its adoption in the legal systems of other maritime powers.

## Book Three: Maritime Commerce

The third book of the code governs the conduct of maritime commerce, including the charter of vessels, the carriage of goods by sea, and the rights and obligations of masters, crews, and passengers. The code defines the charter party, the contract by which the owner of a vessel hires the vessel to a merchant for a specified voyage or period of time. The charter party must specify the vessel, the voyage or period of hire, the freight to be paid, and the obligations of the parties with respect to loading, unloading, and the provision of provisions and crew.

The code regulates the rights and obligations of the master of a vessel, who is the representative of the owner at sea. The master has the authority to make decisions necessary for the safety of the vessel and the successful completion of the voyage, including the authority to jettison cargo in an emergency, to deviate from the planned route to avoid danger, and to incur necessary expenses for repairs and provisions. The master is liable to the owner for losses caused by negligence or misconduct, but is not liable for losses caused by perils of the sea or by acts of the gods.

The code also regulates the carriage of goods by sea, establishing the obligations of the carrier with respect to the loading, stowage, carriage, and discharge of goods. The carrier must exercise due diligence to make the vessel seaworthy before the voyage, to properly equip and crew the vessel, and to carry the goods to their destination without unnecessary delay. The carrier is liable for loss or damage to the goods caused by the carrier's negligence, but is not liable for losses caused by perils of the sea, acts of the gods, or the inherent vice of the goods themselves.

## Book Four: Bills of Exchange and Credit

The fourth book of the code governs bills of exchange, promissory notes, and other instruments of credit that are the foundation of commercial finance. A bill of exchange is a written order by which one person directs another to pay a specified sum of money to a third person. The code establishes the requirements for a valid bill of exchange, the rights and obligations of the parties, and the remedies available to a holder who is not paid when the bill falls due.

The code recognises the concept of negotiability, by which a bill of exchange may be transferred from one holder to another by endorsement. The holder of a negotiable bill of exchange who takes it in good faith and for value acquires the right to payment free from any defences that the drawer or acceptor might have against the original payee. The negotiability of bills of exchange is the foundation of commercial credit in the known world, for it allows merchants to transfer value across long distances without the risk and inconvenience of transporting coin or bullion.

The code also governs the practice of banking, regulating the rights and obligations of bankers and their customers. The code requires bankers to maintain records of their transactions and to account to their customers for the funds entrusted to their care. The code prohibits bankers from lending money at rates of interest exceeding the maximum established by royal decree, and it provides for the forfeiture of loans made at usurious rates.

## Book Five: Commercial Procedure and Courts

The fifth book of the code governs the procedure of the commercial courts of Aldreth and the enforcement of commercial judgments. The commercial courts are distinct from the ordinary courts of the kingdom and are presided over by judges appointed from among the senior merchants and legal scholars of the realm. The procedure of the commercial courts is designed to be swift and efficient, for the needs of commerce require that disputes be resolved without the delays that characterise ordinary litigation.

The commercial courts have jurisdiction over disputes arising from matters governed by the code, including disputes over contracts, insurance, bills of exchange, and the carriage of goods. The courts also have jurisdiction over disputes between merchants arising from any commercial transaction, whether or not the transaction is governed by a specific provision of the code. The courts apply the code, the customs of the merchant community, and principles of equity and good conscience in resolving disputes.

The code requires commercial disputes to be submitted to arbitration before they may be brought to court. The arbitration is conducted by a panel of three merchants chosen by the parties, and the decision of the arbitrators is binding on the parties and may be enforced by the commercial courts. The requirement of arbitration reflects the principle that merchants are best qualified to resolve disputes arising from commercial transactions, and that the interests of commerce are better served by swift resolution than by prolonged litigation.

## Book Six: Enforcement and Penalties

The sixth book of the code governs the enforcement of commercial obligations and the penalties for violations of the code. The code provides for the seizure and sale of the goods of a debtor who fails to pay a debt that has been established by a judgment of a commercial court. The code also provides for the imprisonment of a debtor who is found to have concealed assets or to have acted fraudulently in incurring the debt.

The code provides for sanctions against merchants who violate its provisions, including fines, suspension of trading privileges, and exclusion from the markets of Aldreth. A merchant who is found to have committed fraud in a commercial transaction may be barred from trading in the kingdom, and the name of such a merchant is recorded in the register of dishonoured traders maintained by the guild of merchants. The social and economic consequences of exclusion from the register are such that the threat of registration is a powerful deterrent to misconduct.

The code also provides for the punishment of those who commit perjury in commercial proceedings, who forge bills of exchange or other commercial documents, or who conspire to defraud merchants or insurers. The penalties for such offences include fines, imprisonment, and, in the most serious cases, the forfeiture of the offender's goods and permanent exclusion from commercial life in the kingdom.

## The Influence of the Code Beyond Aldreth

The influence of the Aldreth Commercial Code extends far beyond the borders of the kingdom. The code has been adopted or adapted by the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league), the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league), and many of the independent city-states of the eastern coast. The principles of the code are cited by the merchant courts of these leagues and cities, and the judgments of the Aldreth commercial courts are enforced by agreement in many of the ports with which the kingdom trades.

The code has also influenced the development of commercial law in the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic), where the spice trade has generated its own body of commercial law that draws upon the principles of the Aldreth code. The merchant courts of [Thessaran](/content/law/thessaran-merchant-courts) apply provisions derived from the Aldreth code in cases involving contracts, insurance, and bills of exchange, and the influence of the code is apparent in the commercial legislation of many of the kingdoms and leagues of Aethelar.

The enduring significance of the Aldreth Commercial Code lies not only in its provisions but in its method. The code represents the principle that commercial law should be systematic, accessible, and responsive to the needs of commerce. The kings and jurists who developed the code understood that the prosperity of the kingdom depends upon the certainty and efficiency of the legal framework within which commerce is conducted, and they created a body of law that has served as a model for commercial legislation across the known world. The code continues to evolve as new forms of commerce develop and new challenges arise, and its adaptability is the foundation of its enduring relevance.
