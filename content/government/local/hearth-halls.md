## The Houses of Worship

The Hearth-Halls are the houses of worship of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the plain structures of stone and timber in which the central fire of the faith is kept burning without ceasing. The hearth-halls are built to hold a central fire that is tended by the hearth-keepers, and the halls lack the towers or ornamentation favoured by some other traditions, for the faith teaches that the Flame needs no adornment to be honoured. The chronicler notes that the hearth-halls are regarded by the faithful as the most visible sign of the unity of the faith, and the halls are held in high esteem by every household that keeps the morning kindling and the evening banking.

The hearth-halls are found throughout the settled [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) populations of the temperate river valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the halls are also known in scattered form along the eastern coasts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), though there they contend with older and more deeply rooted traditions. The chronicler notes that the greatest of the hearth-halls stand in the older river cities of Aethelar, and the most ancient is said to lie in the heart of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), though the faithful dispute which hall may claim true precedence, and the question is of little moment to the practice of the faith. Travellers report hearth-halls of varying size in the towns of the southern plains and along the eastern [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast, each maintained by its local keepers according to the same plain discipline.

## Origins among the First Hearths

The hearth-halls trace their foundation to the hearth-keepers who travelled between the river valleys of central Aethelar teaching that the flame upon the hearth and the warmth of the body and the light of the sun were all of one nature. The earliest certain records of the Still Flame are found among the chronicles of the river valleys, where the faith appears already well established by the time the first great human kingdoms arose, and the hearth-halls were already in use by the time the chronicles begin. The chronicler notes that whether the first hearth-halls were erected by a single line of hearth-keepers or by a succession of teachers is disputed, and the faithful themselves are content to leave the question open, holding that the truth of the Flame does not depend upon the name of its first messenger.

The first hearth-halls were modest structures of stone and timber, and the halls were built to hold a central fire that was kept burning through the day and the night. The community's successors added larger halls and then greater halls, and the hearth-halls grew in the measure of the number of households that kept the morning kindling. The faith spread along the river systems of Aethelar and into the coastal plains, carried by settlers and by the steady movement of peoples rather than by conquest, and each new town that received the teaching erected its own hearth-hall. The chronicler notes that the hearth-halls of the faith are therefore many, though each hall is modelled upon the plain discipline of the first, and each hall is governed by the same customs that the first communities established.

## The Central Fire

The central fire of the hearth-hall is the heart of the building, and the fire is kept burning without ceasing by the hearth-keepers who serve the hall. The fire is maintained in a broad hearth of stone that occupies the centre of the principal chamber, and the fire is fed with the wood that the keepers gather from the surrounding country. The chronicler notes that the fire is the most visible sign of the faith, and the fire is the source of the warmth that the hall extends to those who come to it. The flame is regarded by the faithful as a fragment of the one Flame that sustains the world, and the tending of the flame is regarded as the highest duty of the hearth-keepers.

The fire is lit at the founding of the hall and is kept burning through every season and every year, and the keepers are required to ensure that the fire is never let to die. The chronicler notes that the keeping of the fire is a discipline of constant attention, and the keepers are trained to feed the fire at the proper intervals and to bank the fire at the proper times of day. A keeper who allows the fire to die is dismissed from the service, and the dismissal is the measure of the importance that the faith attaches to the keeping of the flame.

## The Hearth-Keepers

The hearth-halls are served by the hearth-keepers, the men and the women who are charged with the tending of the central fire and the instruction of the young. The hearth-keepers are appointed by the elders of the community that the hall serves, and the hearth-keepers undergo a longer training in the keeping of the great flames of the houses of worship. The chronicler notes that the hearth-keepers hold no worldly authority and are expected to labour with their hands as do other folk, and the hearth-keepers are counseled against the temptation to mere governance.

The hearth-keepers are responsible for the daily tending of the fire, for the instruction of the young in the doctrine of the Still Flame, and for the maintenance of the records of the hall. The chronicler notes that the hearth-keepers are the principal point of contact between the hall and the wider community, and the hearth-keepers are the measure of the hall's reputation for hospitality. The hearth-keepers are also responsible for the keeping of the store of fuel and food that the hall maintains against the winter, and the keeping of the store is among the foremost responsibilities of the office.

## Rites and the Hearth

The hearth-halls are the seat of the principal rites of the faith, and the rites are conducted at the central fire. The faithful gather at the halls upon the turning of the seasons, and a single great flame is maintained through the observance and portions of it are borne home by the gathered to relight their own hearths. The chronicler notes that the gathering at the turning of the seasons is regarded by the faith as the chief communal observance, and the gathering is the measure of the community's care for the flame.

Marriage, birth, and death are each marked by a rite of the flame. A child is presented to the light within days of birth, and a marriage is solemnised before the shared hearth of the two households. At death, the body is given to the fire in the manner of the region, and the ashes are kept or scattered according to local custom, the act understood as the returning of the individual flame to its source. The chronicler notes that the rites of the flame are regarded by the faith as the second of the principal observances of the hall, and the rites are the measure of the community's continuity.

## The Hall as Refuge

The hearth-halls serve also as places of refuge in hard weather and as centres of the duty of hospitality, for the faith holds that none who come to a flame in need should be turned away. It is common for the halls to keep a store of fuel and food against the winter, and the keeping of this store is among the foremost responsibilities of the keepers. In this practical charity the Still Flame finds its widest expression, and many who hold to other beliefs nonetheless speak well of the hearth-halls and the warmth they extend without condition of creed. The chronicler notes that the duty of hospitality is regarded by the faith as the third of the principal observances of the hall, and the duty is the measure of the hall's reputation among the travellers of the road.

The hall is open to any who seek shelter from a storm or from a season of want, and the hearth-keepers are required to extend the warmth of the fire to all who come. The chronicler notes that the shelter of the hall is a duty that the keepers discharge without exception, and the discharge of the duty is the measure of the hall's reputation in the surrounding country. The hall is regarded by the faithful as a worthy extension of the household hearth, and the hall is held in greater esteem than any of the meeting-places of other traditions.

## The Architecture of the Hall

The hearth-hall is a plain structure of stone and timber, lacking the towers or the ornamentation that other traditions have favoured. The hall is built to hold the central fire and to shelter the community that gathers for the rites, and the hall is constructed with a single eye to the practical needs of the faith. The chronicler notes that the architecture of the hall is regarded by the faith as a measure of the faith's plainness, and the architecture is held to be one of the strengths of the tradition.

The principal chamber of the hall is a single room of considerable size, and the room is dominated by the broad hearth of stone that occupies the centre. The chamber is lit by windows that are placed high in the walls, and the chamber is warmed by the central fire and by the smaller fires that are kindled at the sides. The chronicler notes that the chamber is the seat of the principal rites of the faith, and the chamber is the place to which every faithful household returns at the turning of the seasons. The smaller chambers of the hall include the meeting-rooms of the keepers, the store-rooms of the fuel and the food, and the chambers of the travellers who seek shelter from the storm.

## Relation to Other Halls

The hearth-halls maintain a correspondence with the halls of the kindred traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the correspondence is the means by which the faith has preserved its kinship with the [Shield and Song](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/shield-and-song) and the other contemplative orders of the continent. A hearth-keeper of the Still Flame will sometimes attend the evening review of a house of memory, and a singer of the order will sometimes attend the morning kindling of a hearth-hall, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. The chronicler notes that the correspondence is the measure of the faith's openness to the wider world, and the faith regards the correspondence as a privilege rather than a claim.

The faith has been especially close to the halls of the [Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/unwritten-name), whose masters teach a kindred humility before the single light, and the two traditions have exchanged senior readers more than once in recent generations. The chronicler notes that the correspondence is preserved in the records of each hall, and the correspondence is the source of the faith's knowledge of the wider world. The hearth-keepers caution against the temptation to make a display of the correspondence, for the faith regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very ornament the faith counsels against in the tending of the flame.

## Significance

The Hearth-Halls are the houses of worship of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), and the halls are the seat of the central fire, the hearth-keepers, the rites of the flame, and the duty of hospitality. The halls are plain structures of stone and timber in the river towns of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and in scattered form along the eastern coasts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and the halls are supported by the labour of the keepers and by the small offerings of the faithful. The chronicler notes that the halls have been the source of the faith's slow growth along the river systems of the temperate world, and the halls are a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the temples of other traditions. The faith asks little and promises little beyond the steady return of the flame each morning, and it is perhaps for this reason that it has outlasted grander and more demanding creeds.
