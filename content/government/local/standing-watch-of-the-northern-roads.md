## Overview

The [Standing Watch](/content/government/local/standing-watch) of the Northern Roads is the standing patrol maintained by the cities and the towns of the cold north to keep the great roads safe for the merchants, the pilgrims, and the king's messengers. The Watch is composed of the mounted patrols that ride the roads between the principal towns, the foot patrols that guard the bridges and the fords, and the small garrisons that hold the watch-towers along the principal routes. The Watch is answerable to the magistrates of the towns through which the roads pass, and the magistrates appoint the captains of the Watch from among the household knights and the trusted men-at-arms of the region. The Watch is sustained by a small toll that is collected at the gates of the principal towns and by the fines that the Watch imposes upon the offenders whom it arrests upon the roads.

The Watch is one of the older of the local bodies of the cold north, and its foundation is ascribed to the accord that the towns of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) and the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) made with the king of the realm more than two centuries ago. The accord established the patrol of the roads, the rules of the arrest, and the procedures by which the disputes are adjudicated. The accord has been revised from time to time, and the revisions have been entered in the register of the Watch and confirmed by the magistrates of the principal towns.

## Composition and Officers

The Watch is composed of a captain, two lieutenants, a clerk, and the sworn riders, footmen, and garrisons that patrol the roads. The captain is appointed by the magistrates of the principal towns for a term of three years, and the captain must be a knight of good repute or a man-at-arms of at least ten years' standing. The lieutenants are appointed by the captain and represent the mounted patrol and the foot patrol. The clerk is appointed by the captain and holds office at the pleasure of the Watch, and the clerk is responsible for the registers, the correspondence, and the accounts of the Watch.

The sworn riders are the mounted patrol, and each rider is armed with a sword, a dagger, a short bow, and a horn for the signal. The sworn footmen are the foot patrol, and each footman is armed with a bill, a short sword, and a horn. The garrisons are the small bodies of men-at-arms that hold the watch-towers, and each garrison consists of a sergeant and four men. The chronicler notes that the sworn riders and the sworn footmen are drawn from the households of the towns and from the villages along the roads, and several captains have been born to no greater station than a sworn rider of the northern patrol.

## Duties upon the Roads

The Watch keeps the roads free of the bandits, the wreckers, and the outlaws who would prey upon the merchants and the pilgrims. The mounted patrol rides the roads in pairs, one rider upon the road and one rider upon the flank, and the riders keep a steady watch for any sign of disturbance. The foot patrol guards the bridges, the fords, and the narrow passes, and the footmen are stationed at the points where the road is most easily blocked. The garrisons hold the watch-towers and signal the approach of any large body of men, and the garrisons also shelter the travellers who are caught upon the roads in time of storm.

The Watch has the power to arrest any person who is found in the act of robbery or of violence upon the roads, and the Watch has the power to bring the offender before the magistrate of the nearest town. The Watch also has the power to fine any traveller who violates the rules of the road: a rider who will not give way to a footman, a merchant who overloads his cart and breaks the road, a pilgrim who refuses to show his staff. The chronicler notes that the rules have been the subject of long debate in the Watch, and the rules have been revised from time to time to reflect the seasons and the traffic.

## The Watch-Towers

The Watch maintains a chain of watch-towers along the principal roads, and the towers are placed at intervals of a day's ride. The tower is a stone building of two stories, with a stout door and a stout roof, and the tower is built upon a rise of ground that commands a wide view of the road in both directions. The tower contains a small hearth, a small store of provisions, and the arms of the garrison. The chronicler notes that the towers have been the foundation of the safety of the roads, and the towers have been the refuge of many a traveller who has been caught upon the road in time of storm.

The garrison of a tower consists of a sergeant and four men, and the garrison holds the tower for a tour of two months. The sergeant is responsible for the maintenance of the tower, for the signals that are exchanged with the neighbouring towers, and for the register of the travellers who shelter in the tower. The chronicler notes that the sergeant has a duty to keep the register carefully, for the register is the evidence by which the magistrates judge the state of the roads in time of dispute. The register is sent to the clerk of the Watch at the end of every month, and the clerk enters the entries in the general register of the Watch.

## The Tolls and the Fines

The Watch is sustained by a small toll that is collected at the gates of the principal towns, and the toll is paid by every merchant who enters or leaves the town with a laden cart. The toll is set by the magistrates of the towns in consultation with the captain of the Watch, and the toll is collected by the gatekeepers of the towns. The toll pays for the wages of the riders, the footmen, and the garrisons, and the toll pays for the maintenance of the towers and the arms. The chronicler notes that the toll has been stable for several generations, and the toll is one of the most carefully audited of the public revenues of the region.

The Watch also keeps its own accounts of the fines, and the fines are paid to the clerk at the principal office of the Watch. The fines are used for the relief of the travellers who have been robbed upon the roads and for the maintenance of the towers that are not otherwise provided for. The chronicler notes that the fines have been small in most years, and the chronicler observes that the smallness of the fines is the measure of the success of the Watch in keeping the roads safe. A year in which the fines are large is a year in which the Watch has been less successful, and the year is recorded in the register of the Watch as an exceptional year.

## Discipline and the Code

The Watch maintains a strict code of discipline, and the code is enforced by the captain and the lieutenants. A rider who is found drunk upon duty is dismissed from the service; a rider who is found guilty of theft is dismissed and prosecuted; a rider who is found guilty of cowardice in the face of the enemy is dismissed and disgraced. The chronicler notes that the code has been severe, and the severity has been justified by the danger of the roads and by the trust that is placed in the Watch by the merchants and the magistrates.

The Watch also maintains a code of courtesy that is observed toward the travellers upon the roads. The rider greets the merchant as he passes, and the rider offers such assistance as he may require. The rider escorts the merchant through the dangerous stretches of the road, and the rider remains with the merchant until the danger is past. The chronicler notes that the courtesy has been the source of much of the goodwill that the Watch enjoys, and the courtesy is one of the reasons that the merchants of the region are content to pay the tolls of the Watch.

## Significance

The Standing Watch of the Northern Roads is one of the most useful and least controversial of the local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and men are drawn from the households of the towns and the villages along the roads, and its jurisdiction covers the routes that have been the principal highways of the cold north since the founding of the realm. The Watch has kept the roads safe, has sheltered the travellers in time of storm, and has prosecuted the bandits and the outlaws who would prey upon the commerce of the region. The chronicler notes that the Watch has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the cold north, and the Watch is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every merchant of the region.
