## The Hall of the Bench

The Masonic Hall is the seat of the [Bench of the Stone Quarries](/content/government/local/bench-of-the-stone-quarries), the substantial building of stone and timber at the centre of the town of [Quarryside](/content/location/settlement/town/quarryside) in which the master, the wardens, and the sworn members of the Bench conduct the business of the trade. The hall was erected more than a century ago by the subscriptions of the sworn members, and the hall is built of the local stone that the quarries of the surrounding hills have produced for as many generations as the chronicles record. The chronicler notes that the hall is the most substantial building of the town, and the hall is held in particular esteem by the quarrymen, the masons, and the merchants whose trade the Bench regulates.

The hall is situated upon a slight rise at the centre of Quarryside, and the hall is approached from the market square by a flight of dressed stone steps that the masons of the lodges cut and laid at the time of the hall's erection. The position of the hall at the centre of the town is held by the Bench to be a sign of the importance of the trade to the life of the region, and the chronicler records that the hall has been the gathering place of the sworn members at every quarterly meeting since the hall was completed. The hall is surrounded by a paved yard of the same local stone, and the yard is kept clear of carts and merchandise by the sworn deputies who attend the meetings of the Bench.

## The Building and Its Stone

The hall is built of the local stone that the quarries of the southern flank of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the foothills of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) have produced, and the stone is of a colour and a grain that the masons of the region have long prized. The walls of the hall are squared and dressed to a standard that the masons of the Bench have used as their own measure of quality, and the chronicler notes that the dressing of the hall is held by the masons to be a perpetual standard against which the dressing of the working stone is to be judged. The joints of the walls are cut to a fineness that the ordinary masons of the lodges do not attempt in their working pieces, and the corners of the hall are laid with a precision that has been the admiration of every mason who has visited the building.

The timber of the hall is of the pine that grows upon the higher slopes of the surrounding hills, and the timber was seasoned for two full years before it was set in the roof and the floor. The roof is supported by a series of trusses that the master carpenter of the lodges designed for the hall and that the carpenters of the lodges cut and assembled in the yard of the hall at the time of the erection. The chronicler notes that the roof has been maintained by the sworn members in the century since the hall was built, and the roof has been renewed only once in that time, when a section of the original timbers was found to have been weakened by a long leak that the keepers had neglected to repair.

The hall is lighted by windows that are set high in the walls of the principal chambers, and the windows are fitted with shutters of stout oak that the keepers close at the end of every day and during the winter months when the Bench is not in session. The chronicler records that the windows were designed to admit the light without admitting the weather, and the windows have served the hall well in the century of the hall's use. The glass of the windows is of the better sort that the merchants of the warm coast have carried northward in trade, and the glass was set in lead that the workers of the Bench soldered with care at the time of the hall's erection.

## The Council Chamber

The council chamber is the principal room of the hall, and the council chamber is the meeting place of the master, the two wardens, and the sworn members when the Bench is in session. The chamber is arranged to permit the orderly conduct of the business of the trade, and the chamber is furnished with a long table of dressed stone that the masons of the lodges cut and polished for the Bench at the time of the hall's erection. The master sits at the head of the table, and the wardens sit at the master's right and left, and the sworn members sit upon the stone benches that the masons set along the walls of the chamber.

The council chamber is the place in which the Bench conducts the testing of the standards of the stone, the inspection of the dressing of the masons, the adjudication of the disputes that have arisen in the interval since the previous meeting, and the election of the master and the wardens when the term of their office expires. The chronicler records that the chamber is held by the Bench to be the most important room in the hall, and the sworn members regard the chamber with a respect that is the measure of the Bench's regard for the orderly conduct of the trade. The chamber is open to the sworn members and to the magistrates of the principal towns, and the chamber is closed to all others when the Bench is in session.

The chamber is hung with a series of wooden tablets upon which the clerk of the Bench inscribes the standards of the stone, the judgements of the disputes, and the names of the sworn members who have served the Bench since the hall was erected. The chronicler records that the tablets are renewed from time to time as the older inscriptions grow faint, and the older tablets are preserved in the archives of the hall as a record of the Bench's history. The tablets are held by the Bench to be a sign of the continuity of the trade, and the sworn members regard the tablets with a respect that is the measure of the Bench's regard for its own history.

## The Offices of the Clerk

The hall contains the offices of the clerk of the Bench, and the offices are situated in a wing of the hall that is set apart from the council chamber for the use of the clerk and the sworn deputies. The offices consist of a principal room in which the clerk keeps the registers of the Bench, a smaller room in which the sworn deputies may consult the registers, and a counting house in which the clerk receives the subscriptions of the sworn members and the fines that the Bench imposes upon those who violate the standards or the regulations of the trade. The chronicler records that the offices are furnished with the desks and the presses that the clerk requires for the keeping of the registers, and the offices are lighted by windows that look out upon the paved yard of the hall.

The clerk is responsible for the accuracy of the entries in the registers of the Bench, and the clerk is assisted by the sworn deputies who represent the quarry villages. The registers include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the apprentices, and the registers are the foundation of the reputation of the Bench for fair dealing and careful administration. The chronicler records that the registers have been consulted by the magistrates and by the historians in many a dispute, and the registers are the source of the Bench's authority in the trade.

## The Yard of the Standard Stones

The hall contains a small yard in which the standard stones are kept, and the yard is situated at the rear of the hall, sheltered from the weather by a roof of stout timber that the sworn members erected above the yard in the second generation of the hall's use. The yard is paved with the local stone, and the yard is furnished with a series of stone benches upon which the standard stones are set when the Bench is not in session. The yard is open to the sworn members and to the magistrates of the principal towns, and the yard is closed to all others.

The standard stones are the blocks of dressed stone that the Bench has chosen as the measure of the colour, the grain, the hardness, and the sound of the stone that the quarries of the region produce. The standards are renewed from time to time as the older blocks are worn or broken, and the renewed standards are entered in the register of the Bench with the date of the renewal and the names of the sworn members who approved the renewal. The chronicler records that the standards are the foundation of the reputation of the stone of Quarryside, and the standards are the source of the trust that the builders of the cold north place in the seal of the Bench.

## The Archives

The hall contains the archives in which the records of the Bench are preserved, and the archives are situated in a vaulted chamber beneath the council chamber that the masons of the lodges cut into the bedrock at the time of the hall's erection. The chamber is dry and cool, and the chamber is furnished with the presses in which the registers, the judgements, and the correspondence of the Bench are kept. The chronicler records that the archives are open to the sworn members and to the magistrates, and the archives are closed to all others without the permission of the master and the wardens.

The records of the Bench include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the apprentices, and the records have been preserved in the archives since the hall was erected more than a century ago. The chronicler records that the records are among the most complete of the records of any trade body of the known world, and the records are the foundation of the reputation of the Bench for fair dealing and careful administration. The archives are held in particular esteem by the magistrates of the principal towns, and the magistrates consult the archives in every dispute that requires a reference to the trade's history.

## The Maintenance of the Hall

The hall is maintained by the sworn members, and the sworn members contribute a small sum at every quarterly meeting toward the cost of the upkeep of the building, the roof, the windows, and the yard. The chronicler records that the subscriptions of the sworn members have been sufficient to maintain the hall in good repair through the century of the hall's use, and the subscriptions have enabled the Bench to renew the roof, the windows, and the presses of the offices from time to time as the wear of the years has required. The maintenance of the hall is the responsibility of a small committee of sworn members that the master appoints at every quarterly meeting, and the committee reports to the Bench upon the state of the building at every meeting.

The yard is swept by the sworn deputies who attend the meetings of the Bench, and the council chamber is set in order by the clerk and the deputies before every session. The chronicler notes that the hall is regarded by the sworn members as a common inheritance, and the sworn members regard the maintenance of the hall as a duty that is no less important than the maintenance of the standards of the stone. The chronicler finds in the careful maintenance of the building a measure of the Bench's fidelity to the trust that the founders of the hall reposed in the sworn members, and the chronicler records that the hall is held in particular esteem by every mason and every merchant of the cold north.
