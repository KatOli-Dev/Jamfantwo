## The Seat of the Stone Quarries' Bench at Quarryside

The Stone Quarries' Hall is the seat of the [Stone Quarries' Bench](/content/government/local/stone-quarries-bench), the substantial building of stone and timber at the centre of the town of [Quarryside](/content/location/settlement/town/quarryside) in which the master, the wardens, and the sworn members of the Bench conduct the business of the trade. The hall was erected more than a century ago by the subscriptions of the sworn members and is built of the local stone that the quarries of the surrounding hills have produced for as many generations as the chronicles record. The hall is the most substantial building of the town and is held in particular esteem by the quarrymen, masons, and merchants whose trade the Bench regulates.

The hall is situated upon a slight rise at the centre of Quarryside, approached from the market square by a flight of dressed stone steps that the masons of the lodges cut and laid at the time of the hall's erection. The position of the hall at the centre of the town is held by the Bench to be a sign of the importance of the trade to the life of the region. The hall has been the gathering place of the sworn members at every quarterly meeting since it was completed. It is surrounded by a paved yard of the same local stone, kept clear of carts and merchandise by the sworn deputies who attend the meetings of the Bench.

## The Local Stone, the Seasoned Timber, and the High Windows

The hall is built of the local stone from the quarries of the southern flank of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the foothills of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands), of a colour and grain that the masons of the region have long prized. The walls are squared and dressed to a standard that the masons of the Bench have used as their own measure of quality, and the dressing of the hall is held by the masons to be a perpetual standard against which working stone is judged. The joints of the walls are cut to a fineness that ordinary masons do not attempt in their working pieces, and the corners are laid with a precision that has been the admiration of every mason who has visited the building.

The timber of the hall is of the pine that grows upon the higher slopes of the surrounding hills, seasoned for two full years before it was set in the roof and floor. The roof is supported by a series of trusses designed by the master carpenter of the lodges and cut and assembled by the carpenters in the yard of the hall at the time of erection. The roof has been maintained by the sworn members in the century since the hall was built and has been renewed only once, when a section of the original timbers was found to have been weakened by a long leak that the keepers had neglected to repair.

The hall is lighted by windows set high in the walls of the principal chambers, fitted with shutters of stout oak that the keepers close at the end of every day and during the winter months when the Bench is not in session. The windows were designed to admit the light without admitting the weather and have served the hall well in the century of its use. The glass is of the better sort carried northward in trade by the merchants of the warm coast, set in lead that the workers of the Bench soldered with care at the time of the hall's erection.

## The Stone Table and the Wooden Tablets

The council chamber is the principal room of the hall, the meeting place of the master, the two wardens, and the sworn members when the Bench is in session. The chamber is arranged to permit the orderly conduct of the business of the trade, furnished with a long table of dressed stone that the masons of the lodges cut and polished for the Bench at the time of the hall's erection. The master sits at the head of the table, the wardens at the master's right and left, and the sworn members upon the stone benches set along the walls.

The council chamber is the place in which the Bench conducts the testing of the standards of stone, the inspection of the dressing of the masons, the adjudication of disputes that have arisen since the previous meeting, and the election of the master and wardens when their term expires. The chamber is held by the Bench to be the most important room in the hall, and the sworn members regard it with a respect that measures the Bench's regard for the orderly conduct of the trade. The chamber is open to the sworn members and to the magistrates of the principal towns and is closed to all others when the Bench is in session.

The chamber is hung with a series of wooden tablets upon which the clerk of the Bench inscribes the standards of the stone, the judgements of the disputes, and the names of the sworn members who have served the Bench since the hall was erected. The tablets are renewed from time to time as the older inscriptions grow faint, and the older tablets are preserved in the archives as a record of the Bench's history. The tablets are held to be a sign of the continuity of the trade.

## The Registers and the Counting House

The hall contains the offices of the clerk of the Bench, situated in a wing of the hall set apart from the council chamber. The offices consist of a principal room in which the clerk keeps the registers of the Bench, a smaller room in which the sworn deputies may consult the registers, and a counting house in which the clerk receives the subscriptions of the sworn members and the fines imposed upon those who violate the standards or regulations of the trade. The offices are furnished with the desks and presses the clerk requires for the keeping of the registers, lighted by windows that look out upon the paved yard of the hall.

The clerk is responsible for the accuracy of the entries in the registers, assisted by the sworn deputies who represent the quarry villages. The registers include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the apprentices, and they are the foundation of the reputation of the Bench for fair dealing and careful administration. The registers have been consulted by magistrates and historians in many a dispute and are the source of the Bench's authority in the trade.

## The Enclosed Yard and the Reference Blocks

The hall contains a small yard at the rear in which the standard stones are kept, sheltered from the weather by a roof of stout timber erected by the sworn members in the second generation of the hall's use. The yard is paved with the local stone and furnished with stone benches upon which the standard stones are set when the Bench is not in session. The yard is open to the sworn members and to the magistrates of the principal towns and closed to all others.

The standard stones are the blocks of dressed stone chosen by the Bench as the measure of the colour, grain, hardness, and sound of the stone produced by the quarries of the region. The standards are renewed from time to time as the older blocks are worn or broken, and the renewed standards are entered in the register with the date of renewal and the names of the sworn members who approved it. The standards are the foundation of the reputation of the stone of Quarryside and the source of the trust that the builders of the cold north place in the seal of the Bench.

## The Vaulted Chamber Beneath the Council Room

The hall contains the archives in which the records of the Bench are preserved, situated in a vaulted chamber beneath the council chamber that the masons of the lodges cut into the bedrock at the time of erection. The chamber is dry and cool, furnished with presses in which the registers, judgements, and correspondence of the Bench are kept. The archives are open to the sworn members and to the magistrates and are closed to all others without the permission of the master and the wardens.

The records include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the apprentices, preserved in the archives since the hall was erected more than a century ago. The records are among the most complete of any trade body in the known world and are the foundation of the Bench's reputation for fair dealing and careful administration. The archives are held in particular esteem by the magistrates of the principal towns, who consult them in every dispute requiring a reference to the trade's history.

## The Committee and the Subscriptions for Upkeep

The hall is maintained by the sworn members, who contribute a small sum at every quarterly meeting toward the upkeep of the building, the roof, the windows, and the yard. The subscriptions have been sufficient to maintain the hall in good repair through the century of its use and have enabled the Bench to renew the roof, the windows, and the presses of the offices from time to time. The maintenance of the hall is the responsibility of a small committee of sworn members appointed by the master at every quarterly meeting, which reports to the Bench upon the state of the building at every meeting.

The yard is swept by the sworn deputies who attend the meetings of the Bench, and the council chamber is set in order by the clerk and the deputies before every session. The hall is regarded by the sworn members as a common inheritance, and its maintenance is a duty no less important than the maintenance of the standards of the stone. In the careful maintenance of the building is found a measure of the Bench's fidelity to the trust that the founders reposed in the sworn members, and the hall is held in particular esteem by every mason and every merchant of the cold north.
