## The Standing Patrol of the Cold North Roads

The Northern Roads Watch is the standing patrol maintained by the cities and towns of the cold north to keep the great roads safe for merchants, pilgrims, and the king's messengers. The Watch is composed of mounted patrols that ride the roads between the principal towns, foot patrols that guard the bridges and fords, and small garrisons that hold the watch-towers along the principal routes. The Watch is answerable to the magistrates of the towns through which the roads pass, and the magistrates appoint the captains of the Watch from among the household knights and trusted men-at-arms of the region. The Watch is sustained by a small toll collected at the gates of the principal towns and by the fines it imposes upon offenders arrested upon the roads.

The Watch is one of the older local bodies of the cold north, its foundation ascribed to the accord the towns of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) and the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands) made with the king of the realm more than two centuries ago. The accord established the patrol of the roads, the rules of arrest, and the procedures by which disputes are adjudicated. The accord has been revised from time to time, with revisions entered in the register of the Watch and confirmed by the magistrates of the principal towns.

## The Captain, Sworn Riders, and Footmen

The Watch is composed of a captain, two lieutenants, a clerk, and the sworn riders, footmen, and garrisons that patrol the roads. The captain is appointed by the magistrates of the principal towns for a term of three years and must be a knight of good repute or a man-at-arms of at least ten years' standing. The lieutenants are appointed by the captain and represent the mounted patrol and the foot patrol. The clerk is appointed by the captain and holds office at the pleasure of the Watch, responsible for the registers, correspondence, and accounts.

The sworn riders are the mounted patrol, each armed with a sword, a dagger, a short bow, and a horn for signalling. The sworn footmen are the foot patrol, each armed with a bill, a short sword, and a horn. The garrisons are the small bodies of men-at-arms that hold the watch-towers, each consisting of a sergeant and four men. The sworn riders and footmen are drawn from the households of the towns and from the villages along the roads, and several captains have been born to no greater station than a sworn rider of the northern patrol.

## Bandits, Bridges, and the Rules of the Road

The Watch keeps the roads free of bandits, wreckers, and outlaws who would prey upon merchants and pilgrims. The mounted patrol rides in pairs, one rider upon the road and one upon the flank, keeping a steady watch for any sign of disturbance. The foot patrol guards the bridges, fords, and narrow passes, stationed at the points where the road is most easily blocked. The garrisons hold the watch-towers and signal the approach of any large body of men, and they also shelter travellers caught upon the roads in time of storm.

The Watch has the power to arrest any person found in the act of robbery or violence upon the roads and to bring the offender before the magistrate of the nearest town. The Watch also has the power to fine any traveller who violates the rules of the road: a rider who will not give way to a footman, a merchant who overloads his cart and breaks the road, a pilgrim who refuses to show his staff. The rules have been the subject of long debate in the Watch and have been revised from time to time to reflect the seasons and the traffic.

## The Chain of Stone Towers

The Watch maintains a chain of watch-towers along the principal roads, placed at intervals of a day's ride. The tower is a stone building of two stories, with a stout door and roof, built upon a rise of ground commanding a wide view of the road in both directions. The tower contains a small hearth, a small store of provisions, and the arms of the garrison. The towers have been the foundation of the safety of the roads and the refuge of many a traveller caught upon the road in time of storm.

The garrison of a tower consists of a sergeant and four men, holding the tower for a tour of two months. The sergeant is responsible for the maintenance of the tower, the signals exchanged with neighbouring towers, and the register of travellers who shelter in the tower. The sergeant has a duty to keep the register carefully, for it is the evidence by which the magistrates judge the state of the roads in time of dispute. The register is sent to the clerk of the Watch at the end of every month, who enters the entries in the general register.

## The Gate Tolls and the Fines

The Watch is sustained by a small toll collected at the gates of the principal towns, paid by every merchant who enters or leaves the town with a laden cart. The toll is set by the magistrates of the towns in consultation with the captain of the Watch and collected by the gatekeepers of the towns. The toll pays for the wages of the riders, footmen, and garrisons, and for the maintenance of the towers and arms. The toll has been stable for several generations and is one of the most carefully audited of the public revenues of the region.

The Watch also keeps its own accounts of the fines, paid to the clerk at the principal office of the Watch. The fines are used for the relief of travellers robbed upon the roads and for the maintenance of towers not otherwise provided for. The fines have been small in most years, and the smallness of the fines is the measure of the Watch's success in keeping the roads safe. A year in which the fines are large is recorded in the register as an exceptional year.

## The Code and the Courtesy of the Riders

The Watch maintains a strict code of discipline enforced by the captain and the lieutenants. A rider found drunk upon duty is dismissed from the service; a rider found guilty of theft is dismissed and prosecuted; a rider found guilty of cowardice in the face of the enemy is dismissed and disgraced. The code has been severe, and the severity has been justified by the danger of the roads and the trust placed in the Watch by the merchants and magistrates.

The Watch also maintains a code of courtesy observed toward travellers upon the roads. The rider greets the merchant as he passes and offers such assistance as may be required. The rider escorts the merchant through dangerous stretches and remains with him until the danger is past. This courtesy has been the source of much of the goodwill the Watch enjoys and is one of the reasons the merchants of the region are content to pay the tolls of the Watch.

## A Quiet Foundation of the Prosperity of the Cold North

The Northern Roads Watch is one of the most useful and least controversial of the local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and men are drawn from the households of the towns and villages along the roads, and its jurisdiction covers the routes that have been the principal highways of the cold north since the founding of the realm. The Watch has kept the roads safe, sheltered travellers in time of storm, and prosecuted the bandits and outlaws who would prey upon the commerce of the region. The Watch has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the cold north and is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every merchant of the region.
