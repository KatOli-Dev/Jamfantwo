## The Song-Half of the Calling

The Houses of Memory are the quiet establishments of the song-half of the [Order of the Shield and the Song](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/order-of-the-shield-and-the-song), the order's houses where the local songs and the local traditions are maintained and where the singers of the order keep the memory of the communities they serve. The houses are found in the larger market towns and at the more important river crossings of the temperate lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the houses are kept by the order's singers, who are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat. The chronicler notes that the houses are regarded by the order as the second of the principal institutions of the calling, and the houses are the seat of the song-half of the order's work in the same measure that the standing of the watch is the seat of the shield-half.

The order teaches that the divine Watcher is revealed in two complementary aspects, which the order names the shield-half and the song-half. The shield-half is the bearing of hardship for the protection of others, and the song-half is the keeping of memory, the recitation of the names of the dead, and the maintenance of the meaning of the community's life through the arts of voice and instrument. The chronicler notes that the houses of memory are the establishments in which the song-half is observed, and the order holds the houses in greater esteem than any of the meeting-places or the chapels that are found in the towns of the order's following.

## Origins among the First Singers

The Houses of Memory trace their foundation to the company of border riders who served the marches of the older Aethelaran kingdoms and who were accustomed, in their off-watch, to maintain the songs of their forebears and of the deeds they had seen. The traditions of the order speak of a woman called Vaelren of the Long Watch, who is held to have first bound the riders to the practice of singing the day's losses and the day's mercies into a single account at evening. The chronicler notes that whether Vaelren was a single figure or a composite of several riders is left to the chronicler's discretion, and the order is content that the binding should be remembered whether or not the binder can be named.

The first house of memory was a single room that the company of riders set aside for the keeping of the songs that they had learned in the field, and the room was furnished with a small hearth and a few wooden benches. The community's successors added a second room and then a third, and the house grew in the measure of the songs that the singers brought to it and the number of communities that were served by the singers. The order grew along the marches and the river country, and each new market town that received the teaching required its own house, for the distance between the towns is such that a single house could not serve the whole of the faithful. The chronicler notes that the houses of memory are therefore many, though each house is modelled upon the first, and each house is governed by the same discipline that the first company established.

## The Singers and Their Training

The houses of memory are kept by the order's singers, and the singers are the principal officers of the song-half of the calling. A singer of the order is required to learn by heart a substantial body of songs and to maintain the accuracy of their transmission from one generation to the next. The tradition holds that a song learned imperfectly is a song lost, and the singers are trained accordingly. The chronicler notes that the training of a singer is conducted by a senior singer of the nearest house, and the training is regarded by the order as the chief instrument by which the song-half is preserved.

The singers travel between the houses and between the orders of the faithful, carrying the songs from one district to the next, and they are regarded by the order as the keepers of its living memory. The chronicler notes that the singers are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat, and the clasp is the mark of the office. The order's houses of memory are open to all who seek a quiet place, and the singers are instructed to teach any who ask without charge, for the maintenance of the community's memory is held to be too important a duty to be turned into a trade.

## The Library of Songs

The houses of memory maintain a small library of the songs that the order has preserved, and the library is the principal room of the house. The library is housed in a chamber that is lined with shelves of plain wood upon which the copies of the older songs are kept. The library is open to the singers who serve the house and to such visitors as the senior singer permits, and the library is closed to all others. The chronicler notes that the library is regarded by the order as the heart of the house, and the library is the place to which every novice singer aspires and to which every senior singer returns.

The songs that the library holds are few in number and well established, and the songs include the older songs of the order and the local songs that the singers have gathered from the communities they serve. The chronicler notes that the order does not maintain an extensive library, for the order values the song learned by heart above the song written down, and the order has been slow to commit its songs to the page. The songs that the order has chosen to write are regarded by the senior singers as the foundation of the tradition, and the additions to the library are made with great care and only after the conference of the senior singers has approved the addition.

## The Schedules of Singing

The order maintains at the house a schedule of singing that the singers observe throughout the year. The schedule is divided into the seasons of teaching and the seasons of travel, and the seasons alternate in a cycle that the order has refined over generations. The seasons of teaching are set apart for the instruction of the novice singers in the songs that the order has preserved, and the seasons of travel are set apart for the visits that the singers make to the communities of the surrounding countryside. The chronicler notes that the schedule is observed without exception, and the breaking of the schedule is regarded by the senior singers as a failure of discipline.

A novice singer is admitted to a house after a probation of some seasons, during which the candidate attends the teaching and the singing and contributes to the common maintenance of the house. The probation is conducted under the direction of a senior singer, and the senior singer reports to the conference of the senior singers upon the candidate's progress at the close of the probation. The chronicler notes that the probation is the principal means by which the order maintains the standard of the singing, and the order regards the probation as a worthy complement to the long training of the singer.

## The Evening Review

The central observance of the order is the evening review, and the houses of memory are the establishments in which the review is kept by the singers and by such of the faithful as are able to attend. The review is conducted in the meeting-hall of the house, and the meeting-hall is a plain room that is lit by a single window and warmed by a small hearth. At the close of the day, the household or the company recalls together the events of the day, giving thanks for the mercies and observing the losses. The chronicler notes that a short song of account is sung, naming the day's sick and recovered, the day's fed and unfed, the day's spared and the day's fallen, and the review is the measure of the order's care for the community.

The review is not a confession and not a punishment, and the order is careful to mark the difference. The sins of the day are observed but not adjudicated, and the remedy is held to lie in the change of conduct on the morrow rather than in any further observance at the evening. The order teaches that the review is meant to keep the account honest, so that the community may know what it has done and may be moved, in good time, to mend what has been done amiss. The chronicler notes that the review is regarded by the order as the chief instrument by which the song-half of the calling is observed in the household.

## The House and the Community

The house of memory is bound to the community it serves by a customary relation that the chroniclers hold to be a part of the discipline of the order. The community is required to maintain the house in provisions and in the small endowment that the singers require, and the house is required to maintain the community in the keeping of its memory and in the instruction of the young in the songs of the order. The chronicler notes that this mutual obligation has done much to preserve the harmony of the market towns and the river country, where the house and the community are accustomed to regard one another as parts of a single order. The breach of the mutual obligation is visited by the censure of the community, and the censure is delivered by the elders of the community in the presence of the senior singer of the house.

The house is also the home of the singers who maintain the discipline in the wider community of the surrounding countryside, and the singers are recruited from among the senior singers of the house. The singers travel between the houses and between the orders of the faithful, and the singers are the principal point of contact between the house and the wider community. The chronicler notes that the singers are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat, and the clasp is the mark of the office. The order regards the house as a worthy complement to the standing of the watch, and the order holds the house in greater esteem than any other of its institutions.

## Relation to Other Houses

The Houses of Memory maintain a correspondence with the houses of the kindred traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the correspondence is the means by which the order has preserved its kinship with the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame) and the other contemplative orders of the continent. A singer of the order will sometimes attend the evening review of a hearth-hall of the Still Flame, and a hearth-keeper of the Still Flame will sometimes attend the singing at a house of memory, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. The chronicler notes that the correspondence is the measure of the order's openness to the wider world, and the order regards the correspondence as a privilege rather than a claim.

The order has also exchanged singers with the [Faith of the Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-forged-flame), whose hearth-keepers teach a discipline of the workshop that the order regards as a worthy complement to the singing of the houses of memory. The chronicler notes that the exchanges are recorded in the register of each house, and the register is the source of the order's knowledge of the wider world. The senior singers caution against the temptation to make a display of the exchanges, for the order regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very ornament the order counsels against in the singing.

## Significance

The Houses of Memory are the quiet establishments of the song-half of the [Order of the Shield and the Song](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/order-of-the-shield-and-the-song), and the houses are the seat of the singers, the library of songs, the schedules of singing, and the evening review. The houses are quiet establishments in the market towns and the river country of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the houses are supported by the labour of the singers and by the small endowment that the communities provide. The chronicler notes that the houses have been the source of the order's slow growth in the market towns and the river country, and the houses are a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the temples. The order asks of its practitioners that they bear what they are called to bear and that they sing what they are called to sing, and it promises them, in return, that the bearing and the singing are themselves a share in the Watcher's keeping of the world.
