## Overview

The Council of Vintners and Cellarers is the standing guild of the merchants and the craftsmen who handle the wine of the inland valleys and the ale of the river towns of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-morvind-uplands), the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-stormfall-coast), and the lower reaches of the Greyring. The Council is the regulator of the trade in wine and ale, the keeper of the standards of the vintners and the brewers, and the adjudicator of the disputes that arise among the merchants, the innkeepers, and the tavern-keepers of the region. The Council is composed of the masters of the principal vintner-houses and the head brewers of the principal towns, and the Council meets in the hall of the vintners at [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) four times in each year.

The Council is one of the older of the merchant guilds of the cold north, and its foundation is ascribed to the accord that the vintners of the inland valleys made with the brewers of the river towns more than two centuries ago. The accord established the standards of the wine and the ale, the rules of the trade between the vintners and the brewers, and the procedures by which the disputes are adjudicated. The accord has been revised from time to time, and the revisions have been entered in the register of the Council and confirmed by the magistrates of the principal towns.

## Composition and Officers

The Council is composed of a master, two wardens, a clerk, and the sworn members who represent the vintner-houses and the breweries. The master is elected by the members for a term of two years, and the master must be a vintner or a brewer of at least fifteen years' standing. The wardens are elected for the same term and represent the vintners and the brewers respectively. The clerk is appointed by the master and the wardens and holds office at the pleasure of the Council, and the clerk is responsible for the registers, the correspondence, and the accounts of the Council.

The sworn members are the masters of the vintner-houses that hold at least a thousand barrels of capacity, the head brewers of the breweries that produce at least five hundred barrels in a year, and the innkeepers who hold a charter from the Council to keep a public house. A vintner or a brewer who falls below the threshold may continue as a sworn member for life, but no new member is admitted who does not meet the threshold. The chronicler notes that the threshold has been raised from time to time as the trade has grown, and the raising has been the subject of long debate in the Council.

## Standards of Wine and Ale

The Council maintains the standards of the wine and the ale that is sold within its jurisdiction, and the standards are enforced by the wardens and by the sworn members. The wine is judged by colour, by clarity, by fragrance, and by taste, and the standard is set by the tasting that the Council conducts each year at the autumn meeting. The ale is judged by colour, by fragrance, by the head that it carries, and by the taste, and the standard is set by the tasting that the Council conducts each year at the spring meeting. A wine or an ale that fails the standard may not be sold under the seal of the Council, and the seller is fined.

The Council also maintains the standards of the vessels in which the wine and the ale are kept. The barrel must be of oak or of beech, and the hoops must be of iron or of well-tanned willow. The barrel must be tight, and the tightness is tested by the wardens at the time of the sealing. The seal of the Council is set upon the barrel that passes the test, and the seal is the guarantee that the barrel holds the wine or the ale of the standard. A barrel that bears the seal of the Council may be sold throughout the region without further inspection, and the chronicler notes that the seal has been the foundation of the reputation of the vintners and the brewers of the cold north.

## The Trade between Vintners and Brewers

The Council regulates the trade between the vintners and the brewers, and the regulations have been the source of much of the prosperity of the inland valleys and the river towns. The vintner sells his wine to the brewer, and the brewer blends the wine with his ale to produce the small beer that is the common drink of the inland towns. The brewer pays the vintner in coin and in ale, and the proportion is set by the Council at the autumn meeting. The proportion has been the subject of long debate, and the Council has revised the proportion from time to time to reflect the harvest and the demand.

The Council also regulates the trade between the brewers and the innkeepers, and the innkeepers are bound by the charter that the Council has issued to them. The charter sets the price at which the innkeeper may sell the ale and the small beer, and the charter sets the quality of the ale and the small beer that the innkeeper must serve. An innkeeper who violates the charter is brought before the Council, and the Council has the power to revoke the charter. The chronicler notes that the revocation has been rare, for the innkeepers are jealous of their charters, and the revocation has been the subject of much local controversy when it has occurred.

## Adjudication of Disputes

The Council adjudicates the disputes that arise among the vintners, the brewers, and the innkeepers, and the adjudication is final within the trade. The dispute is brought before the master and the wardens, and the master and the wardens hear the parties and the witnesses. The master and the wardens then retire to consider the matter, and they return with a judgement that is entered in the register of the Council. The judgement may be appealed to the full Council at the next quarterly meeting, and the full Council may confirm, modify, or reverse the judgement of the master and the wardens.

The Council has the power to compel the attendance of the parties and the witnesses, and the Council has the power to fine any party who is found to have violated the standards or the regulations of the Council. The Council does not have the power to imprison, and a party who refuses to pay the fine is reported to the magistrate of the town. The chronicler notes that the magistrates have always supported the Council in the collection of the fines, for the magistrates recognise that the Council is the regulator of a trade that is essential to the prosperity of the region.

## The Hall and the Records

The Council meets in the hall of the vintners at Vassholm, and the hall is a substantial building of stone and timber that was erected more than a century ago by the subscriptions of the members. The hall contains the council chamber, the offices of the clerk, the cellars in which the standard wines and ales are kept, and the archives in which the records of the Council are preserved. The archives are open to the sworn members and to the magistrates, and the chronicler notes that the archives are among the most complete of the records of any guild of the known world.

The records of the Council include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the seals. The registers are kept by the clerk, and the clerk is responsible for the accuracy of the entries. The chronicler notes that the registers have been consulted by the magistrates and by the historians in many a dispute, and the registers are the foundation of the reputation of the Council for fair dealing and careful administration.

## Significance

The Council of Vintners and Cellarers is one of the most useful and least controversial of the local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and members are drawn from the vintner-houses and the breweries of the inland valleys and the river towns, and its jurisdiction covers a trade that is essential to the prosperity and the comfort of the people of the cold north. The Council has maintained the standards of the wine and the ale, has regulated the trade between the vintners and the brewers, and has adjudicated the disputes that arise among the merchants and the innkeepers of the region. The chronicler notes that the Council has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the inland valleys and the river towns, and the Council is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every merchant of the region.
