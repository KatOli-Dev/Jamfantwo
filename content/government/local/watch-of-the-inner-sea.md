## Overview

The Watch of the Inner Sea is the standing patrol maintained by the city of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and its neighbours along the sheltered waters of the southern reach of the Greyring, from the mole of Khalisar to the southern mouth of the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden). The Watch is the oldest continuous maritime body of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast, and its officers answer jointly to the city council of Khalisar and to the magistrates of the smaller ports that share the waters. The Watch keeps the channels free of wreck and of floating debris, directs vessels in and out of the harbours, supervises the lading and unlading of cargo at the public quays, and collects the small dues by which the Watch is sustained. The Watch has its own barges, its own beacons, and its own small fleet of light galleys for the pursuit of offenders.

The Watch is a body of practitioners rather than a council of deliberation. The commander is a senior captain chosen for a term of three years by the council of Khalisar, and the commanders of the smaller ports may each nominate a lieutenant who serves aboard the flag-barge and who represents the smaller port in the daily orders. The Watch numbers some forty officers and seamen in permanent service, and the number is augmented in the season of the great grain-ships by a levy of landsmen from the coastal villages.

## Composition and Ranks

The Watch is composed of a commander, two lieutenants, four senior pilots, eight junior pilots, and the seamen and deckhands who row the galleys and tend the beacons. The commander is responsible for the overall conduct of the patrol and for the quarterly report that is submitted to the council. The lieutenants command the two divisions of the patrol, the inner division that works the harbours of Khalisar and the outer division that works the southern waters of the strait. The senior pilots are veterans who have served the Watch for at least ten years and who hold the rank that entitles them to command a vessel of the Watch without the supervision of a lieutenant.

The junior pilots are the rising officers of the Watch, and they are drawn from the sons of pilots, from the wards of the council of Khalisar, and from the apprentices of the merchant guilds. A junior pilot must serve for at least five years before he is considered for the senior rank, and the years of service must include at least two winters upon the outer division. The seamen and the deckhands are the working force of the Watch, and they are drawn from the coastal villages and from the fishermen who ply the strait. The chronicler notes that the Watch has been an avenue of advancement for the sons of the fishermen, and several commanders of the Watch have been born to no greater station than a deckhand of the outer division.

## Duties at the Harbours

The Watch keeps a barge at the principal quays of Khalisar, and the barge carries a junior pilot at all hours of the day and the night. The pilot directs the mooring and the unmooring of the merchant vessels, sees that the vessels are properly fendered against the quay, and reports any damage that occurs in the lading or the unlading. The pilot also supervises the discharge of ballast, which is dumped at a designated ground below the harbour, and the pilot reports any vessel that discharges its ballast within the harbour. The chronicler notes that the discharge of ballast within the harbour has been a long-standing offence, and the Watch has the power to fine the master and to detain the vessel until the fine is paid.

The Watch also maintains the lighthouses and the beacons that mark the channels of the strait. The principal lighthouse stands upon the southern mole of Khalisar, and the keeper of the lighthouse is a senior seaman of the Watch who has been relieved of duty afloat by reason of age or injury. The keeper maintains the fire, trims the wicks at dusk, and signals the state of the tide by the colour of the light that he shows above the doorway of the lantern room. The Watch maintains smaller beacons upon the rocks and the shoals of the strait, and the beacons are tended by the fishermen of the nearby villages under the supervision of the outer division.

## Duties upon the Outer Waters

The outer division of the Watch patrols the southern waters of the strait, from the mole of Khalisar to the southern mouth. The patrol consists of two light galleys that cruise in company, one upon the eastern shore and one upon the western, and the galleys keep a steady watch for vessels in distress, for wreckage that may foul the anchors of the merchantmen, and for the small boats of the wreckers who sometimes lure vessels upon the rocks. The galleys are armed with a single bow-chaser and with a stand of pikes for boarding, and the officers of the Watch are authorised to detain any vessel that refuses to heave to at their signal.

The Watch has the power to arrest any person found in the act of luring a vessel upon the rocks, and the Watch has the duty to bring the offender before the magistrate of the nearest port. The chronicler notes that the wreckers have been a long-standing problem of the southern waters, and the Watch has prosecuted several cases in each generation. The Watch also has the duty to recover the cargo of any vessel that is wrecked upon the coast, and the cargo is held by the Watch until the owner can be found or until the cargo is sold by public auction and the proceeds are deposited in the court.

## The Dues of the Watch

The Watch is sustained by a small due that is collected from every vessel that uses the harbours of the Sahrani coast. The due is set by the council of Khalisar in consultation with the smaller ports, and the due is collected by the harbour-master at the time of the unlading. The due pays for the maintenance of the lighthouses, the beacons, and the galleys, and the due pays the wages of the officers and the seamen. The chronicler notes that the due has been stable for several generations, and the due is one of the most carefully audited of the public revenues of the region.

The Watch keeps its own accounts, and the accounts are audited twice in each year by a committee of the council. The committee reports to the council upon the state of the Watch and upon the sufficiency of the dues, and the committee has the power to recommend an increase or a decrease of the dues. The chronicler notes that the Watch has been one of the most efficient of the public bodies of the Sahrani coast, and the small cost of the Watch has been repaid many times over by the safety that it provides to the commerce of the region.

## Discipline and the Code

The Watch maintains a strict code of discipline, and the code is enforced by the commander and the lieutenants. An officer who is found drunk upon duty is dismissed from the service; an officer who is found guilty of theft is dismissed and prosecuted; an officer who is found guilty of cowardice in the face of the enemy is dismissed and disgraced. The chronicler notes that the code has been severe, and the severity has been justified by the danger of the waters and by the trust that is placed in the Watch by the merchants and the magistrates.

The Watch also maintains a code of courtesy that is observed toward the vessels that use the harbours. The pilot of the barge greets the master of a vessel as the master comes ashore, and the pilot offers the master such assistance as he may require. The pilot escorts the master to the office of the harbour-master, and the pilot remains at the master's side until the master has completed his business. The chronicler notes that the courtesy has been the source of much of the goodwill that the Watch enjoys, and the courtesy is one of the reasons that the merchants of the coast are content to pay the dues of the Watch.

## Significance

The Watch of the Inner Sea is one of the oldest and most respected of the local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and seamen are drawn from the coastal villages and the merchant families, and its jurisdiction covers the waters that have been the principal highway of the Sahrani coast since the founding of Khalisar. The Watch has kept the channels safe, has recovered the cargoes of the wrecked, and has prosecuted the wreckers and the smugglers who would prey upon the commerce of the region. The chronicler notes that the Watch has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the Sahrani coast, and the Watch is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every merchant of the region.
