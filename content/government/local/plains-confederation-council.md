## The Governing Council of the Grassland Alliance

The Confederation Council is the governing body of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), the alliance of the central grassland cities and the [centaur](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) kindreds of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The council is the primary political institution of the confederation, responsible for the coordination of trade, agriculture, and defence across the full extent of the central grasslands. The council is composed of representatives from each member city and each centaur kindred, and its decisions are binding on all members of the confederation. The council's governance is built upon the principles of representation, consensus, and accountability, and it is among the most broadly participatory political institutions in the known world.

The council meets at designated meeting places situated at the crossroads of the grassland routes. The meeting places are marked by stone circles that the centaurs have built at traditional gathering points, and these circles are the most distinctive monuments of the plains. The council convenes three times each year: the spring council, the summer council, and the autumn council. Each session lasts for several days, and the meetings are attended by representatives of the member cities and kindreds, together with the officials and staff necessary for the conduct of business.

## Composition and Representation

The Confederation Council is composed of representatives from each member city and each centaur kindred. The number of representatives from each member is fixed: each city sends two representatives, and each centaur kindred sends two elders. This equality of representation is a deliberate feature of the confederation's constitution, designed to ensure that the interests of the smaller members are not overshadowed by the larger. The representatives of the settled cities are chosen by popular vote of all free adults in each community who have reached the age of majority, regardless of species, gender, or wealth. Among the centaur kindreds, the elders who serve as representatives must command the consent of their kindreds to hold their seats, and a kindred that loses confidence in its elder may set aside that elder at any gathering and choose another.

The representatives serve for a term of three years and may be re-elected without limit. There is no formal qualification for membership beyond the age of majority and the citizenship of a member community. The representatives are expected to attend every council session, and a representative who misses two consecutive sessions without good cause may be replaced by the member community. The council maintains a register of representatives, and the credentials of each representative are verified at the opening of each session.

The franchise among the settled cities is exercised through a system of public voting conducted at the town hall or market square of each community. Every free adult who has reached the age of majority and who has resided in the community for at least one year may vote. The vote is conducted by a show of hands, and the result is recorded by the town clerk and certified by the elders of the community. Among the centaur kindreds, the franchise is exercised through open deliberation at the kindred fires, where every adult member of the kindred may speak and vote. The elders who serve as representatives must command the consent of the kindred, and a kindred that loses confidence in its elder may set aside that elder at any gathering and choose another.

## The Three Annual Sessions

The spring council is the most important of the three annual sessions. It convenes at the first full moon after the winter solstice, at a meeting place designated by the preceding autumn council. The spring council is the occasion at which plans for the coming season are made and arrangements for grazing and planting are agreed. The representatives bring with them the assessments of their communities regarding the expected harvest, the condition of the herds, and the state of the grazing lands. The council allocates the grazing rights for the coming season, determines the areas to be planted with crops, and sets the contributions that each member will make to the collective defence and administration of the confederation.

The spring council also elects the presiding officer of the confederation, known as the Convener, who serves for a term of one year. The Convener is chosen from among the representatives by a majority vote, and the office rotates among the members to ensure that no single city or kindred dominates the governance of the confederation. The Convener is responsible for the conduct of the council's business, the implementation of its decisions, and the representation of the confederation in dealings with external powers. The Convener may address the Open Grass Assembly of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) at its opening, and the Convener's report on the state of the confederation is the first item of business at each council session.

The summer council convenes at the height of the growing season, at a meeting place in the central grasslands. The summer council is a shorter affair than the spring council, typically lasting two or three days. Its business is concerned with the management of trade and the resolution of any disputes that have arisen during the season. The summer council also reviews the condition of the roads and bridges that are the arteries of grassland commerce and authorises any repairs or improvements that are needed. The summer council is also the occasion at which the representatives report on the progress of the season in their communities, and the council may adjust the allocation of grazing rights or planting areas if the circumstances require.

The autumn council convenes after the harvest, at a meeting place in the southern grasslands. The autumn council is the occasion at which the harvest is assessed, contributions are levied, and preparations for winter are co-ordinated. The representatives bring with them the accounts of their communities' harvests, and the council assesses the contributions that each member will make to the collective stores and the winter relief fund. The autumn council also reviews the condition of the collective herds and determines the distribution of the meat, wool, and leather that will be available for trade over the winter months. The autumn council is also the occasion at which the council reviews the conduct of the Convener and the administration of the confederation over the preceding year.

## Decision-Making and Consensus

The Confederation Council reaches its decisions through a process of deliberation and voting. Each representative has one vote, and decisions are made by a simple majority of those present, except for matters of particular importance, which require a two-thirds majority. The matters that require a two-thirds majority include the admission of new members, the amendment of the Rights of the Plains, the declaration of war, and the negotiation of treaties with external powers. The council may also refer a matter to the member communities for a vote if the representatives cannot reach agreement.

In practice, the council seeks to reach its decisions by consensus rather than by formal vote. The representatives deliberate on each matter at length, and the Convener guides the discussion toward a resolution that all members can accept. The consensus tradition reflects the centaur influence on the confederation's governance, for the centaur kindreds make their own decisions by consensus and have encouraged the settled cities to adopt a similar approach. A decision reached by consensus is regarded as more legitimate than a decision reached by a narrow majority, and the council generally avoids forcing a vote on a matter that is deeply contested.

The council's decisions are binding on all members of the confederation. The council has the power to levy contributions, negotiate treaties, and dispatch a collective force in times of need. The council may also impose sanctions on a member that fails to meet its obligations, including the suspension of voting rights and the exclusion from the benefits of confederation membership. The council has rarely exercised its power of sanction, for the members generally recognise that the obligations of membership are the price of the benefits that the confederation provides.

## The Open Grass Assembly

Alongside the council of representatives, each council meeting is preceded by an Open Grass Assembly, a gathering open to any member of the confederation who wishes to attend. The Open Grass Assembly is a distinctive feature of the confederation's governance, and it is the means by which the ordinary people of the plains the farmers, herders, traders, and craftworkers make their voices heard in the affairs of the confederation. The Assembly has no formal authority to overrule the council, but its deliberations carry weight, and a council that ignores a clear consensus of the Assembly risks unrest and, in extreme cases, the withdrawal of member communities.

The Open Grass Assembly gathers in a great circle at the edge of the council meeting place, with the representatives seated in the centre and any citizen who wishes to speak standing in the open space before them. Speakers are heard in order of request, and the council is expected to respond to each concern before proceeding to its own business. The Assembly is also the occasion at which representatives are held to account: any citizen may question a representative about decisions taken at the previous council or about the conduct of the representative since the last meeting. A representative who cannot satisfy the Assembly may face a motion of recall.

## The Council Officers and Administration

The Confederation Council is assisted by a body of officers and administrators who manage the day-to-day affairs of the confederation. The principal officers are the Clerk of the Council, who maintains the records of the council's proceedings and the registers of members and contributions; the Keeper of the Roads, who oversees the maintenance of the roads and bridges that connect the member communities; the Keeper of the Herds, who manages the collective herds and the distribution of grazing rights; and the Keeper of the Stores, who manages the grain warehouses and the winter relief fund. The officers are appointed by the council and serve at its pleasure.

The administration of the confederation is funded by the contributions of the member communities. Each member contributes a share of its harvest or its herd to the collective stores, and the council uses these contributions to fund the maintenance of the roads and bridges, the operation of the grain warehouses, and the defence of the grasslands. The contributions are assessed at the autumn council on the basis of the harvest reports submitted by the members, and the council may adjust the contributions if the circumstances require.

## The Council in the Life of the Plains

The Confederation Council is the central institution of the Plains Confederation, and its deliberations shape the lives of the people of the grasslands. The council's decisions determine where the herds will graze, which fields will be planted, and how the harvest will be distributed. The council's management of the roads and bridges determines the routes of trade and the volume of commerce that flows through the member communities. The council's defence arrangements determine the security of the grasslands and the protection of the routes against raiders and wild peoples.

The council is also the forum in which the diverse communities of the plains Settled and nomadic, human and centaur, farmer and herder negotiate their shared future. The council's tradition of consensus and open deliberation has proved remarkably effective in reconciling the interests of communities whose ways of life are very different. The council has sustained the peace and prosperity of the plains for many centuries, and it stands as a model of inclusive and accountable governance in a world where such models are rare.
