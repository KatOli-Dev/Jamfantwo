## Overview

The Bench of the Stone Quarries is the standing body of the master masons, the quarrymen, and the stone-cutters who work the quarries of the southern flank of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-morvind-uplands) and the foothills of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/the-stoneheim-highlands). The Bench is the regulator of the quarries, the keeper of the standards of the stone, and the adjudicator of the disputes that arise among the quarrymen, the masons, and the merchants who buy the stone of the region. The Bench is composed of the masters of the principal quarries, the heads of the masonic lodges, and the sworn deputies who represent the quarry villages. The Bench meets in the masonic hall at the town of Quarryside four times in each year.

The Bench is one of the older of the trade bodies of the cold north, and its foundation is ascribed to the accord that the master masons of the southern quarries made with the lords of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) more than three centuries ago. The accord established the bounds of the principal quarries, the standards of the stone, the rules of the apprenticeship, and the procedures by which the disputes are adjudicated. The accord has been revised from time to time, and the revisions have been entered in the register of the Bench and confirmed by the magistrates of the principal towns.

## Composition and Officers

The Bench is composed of a master, two wardens, a clerk, and the sworn members who represent the quarries and the masonic lodges. The master is elected by the sworn members for a term of three years, and the master must be a master mason of at least fifteen years' standing. The wardens are elected for the same term and represent the quarrymen and the masons respectively. The clerk is appointed by the master and the wardens and holds office at the pleasure of the Bench, and the clerk is responsible for the registers, the correspondence, and the accounts of the Bench.

The sworn members are the masters of the quarries that produce at least five hundred tonnes of dressed stone in a year, the heads of the masonic lodges that employ at least twenty journeymen, and the sworn deputies of the quarry villages. A quarry or a lodge that falls below the threshold may continue as a sworn member for life, but no new member is admitted who does not meet the threshold. The chronicler notes that the threshold has been raised from time to time as the trade has grown, and the raising has been the subject of long debate in the Bench.

## Standards of the Stone

The Bench maintains the standards of the stone that is quarried and dressed within its jurisdiction, and the standards are enforced by the wardens and by the sworn members. The stone is judged by colour, by grain, by hardness, and by the sound that it gives when it is struck with a hammer. The standard is set by the testing that the Bench conducts each year at the autumn meeting, and the standard is entered in the register of the Bench. A stone that fails the standard may not be sold under the seal of the Bench, and the seller is fined.

The Bench also maintains the standards of the dressing, and the dressing is judged by the squareness of the face, by the straightness of the edge, and by the fineness of the joint. The standard is set by the inspection that the Bench conducts each year at the spring meeting, and the inspection is conducted at the principal yards. A stone that fails the standard of dressing must be re-dressed before it may be sold, and the quarryman or the mason who offers the stone for sale is fined. The chronicler notes that the seal of the Bench has been the foundation of the reputation of the stone of the region, and the stone of the southern quarries is held in high esteem by the builders of the cold north.

## The Trade in Stone

The Bench regulates the trade in stone, and the regulations have been the source of much of the prosperity of the quarry villages and the masonic towns. The quarryman sells his stone to the mason, and the mason dresses the stone and sells it to the builder. The mason pays the quarryman in coin and in dressed stone, and the proportion is set by the Bench at the autumn meeting. The proportion has been the subject of long debate, and the Bench has revised the proportion from time to time to reflect the harvest of the quarry and the demand of the builders.

The Bench also regulates the apprenticeship, and the apprentice is bound to a master mason for a term of seven years. The master is responsible for the training of the apprentice in the craft of the mason, and the apprentice is examined by the Bench at the end of the term. The examination is conducted by the wardens and by two senior sworn members, and the apprentice who passes the examination is admitted to the Bench as a journeyman. The chronicler notes that the apprenticeship has been the foundation of the skill of the masons of the region, and the Bench maintains the standards of the apprenticeship as one of its most important responsibilities.

## Adjudication of Disputes

The Bench adjudicates the disputes that arise among the quarrymen, the masons, and the merchants who buy the stone of the region, and the adjudication is final within the trade. The dispute is brought before the master and the wardens, and the master and the wardens hear the parties and the witnesses. The master and the wardens then retire to consider the matter, and they return with a judgement that is entered in the register of the Bench. The judgement may be appealed to the full Bench at the next quarterly meeting, and the full Bench may confirm, modify, or reverse the judgement of the master and the wardens.

The Bench has the power to compel the attendance of the parties and the witnesses, and the Bench has the power to fine any party who is found to have violated the standards or the regulations of the Bench. The Bench does not have the power to imprison, and a party who refuses to pay the fine is reported to the magistrate of the nearest town. The chronicler notes that the magistrates have always supported the Bench in the collection of the fines, for the magistrates recognise that the Bench is the regulator of a trade that is essential to the prosperity of the region.

## The Hall and the Records

The Bench meets in the masonic hall at the town of Quarryside, and the hall is a substantial building of stone and timber that was erected more than a century ago by the subscriptions of the sworn members. The hall contains the council chamber, the offices of the clerk, the small yard in which the standard stones are kept, and the archives in which the records of the Bench are preserved. The archives are open to the sworn members and to the magistrates, and the chronicler notes that the archives are among the most complete of the records of any trade body of the known world.

The records of the Bench include the register of the sworn members, the register of the judgements, the register of the standards, and the register of the apprentices. The registers are kept by the clerk, and the clerk is responsible for the accuracy of the entries. The chronicler notes that the registers have been consulted by the magistrates and by the historians in many a dispute, and the registers are the foundation of the reputation of the Bench for fair dealing and careful administration.

## Significance

The Bench of the Stone Quarries is one of the most useful and least controversial of the local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and members are drawn from the quarries and the masonic lodges of the southern flank of Morvind and the foothills of the Stoneheim Highlands, and its jurisdiction covers a trade that is essential to the prosperity and the comfort of the people of the cold north. The Bench has maintained the standards of the stone and the dressing, has regulated the trade between the quarrymen and the masons, and has adjudicated the disputes that arise among the merchants and the builders of the region. The chronicler notes that the Bench has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the quarry villages and the masonic towns, and the Bench is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every builder of the region.
