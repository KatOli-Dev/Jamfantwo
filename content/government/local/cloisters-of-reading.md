## The Houses of the Doctrine

The Cloisters of Reading are the houses of the [Doctrine of the Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/doctrine-of-the-unwritten-name), the quiet establishments where the order of readers maintains the libraries, the schedules of study, and the conference of senior and junior scholars. The cloisters are found chiefly in the river towns and the older cloister districts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the cloisters are not monasteries in the older sense, for the doctrine does not require of its readers a withdrawal from the world. The chronicler notes that many of the most senior scholars have kept their households and their trades while pursuing the long study, and the cloisters are held to be places of meeting rather than places of retreat.

The cloisters are the seat of the masters of reading, the senior readers who are charged with the oversight of the libraries and the instruction of the juniors. The masters hold no worldly authority and are counseled against the temptation to mere governance, and the masters continue to maintain themselves by their hands and by the contributions of the readers who attend the cloisters. The chronicler notes that the cloisters are supported by a small endowment and by the labour of the readers themselves, and the doctrine teaches that the work of the household is itself a discipline of attention that complements the work of the study.

## Origins among the First Readers

The Cloisters of Reading trace their foundation to a small community of readers who withdrew from the court of an early Aethelaran kingdom and devoted themselves to the comparison of the older texts that had begun to circulate among the libraries of the time. The community's founder, a scholar remembered only by the title of the First Reader, was held to have maintained that the texts of the age contradicted one another in their accounts of the divine, and that the contradictions were not errors to be resolved but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. The chronicler notes that whether the First Reader was a single figure or a succession of senior readers is left open, and the doctrine regards the question as one of the lesser mysteries that the student will learn to leave aside.

The first cloister was a single chamber that the community's founders set aside for the keeping of the older texts and the conference of the readers who had withdrawn from the court. The community's successors added a second chamber and then a third, and the cloister grew in the measure of the texts that the readers brought to it and the number of readers who were admitted to the conference. The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of the study in their home towns. The chronicler notes that the cloisters of the doctrine are therefore many, though each cloister is modelled upon the first, and each cloister is governed by the same discipline that the first community established.

## The Library and Its Care

The central observance of the doctrine is the long reading, and the library is the room in which the long reading is conducted. The library is the principal chamber of the cloister, and the library is lined with shelves of plain wood upon which the copies of the older texts are kept. The library is open to the readers who have been admitted to a long reading, and the library is closed to all others. The chronicler notes that the library is regarded by the doctrine as the heart of the cloister, and the library is the place to which every novice aspires and to which every senior reader returns.

The texts that the library holds are few in number and well established, and the texts include the older scriptures of several of the world's faiths as well as certain secular works of careful philosophy. The chronicler notes that the doctrine does not maintain an extensive library, for the doctrine values the long reading of a few texts above the brief reading of many. The texts that the doctrine has chosen are regarded by the masters as the foundation of the discipline, and the additions to the library are made with great care and only after the conference of the senior readers has approved the addition. The chronicler notes that the additions to the library are recorded in the register of the cloister, and the register is the measure of the cloister's reputation for careful administration.

## The Schedules of Study

The doctrine maintains at the cloister a schedule of study that the readers observe throughout the year. The schedule is divided into the seasons of reading and the seasons of conference, and the seasons alternate in a cycle that the doctrine has refined over generations. The seasons of reading are set apart for the long reading of the chosen text, and the seasons of conference are set apart for the discussion of the readings at the conference of the readers. The chronicler notes that the schedule is observed without exception, and the breaking of the schedule is regarded by the masters as a failure of discipline.

A reader is admitted to a cloister after a probation of some seasons, during which the candidate attends the readings and the conferences and contributes to the common maintenance of the library. The probation is conducted under the direction of a senior reader, and the senior reader reports to the conference upon the candidate's progress at the close of the probation. The chronicler notes that the probation is the principal means by which the doctrine maintains the standard of the reading, and the doctrine regards the probation as a worthy complement to the long reading. The cloister's probation is not a test of memory but a test of attention, and the candidate who passes the probation is admitted to the cloister as a reader.

## The Long Reading

The principal discipline of the doctrine is the long reading, a sustained practice of attention in which the student returns again and again to a chosen text over a span of years. The doctrine teaches that the same text, read with patience over time, becomes a different text to the reader who has grown in the meantime, and the long reading is the chief instrument by which the discipline of nouns is acquired. The chronicler notes that the long reading is undertaken with the counsel of a senior reader and is brought to the conference at intervals to be discussed and corrected, and the long reading is the measure of the reader's progress in the doctrine.

A long reading is begun with a ceremony that is conducted in the library of the cloister, and the ceremony is attended by the senior reader who is to counsel the reader and by such other readers as wish to be present. The chosen text is taken from the shelf and placed in the reader's hands, and the senior reader speaks a brief blessing in the ancient tongue that the doctrine has preserved. The reader is permitted thereafter to take the text to the reader's chamber and to keep the text for the duration of the long reading, and the reader is required to bring the text to the conference at the appointed intervals. The chronicler notes that the long reading is the chief observance of the cloister, and the doctrine regards the long reading as the foundation of the reader's calling.

## The Conference of Readers

The conference of readers is the social observance of the doctrine, and it is held at the cloister at regular intervals throughout the year. The conference is conducted with great formality, and the order of speaking is observed without variation. The junior readers open with a passage from their current long reading, and the senior readers respond in turn, and the conference closes with a brief silence in which the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named. The chronicler notes that the conference is regarded by the doctrine as the second of the principal observances of the cloister, and the doctrine holds that the conference is the measure of the reader's progress in the discipline.

The conference is open to visitors of good faith, and the doctrine welcomes those who come from other traditions and wish to attend. The teachers caution visitors against mistaking the conference for a debate, for the doctrine does not maintain that any of its readings is the last word, and the conference is held to be a place of correction rather than of advocacy. A visitor who conducts himself with patience and attention will be received with consideration, and the masters see in the visitor's presence a chance to test their own readings against the attention of someone who does not share the doctrine's habits of thought. The chronicler notes that the conference is the only occasion upon which the readers of the several cloisters meet in any number, and the conference is the source of the unity of the doctrine.

## The Cloister and the Wider Community

The cloister is bound to the wider community of the river towns by a customary relation that the chroniclers hold to be a part of the discipline of the doctrine. The town is required to maintain the cloister in provisions and in the small endowment that the masters require, and the cloister is required to maintain the town in counsel and in the instruction of the young. The chronicler notes that this mutual obligation has done much to preserve the harmony of the river towns, where the cloister and the town are accustomed to regard one another as parts of a single order. The breach of the mutual obligation is visited by the censure of the community, and the censure is delivered by the elders of the town in the presence of the masters of the cloister.

The cloister is also the home of the readers who maintain the discipline in the wider community of the river towns, and the readers are recruited from among the senior readers of the cloister. The readers travel between the cloisters and the towns, and the readers are the principal point of contact between the cloister and the wider community. The chronicler notes that the readers are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat, and the readers are the measure of the cloister's reputation for careful administration.

## Relation to Other Cloisters

The Cloisters of Reading maintain a correspondence with the cloisters of the kindred traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the correspondence is the means by which the doctrine has preserved its kinship with the [Path of the Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/path-of-the-sunless-veils) and the other contemplative orders of the continent. A senior reader of the [Doctrine of the Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/doctrine-of-the-unwritten-name) will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the cloister of the Veils, and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of readers, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. The chronicler notes that the correspondence is the measure of the doctrine's openness to the wider world, and the doctrine regards the correspondence as a privilege rather than a claim.

The doctrine has been especially close to the cloisters of the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame), whose hearth-keepers teach a kindred humility before the single light, and the two traditions have exchanged senior readers more than once in recent generations. The chronicler notes that the correspondence is preserved in the library of each cloister, and the correspondence is the source of the doctrine's knowledge of the wider world. The masters caution against the temptation to make a display of the correspondence, for the doctrine regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very veil the doctrine counsels against.

## Significance

The Cloisters of Reading are the houses of the [Doctrine of the Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/doctrine-of-the-unwritten-name), and the cloisters are the seat of the long reading, the schedules of study, the libraries, and the conference of the readers. The cloisters are quiet establishments in the river towns and the older cloister districts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the cloisters are supported by the labour of the readers and by the small endowment that the towns provide. The chronicler notes that the cloisters have been the source of the doctrine's slow growth along the trade routes of the river country, and the cloisters are a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the academies. The doctrine asks of its readers that they attend to the difference between naming and pointing, and it offers in return the long discipline by which the attention is refined and the language is brought, slowly, into better service.
