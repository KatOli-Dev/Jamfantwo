## The Standing Patrol of the Sahrani Southern Waters

The Inner Sea Watch is the standing patrol maintained by the city of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and its neighbours along the sheltered waters of the southern reach of the Greyring, from the mole of Khalisar to the southern mouth of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden). The Watch is the oldest continuous maritime body of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast, and its officers answer jointly to the city council of Khalisar and to the magistrates of the smaller ports that share the waters. The Watch keeps the channels free of wreck and floating debris, directs vessels in and out of the harbours, supervises the lading and unlading of cargo at the public quays, and collects the small dues by which it is sustained. The Watch has its own barges, beacons, and a small fleet of light galleys for the pursuit of offenders.

The Watch is a body of practitioners rather than a council of deliberation. The commander is a senior captain chosen for a term of three years by the council of Khalisar. [Esryn Innersea](/content/people/notable/esryn-innersea) presently holds the command, having succeeded a line of pilots that reaches back to the founding of the Watch. The commanders of the smaller ports may each nominate a lieutenant who serves aboard the flag-barge and represents the smaller port in daily orders. The Watch numbers some forty officers and seamen in permanent service, augmented in the season of the great grain-ships by a levy of landsmen from the coastal villages.

## The Commander, Lieutenants, and the Ranks

The Watch is composed of a commander, two lieutenants, four senior pilots, eight junior pilots, and the seamen and deckhands who row the galleys and tend the beacons. The commander is responsible for the overall conduct of the patrol and the quarterly report submitted to the council. The lieutenants command the two divisions of the patrol: the inner division that works the harbours of Khalisar and the outer division that works the southern waters of the strait. The senior pilots are veterans who have served the Watch for at least ten years and who hold the rank entitling them to command a vessel without the supervision of a lieutenant.

The junior pilots are the rising officers of the Watch, drawn from the sons of pilots, from the wards of the council of Khalisar, and from the apprentices of the merchant guilds. A junior pilot must serve for at least five years before consideration for the senior rank, including at least two winters upon the outer division. The seamen and deckhands are the working force of the Watch, drawn from the coastal villages and from the fishermen who ply the strait. The Watch has been an avenue of advancement for the sons of the fishermen, and several commanders have been born to no greater station than a deckhand of the outer division.

## Mooring, Ballast, and the Lighthouses of the Strait

The Watch keeps a barge at the principal quays of Khalisar, carrying a junior pilot at all hours. The pilot directs the mooring and unmooring of merchant vessels, sees that they are properly fendered against the quay, and reports any damage that occurs in the lading or unlading. The pilot also supervises the discharge of ballast, which is dumped at a designated ground below the harbour, and reports any vessel that discharges ballast within the harbour. The discharge of ballast within the harbour has been a long-standing offence, and the Watch has the power to fine the master and detain the vessel until the fine is paid.

The Watch also maintains the lighthouses and beacons that mark the channels of the strait. The principal lighthouse stands upon the southern mole of Khalisar, and the keeper is a senior seaman of the Watch who has been relieved of duty afloat by reason of age or injury. The keeper maintains the fire, trims the wicks at dusk, and signals the state of the tide by the colour of the light shown above the doorway of the lantern room. The Watch maintains smaller beacons upon the rocks and shoals of the strait, tended by the fishermen of the nearby villages under the supervision of the outer division.

## The Light Galleys and the Pursuit of Wreckers

The outer division of the Watch patrols the southern waters of the strait, from the mole of Khalisar to the southern mouth. The patrol consists of two light galleys that cruise in company, one upon the eastern shore and one upon the western, keeping a steady watch for vessels in distress, for wreckage that may foul the anchors of merchantmen, and for the small boats of wreckers who sometimes lure vessels upon the rocks. The galleys are armed with a single bow-chaser and a stand of pikes for boarding, and the officers of the Watch are authorised to detain any vessel that refuses to heave to at their signal.

The Watch has the power to arrest any person found in the act of luring a vessel upon the rocks and the duty to bring the offender before the magistrate of the nearest port. Wreckers have been a long-standing problem of the southern waters, and the Watch has prosecuted several cases in each generation. The Watch also has the duty to recover the cargo of any vessel wrecked upon the coast, holding the cargo until the owner can be found or until the cargo is sold by public auction and the proceeds deposited in the court.

## The Dues Collected from Every Vessel

The Watch is sustained by a small due collected from every vessel that uses the harbours of the Sahrani coast. The due is set by the council of Khalisar in consultation with the smaller ports and collected by the harbour-master at the time of unlading. The due pays for the maintenance of the lighthouses, beacons, and galleys, and the wages of the officers and seamen. The due has been stable for several generations and is one of the most carefully audited of the public revenues of the region.

The Watch keeps its own accounts, audited twice each year by a committee of the council. The committee reports to the council upon the state of the Watch and the sufficiency of the dues and has the power to recommend an increase or decrease of the dues. The Watch has been one of the most efficient public bodies of the Sahrani coast, and its small cost has been repaid many times over by the safety it provides to the commerce of the region.

## The Code of the Watch and the Courtesy to Masters

The Watch maintains a strict code of discipline enforced by the commander and the lieutenants. An officer found drunk upon duty is dismissed from the service; an officer found guilty of theft is dismissed and prosecuted; an officer found guilty of cowardice in the face of the enemy is dismissed and disgraced. The code has been severe, and the severity has been justified by the danger of the waters and by the trust placed in the Watch by the merchants and the magistrates.

The Watch also maintains a code of courtesy observed toward the vessels that use the harbours. The pilot of the barge greets the master of a vessel as the master comes ashore and offers such assistance as may be required. The pilot escorts the master to the office of the harbour-master and remains at the master's side until the business is completed. This courtesy has been the source of much of the goodwill the Watch enjoys and is one of the reasons the merchants of the coast are content to pay the dues of the Watch.

## The Oldest Maritime Body of the Sahrani Coast

The Inner Sea Watch is one of the oldest and most respected local governing bodies of the known world. Its officers and seamen are drawn from the coastal villages and the merchant families, and its jurisdiction covers the waters that have been the principal highway of the Sahrani coast since the founding of Khalisar. The Watch has kept the channels safe, recovered the cargoes of the wrecked, and prosecuted the wreckers and smugglers who would prey upon the commerce of the region. The Watch has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the Sahrani coast and is held in high esteem by every magistrate and every merchant of the region.
