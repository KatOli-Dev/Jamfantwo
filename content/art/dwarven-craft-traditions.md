## The Mountain as Workshop

The Dwarven Craft Traditions are the body of practice and custom that governs the working of materials in the [dwarven](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) communities of the known world. The traditions are not a single set of rules but a collection of approaches that have developed over the centuries in the different holds and regions where dwarves dwell. The common thread that unites the traditions is the dwarven relationship with the mountain, the source of the materials that the dwarven craftsmen work and the context within which the work is done.

The dwarf regards the mountain as a living thing, a being that provides the metals and the stones that the dwarven people need and that demands respect in return. The dwarven craftsman does not take from the mountain without giving something back, and the traditions of dwarven craft include ceremonies of thanks and propitiation that are performed before the extraction of materials and after the completion of a work. The relationship between the dwarf and the mountain is the foundation of the dwarven approach to craft.

## The Philosophy of the Material

Dwarven craft is founded upon a philosophy of the material that is distinct from the approaches of other peoples. The dwarf believes that every material has an inherent character, a nature that the craftsman must understand before the working can begin. The metal or the stone is not a passive substance that the craftsman shapes to an arbitrary will but a partner in the work, a material that offers possibilities and imposes limitations that the craftsman must respect.

The dwarven craftsman therefore spends a long time in the study of the material before the working begins. The apprentice learns to identify the grain of the stone and the structure of the metal, to recognise the signs that indicate the quality of the material and the way it will behave under the tool. The apprentice learns that the material will reveal its nature only to the patient observer, and that the craftsman who rushes the work will produce a result that is inferior to the work of the craftsman who waits.

The philosophy of the material is also expressed in the dwarven approach to the design of the object. The dwarven craftsman does not impose a design upon the material but allows the design to emerge from the material, selecting the metal or the stone that is best suited to the purpose and shaping it in a manner that follows the natural grain and structure. The result is an object that appears to have grown from the material rather than to have been forced upon it, and the object bears the marks of the material as well as the marks of the craftsman.

## The Techniques of Dwarven Metalwork

The metalwork of the dwarven holds is the most accomplished in the known world, and the techniques that the dwarven smiths have developed are the foundation of the dwarven reputation. The dwarven smiths work in all the metals that the mountain provides, from the common iron to the precious gold, and they have developed methods for the extraction, the alloying, and the shaping of metal that are unknown to the smiths of other peoples.

The dwarven technique for the extraction of metal from ore is the most advanced in the known world. The dwarven smelters use furnaces that are built into the natural vents of the mountain, using the heat of the earth to assist in the smelting process. The furnaces are designed to produce a temperature that is higher than the temperature that a surface furnace can achieve, and the dwarven smelters can therefore extract metal from ores that the surface smelters cannot work.

The dwarven technique for the alloying of metals is also more advanced than the techniques of other peoples. The dwarven smiths have developed a range of alloys that produce properties of strength, flexibility, and resistance to corrosion that are not available in the pure metals. The dwarven bronze that is produced in the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) is the standard of quality for the bronze of the known world, and the dwarven steel that is produced in the forges of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) is the finest that can be obtained.

## The Techniques of Dwarven Stonework

The stonework of the dwarven holds is as accomplished as the metalwork, and the techniques that the dwarven masons have developed are the foundation of the architecture of the holds. The dwarven masons work in the granite and the marble of the mountains, using tools of dwarven steel that can cut the hardest stone with precision.

The dwarven technique for the cutting of stone is based on the use of the saw and the drill, tools that are powered by the water of the mountain streams and that can cut through stone with a speed that the human mason cannot match. The dwarven saws are made from a flexible band of steel that is set with fragments of diamond, the hardest material known to the dwarven craftsmen. The dwarven drills are made from the same materials, and they are used to create the holes that are required for the fixing of the stone and for the decoration of the surface.

The dwarven technique for the finishing of stone is also distinctive. The dwarven masons use abrasives of graded fineness, from the coarse grit that removes the marks of the saw to the fine powder that produces a surface that is as smooth as glass. The dwarven masons also use techniques for the polishing of stone that produce a surface that reflects the light and that reveals the colour and the grain of the material in its full beauty.

## The Inlay Art and Dwarven Decoration

The [Inlay Art](/content/culture/inlay-art) is the principal decorative tradition of the dwarven communities, and the techniques of inlay are practised by every dwarven craftsman who works in metal or stone. The inlay of metal into stone and stone into metal is the foundation of dwarven decorative art, and the works that are produced under the tradition are among the most valued of dwarven exports.

The dwarven approach to decoration is distinct from the approach of other peoples. The decoration of a dwarven object is not applied to the surface after the object is complete but is integrated into the structure of the object from the beginning. The inlay is not an addition to the object but a part of it, and the removal of the decoration would damage the object itself.

The patterns of the dwarven decorative tradition are based on the geometric forms that the dwarven craftsmen observe in the natural world. The patterns of the crystal and the snowflake, the structure of the honeycomb and the shell, the arrangement of the leaves on the branch and the petals on the flower all provide inspiration for the dwarven designer. The patterns are repeated and interwoven in the manner of the dwarven decorative style, and the result is a surface that is both ordered and infinitely varied.

## The Transmission of the Craft

The transmission of the dwarven craft traditions from one generation to the next occurs through the system of apprenticeship that has served the dwarven communities for centuries. The apprentice is bound to a master craftsman at the age of twelve or thirteen, and the period of apprenticeship lasts for no less than twenty years. The apprentice learns the techniques of the craft through the observation and the imitation of the master, and through the performance of the simpler tasks that the master assigns.

The master does not teach the apprentice in the manner of a schoolmaster but allows the apprentice to learn through the work itself. The apprentice is given the materials and the tools and is allowed to make mistakes, for the dwarven craftsmen believe that the mistakes are as valuable as the successes in the formation of the craftsman's skill. The master intervenes only when the apprentice is in danger of harming himself or the material, or when the apprentice has made a mistake that cannot be corrected.

The dwarven craft traditions are also transmitted through the works that the masters have produced, preserved in the halls of the holds. The works serve as reference for the apprentices who are learning the craft and as the standard by which the masters are judged. The works are displayed in the halls as a measure of the achievement of the masters who produced them, and they form the foundation of the reputation of the hold in the surface world.
