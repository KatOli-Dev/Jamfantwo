## The Houses of the Unwritten Name

The Reading Cloisters are the houses of the Unwritten Name, quiet establishments where the order of readers maintains libraries, schedules of study, and conferences of senior and junior scholars. The cloisters are found chiefly in the river towns and the older cloister districts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Unlike monasteries in the older sense, the doctrine does not require its readers to withdraw from the world. Many of the most senior scholars have kept their households and trades while pursuing the long study, and the cloisters are places of meeting rather than retreat.

The cloisters are the seat of the masters of reading, the senior readers charged with overseeing the libraries and instructing the juniors. The masters hold no worldly authority and are counseled against the temptation to mere governance. They maintain themselves by their hands and by the contributions of the readers who attend the cloisters. The cloisters are supported by a small endowment and by the labour of the readers themselves. The doctrine teaches that the work of the household is itself a discipline of attention that complements the work of the study.

## The First Community and the Founder

The Reading Cloisters trace their foundation to a small community of readers who withdrew from the court of an early Aethelaran kingdom and devoted themselves to comparing the older texts that had begun to circulate among the libraries of the time. The founder, a scholar remembered as [Orin Aldmere](/content/people/historical/orin-aldmere), held that the texts of the age contradicted one another in their accounts of the divine and that these contradictions were not errors to be resolved but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. Whether Orin Aldmere was a single figure or a succession of senior readers is left open, and the doctrine regards the question as one of the lesser mysteries.

The first cloister was a single chamber set aside for keeping the older texts and for the conference of the readers who had withdrawn from the court. The community's successors added a second chamber and then a third, and the cloister grew in the measure of the texts brought to it and the number of readers admitted to the conference. The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of the study in their home towns. Though each cloister is modelled upon the first, each is governed by the same discipline that the first community established.

## The Central Chamber and the Few Chosen Texts

The central observance of the doctrine is the long reading, and the library is the room in which it is conducted. The library is the principal chamber of the cloister, lined with shelves of plain wood upon which copies of the older texts are kept. The library is open to readers who have been admitted to a long reading and closed to all others. It is regarded by the doctrine as the heart of the cloister, the place to which every novice aspires and to which every senior reader returns.

The texts held by the library are few in number and well established, including the older scriptures of several of the world's faiths and certain secular works of careful philosophy. The doctrine does not maintain an extensive library, for it values the long reading of a few texts above the brief reading of many. The texts the doctrine has chosen are regarded by the masters as the foundation of the discipline, and additions to the library are made with great care, only after the conference of the senior readers has approved the addition. These additions are recorded in the register of the cloister.

## The Alternating Seasons of Reading and Conference

The doctrine maintains at the cloister a schedule of study observed throughout the year. The schedule is divided into seasons of reading and seasons of conference, alternating in a cycle refined over generations. The seasons of reading are set apart for the long reading of the chosen text, and the seasons of conference are set apart for discussing the readings at the conference of the readers. The schedule is observed without exception, and breaking it is regarded by the masters as a failure of discipline.

A reader is admitted to a cloister after a probation of some seasons, during which the candidate attends readings and conferences and contributes to the common maintenance of the library. The probation is conducted under the direction of a senior reader, who reports to the conference upon the candidate's progress at the close of the probation. The probation is the principal means by which the doctrine maintains the standard of the reading. It is not a test of memory but a test of attention, and the candidate who passes is admitted as a reader.

## The Sustained Practice of Attentive Reading

The principal discipline of the doctrine is the long reading, a sustained practice of attention in which the student returns again and again to a chosen text over a span of years. The doctrine teaches that the same text, read with patience over time, becomes a different text to the reader who has grown in the meantime. The long reading is the chief instrument by which the discipline of nouns is acquired. It is undertaken with the counsel of a senior reader and brought to the conference at intervals to be discussed and corrected.

A long reading is begun with a ceremony in the library of the cloister, attended by the senior reader who is to counsel the reader and by such other readers as wish to be present. The chosen text is taken from the shelf and placed in the reader's hands, and the senior reader speaks a brief blessing in the ancient tongue preserved by the doctrine. The reader is permitted to take the text to the reader's chamber and keep it for the duration of the long reading, and is required to bring the text to the conference at appointed intervals. The long reading is the chief observance of the cloister, the foundation of the reader's calling.

## The Formal Gathering of the Readers

The conference of readers is the social observance of the doctrine, held at the cloister at regular intervals throughout the year. The conference is conducted with great formality, and the order of speaking is observed without variation. The junior readers open with a passage from their current long reading, the senior readers respond in turn, and the conference closes with a brief silence in which the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named. The conference is regarded by the doctrine as the second of the principal observances of the cloister, the measure of the reader's progress in the discipline.

The conference is open to visitors of good faith, and the doctrine welcomes those from other traditions who wish to attend. Teachers caution visitors against mistaking the conference for a debate, for the doctrine does not maintain that any of its readings is the last word; the conference is a place of correction rather than advocacy. A visitor who conducts himself with patience and attention will be received with consideration, and the masters see in the visitor's presence a chance to test their own readings against the attention of someone who does not share the doctrine's habits of thought. The conference is the only occasion upon which the readers of the several cloisters meet in any number, and it is the source of the unity of the doctrine.

## The Mutual Obligation Between Cloister and Town

The cloister is bound to the wider community of the river towns by a customary relation held to be part of the discipline of the doctrine. The town is required to maintain the cloister in provisions and in the small endowment the masters require, and the cloister is required to maintain the town in counsel and in the instruction of the young. This mutual obligation has done much to preserve the harmony of the river towns, where the cloister and the town regard one another as parts of a single order. The breach of this mutual obligation is visited by the censure of the community, delivered by the elders of the town in the presence of the masters of the cloister.

The cloister is also the home of the readers who maintain the discipline in the wider community of the river towns, recruited from among the senior readers of the cloister. These readers travel between the cloisters and the towns and are the principal point of contact between the cloister and the wider community. They are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat.

## Kindred Traditions and the Correspondence

The Reading Cloisters maintain correspondence with the cloisters of kindred traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), the means by which the doctrine has preserved its kinship with the Sunless Veils and other contemplative orders of the continent. A senior reader of the Unwritten Name will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the [Veil Cloister](/content/religion/veil-cloister), and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of readers, each welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. This correspondence reflects the doctrine's openness to the wider world, a privilege rather than a claim.

The doctrine has been especially close to the cloisters of the Still Flame, whose hearth-keepers teach a kindred humility before the single light, and the two traditions have exchanged senior readers more than once in recent generations. The correspondence is preserved in the library of each cloister and is the source of the doctrine's knowledge of the wider world. The masters caution against the temptation to make a display of the correspondence, for the doctrine regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very veil the doctrine counsels against.

## The Quiet Counterweight of the Unwritten Name

The Reading Cloisters are the houses of the Unwritten Name, the seat of the long reading, the schedules of study, the libraries, and the conference of the readers. They are quiet establishments in the river towns and the older cloister districts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), supported by the labour of the readers and by the small endowment the towns provide. The cloisters have been the source of the doctrine's slow growth along the trade routes of the river country, a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the academies. The doctrine asks of its readers that they attend to the difference between naming and pointing, and it offers in return the long discipline by which the attention is refined and the language is brought, slowly, into better service.
