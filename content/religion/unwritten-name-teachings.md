## The Discipline of the Nouns

The Unwritten Name is a philosophical and contemplative doctrine that centres upon the discipline of the nouns, the careful attention to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it. The doctrine teaches that language is a tool of pointing, and that the proper use of language is to direct the attention of the hearer toward the thing spoken of and not to substitute the word for the thing. The discipline of the nouns trains the practitioner to use names with the precision and without the attachment, to hold the names lightly, and to attend to the silence that surrounds the naming as carefully as to the naming itself.

The central figure of the doctrine is Orin Aldmere, the scholar who is remembered as the founder of the first community of readers. Orin Aldmere taught that the older texts of the world contradicted one another in their accounts of the divine and that these contradictions were not errors to be resolved but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. The teaching that arose from this recognition is the discipline of the nouns, and the discipline is the foundation upon which the practices of the Reading Cloisters are built.

## The Teaching of the Limit of the Language

The Unwritten Name teaches that the language is a limited tool, capable of pointing toward the truth but not of capturing it. The word is not the thing, and the name is not the reality that the name indicates. The practitioner who mistakes the word for the thing is caught in the veil of the naming, and the discipline of the nouns is the means by which the veil is lifted.

The doctrine does not teach that the language is useless or that the words should be abandoned. The language is the tool that the human being has for the communication and the thought, and the tool must be used. The discipline teaches that the tool must be used with the awareness of its limits, and that the practitioner must hold the words lightly, ready to release them when the words have served the purpose of the pointing.

The limit of the language is most apparent when the practitioner attempts to name the divine. The Unwritten Name teaches that every name for the divine is, in the moment of its utterance, a name for something less than the divine. The name that is spoken is a human word, limited by the human experience and the human understanding, and the divine that the word attempts to name is beyond the reach of the human word.

## The Practice of the Long Reading

The principal practice of the Unwritten Name is the long reading, a sustained discipline of the attention in which the reader returns again and again to a chosen text over a span of years. The doctrine teaches that the same text, read with the patience over time, becomes a different text to the reader who has grown in the meantime. The contradictions and the gaps in the text are not obstacles to the understanding but the very places where the understanding must be sought.

The long reading is begun with a ceremony in the library of the cloister, attended by the senior reader who is to counsel the reader and by such other readers as wish to be present. The chosen text is taken from the shelf and placed in the reader's hands, and the senior reader speaks a brief blessing in the ancient tongue preserved by the doctrine. The reader is permitted to take the text to the reader's chamber and keep it for the duration of the long reading, and the reader is required to bring the text to the conference at the appointed intervals.

The long reading is the chief instrument by which the discipline of the nouns is acquired. The reader who returns to the same text over the years learns to attend to the words with the precision, to notice the shifts in the meaning that the passage of the time reveals, and to hold the words lightly as the pointers toward the understanding that the words cannot contain.

## The Teaching of the Conference

The conference of the readers is the social observance of the doctrine, held at the cloister at the regular intervals throughout the year. The junior readers open with a passage from the current long reading, the senior readers respond in turn, and the conference closes with a brief silence in which the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named.

The conference is not a debate, and the doctrine does not maintain that any of the readings is the last word. The conference is a place of the correction rather than the advocacy, and the readers attend to one another's readings with the same attention that they bring to their own. The conference is the occasion on which the readers test the readings against the attention of the other readers, and the correction that arises from the testing is the refinement of the understanding.

The doctrine teaches that the conference is the means by which the solitary reading is brought into the community of the readers. The reader who has read the text alone through the season of the reading brings the discoveries to the conference, and the discoveries are tested and refined by the attention of the other readers. The conference is the source of the unity of the doctrine across the river towns and the cloister districts.

## The Teaching of the Unspeakable

The Unwritten Name teaches that there is a reality that cannot be named, and the doctrine refers to this reality as the Unspeakable. The Unspeakable is not a deity in the manner of the Sea Mother or a stillness in the manner of the Veiled One; the Unspeakable is the limit of the language, the point beyond which the words cannot reach.

The doctrine does not claim to know the nature of the Unspeakable, for the claim of the knowledge would be the claim that the Unspeakable can be named. The doctrine teaches that the Unspeakable is to be approached through the silence that follows the conference and through the gaps in the text that the long reading reveals. The silence is the most appropriate form of the acknowledgment, for the silence does not attempt to name what cannot be named.

## The Teaching of the Kinship with the Other Traditions

The Unwritten Name maintains the correspondence with the Sunless Veils and the Still Flame, and the doctrine teaches that the three traditions are kindred paths that approach the same mystery from the different directions. The Sunless Veils approach through the discipline of the silence and the withdrawal, the Still Flame approaches through the tending of the hearth and the warmth of the hospitality, and the Unwritten Name approaches through the discipline of the nouns and the attention to the language.

The doctrine does not claim that the path of the Unwritten Name is the only path or the superior path. The doctrine teaches that the kindred traditions are the different forms of the attending to the divine that the different temperaments and the different conditions require, and that the practitioner who follows the path with the sincerity is already approaching the truth that the path reveals.

## The Teaching of the Growth of the Doctrine

The Unwritten Name is not a faith that seeks the converts or that claims the exclusive possession of the truth. The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by the scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of the study in the home towns. The cloisters have been the source of the doctrine's slow growth over the centuries, a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the academies.

The doctrine asks of the readers that they attend to the difference between naming and pointing, and it offers in return the long discipline by which the attention is refined and the language is brought, slowly, into the better service. The Unwritten Name does not promise the salvation or the enlightenment or any of the rewards that the temples and the mystery cults offer; it promises only the discipline itself, and the patient work of the long reading, and the silence that follows the conference when the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named.
