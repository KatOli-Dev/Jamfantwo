## The Two Halves of the Calling

The orders of the Shield and Song are the institutions in which the calling of the order is organised and sustained. The order is divided into two complementary halves: the shield-half, which is the bearing of hardship for the protection of others, and the song-half, which is the keeping of memory and the maintenance of the community's meaning through the arts of voice and instrument. The shield-half is embodied in the Standing Watch, the body of watchmen who maintain the holding of the beacon and the patrol of the border in the northern frontier. The song-half is embodied in the Memory Houses, the quiet establishments where the singers of the order keep the local songs and traditions and maintain the evening review.

The orders are found chiefly in the northern regions of Aethelar, where the frontier between settled land and the ancient forests requires the discipline of the watch, and in the market towns and river crossings of the temperate lowlands, where the communities value the keeping of memory. The orders are not religions of the kind that maintain temples or doctrines of the kind that the academies cultivate; they are callings, disciplines of bearing and singing that the practitioner undertakes for the duration of life.

## The Standing Watch

The Standing Watch is the principal embodiment of the shield-half of the calling. The watchmen are not soldiers who go to war but guardians who hold the line against the dangers that emerge from the deeper woodland. The discipline of the watch is the discipline of endurance, and the watchmen are trained to withstand the cold and the dark and the long silences of the frontier without losing the alertness that the watch requires.

The watch is organised into the posts that are spaced along the frontier, each post manned by a company of the watchmen who serve for a season of the year. The watchmen are drawn from the communities of the northern region, and the service in the watch is a duty that the community regards as the highest honour. The watchman who completes the season of the service returns to the community with the knowledge that the frontier has been held and that the inmost has been secure.

The watch posts are simple structures of the timber and the stone, built at the strategic points where the frontier can be observed and the approach of the danger can be detected. The watch post is the home of the watchman through the season, and the watchman maintains the post with the same care that the householder maintains the home.

## The Memory Houses

The Memory Houses are the quiet establishments of the song-half of the calling, where the singers of the order keep the memory of the communities they serve. The houses are found in the larger market towns and at the more important river crossings, kept by the order's singers who are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat.

The Memory House is a building of the stone and the timber, with a meeting-hall for the evening review and a library for the preservation of the songs that the order has gathered. The house maintains the schedule of the singing that is observed throughout the year, with the seasons of the teaching and the seasons of the travel that alternate in the cycle refined over the generations.

The singers who serve the Memory Houses are required to learn by heart a substantial body of the songs and to maintain the accuracy of their transmission from one generation to the next. The tradition holds that a song learned imperfectly is a song lost, and the singers are trained accordingly. The training of a singer is conducted by a senior singer of the nearest house and is regarded as the chief instrument by which the song-half is preserved.

## The Evening Review

The central observance of the order is the evening review, conducted at the Memory Houses or at the watch posts of the Standing Watch. At the close of the day, the household or the company recalls together the events of the day, giving thanks for the mercies and observing the losses. A short song of account is sung, naming the day's sick and recovered, the day's fed and unfed, the day's spared and the day's fallen.

The evening review is the chief instrument by which the two halves of the calling are united in a single observance. The watchman who has held the line through the day and the singer who has kept the songs of the community both attend the review, and the accounting of the day's mercies and losses is the occasion on which the shield-half and the song-half are seen to be two aspects of a single calling. The order teaches that the review is meant to keep the account honest, so that the community may know what it has done and may be moved to mend what has been done amiss.

## The Orders and the Kindred Traditions

The Shield and Song maintains correspondence with the Still Flame and the Unwritten Name, the kindred traditions of the continent, and the three traditions have exchanged practitioners for many generations. A singer of the Shield and Song will sometimes attend the evening review of a hearth-hall of the Still Flame, and a hearth-keeper will sometimes attend the singing at a house of memory.

The order has also exchanged singers with the Forged Flame, whose hearth-keepers teach a discipline of the workshop that the order regards as a worthy complement to the singing of the Memory Houses. The exchange has enriched both orders and has deepened the understanding of the relation between the work of the hands and the work of the voice.

## The Calling and the Life

The Shield and Song is a calling rather than a profession, and the orders do not offer worldly advancement or the respect of the courts. The orders ask of the practitioners that they bear what they are called to bear and sing what they are called to sing, and they promise in return that the bearing and the singing are themselves a share in the Watcher's keeping of the world. The practitioner who undertakes the calling does not seek reward or recognition, and the order teaches that the reward of the calling is the calling itself.

The orders have endured through the centuries because the need for the calling has endured. The frontier still requires the watch, and the communities still require the memory that the singers preserve. The orders grow slowly, attracting those who are drawn to the discipline of bearing and singing, and the practices have been maintained through the generations without seeking the world's adherence or the world's approval.
