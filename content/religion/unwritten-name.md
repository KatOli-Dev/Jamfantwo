## The Doctrine of the Discipline of Nouns

The Unwritten Name is a philosophical and contemplative doctrine centred upon the discipline of nouns, the careful attention to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it. The doctrine maintains the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading), quiet establishments where the order of readers pursues the long reading of chosen texts and the discipline of the conference. The Unwritten Name is neither a religion in the sense that the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame) is a religion nor a mystery cult of the kind found in the older temples; it is a discipline of attention, a practice of language, and a way of approaching the divine through the recognition that every name for the divine is, in the moment of its utterance, a name for something less than the divine.

The central figure of the doctrine is [Orin Aldmere](/content/people/historical/orin-aldmere), the scholar remembered as the founder of the first community of readers. [Orin Aldmere](/content/people/historical/orin-aldmere) taught that the older texts of the world contradicted one another in their accounts of the divine and that these contradictions were not errors to be resolved but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. The teaching that arose from this recognition is the discipline of nouns: the practice of using names while remaining aware that the name is not the thing named, and of attending to the difference between naming and pointing with the same care that a craftsman attends to the difference between the tool and the work.

## The Discipline of Nouns

The discipline of nouns is the principal teaching of the Unwritten Name, and it is the foundation upon which the practices of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) are built. The doctrine holds that language is a tool of pointing, and that the proper use of language is to direct the attention of the hearer toward the thing spoken of and not to substitute the word for the thing. The discipline of nouns trains the practitioner to use names with precision and without attachment, to hold the names lightly, and to attend to the silence that surrounds the naming as carefully as to the naming itself.

The discipline is acquired through the long reading, a sustained practice of attention in which the student returns again and again to a chosen text over a span of years. The doctrine teaches that the same text, read with patience over time, becomes a different text to the reader who has grown in the meantime, and that the contradictions and gaps in the text are not obstacles to understanding but the very places where the understanding must be sought. The long reading is the chief instrument by which the discipline of nouns is acquired, and it is undertaken with the counsel of a senior reader who guides the student through the difficulties and the discoveries that the long practice reveals.

## The Reading Cloisters

The [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) are the houses of the Unwritten Name, the places where the libraries are kept, the schedules of study are observed, and the conferences of readers are conducted. The cloisters are found chiefly in the river towns and the older cloister districts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and they are quiet establishments that lack the ornamentation or grandeur of the temples and the academies. The cloisters are places of meeting rather than retreat, for the doctrine does not require its readers to withdraw from the world; many of the most senior scholars have kept their households and trades while pursuing the long study.

The library is the principal chamber of the cloister, lined with shelves of plain wood upon which copies of the older texts are kept. The texts held by the library are few in number and well established, for the doctrine values the long reading of a few texts above the brief reading of many. The texts include the older scriptures of several of the world's faiths and certain secular works of careful philosophy, and additions to the library are made with great care and only after the conference of the senior readers has approved the addition.

## The Conference of Readers

The conference of readers is the social observance of the doctrine, held at the cloister at regular intervals throughout the year. The junior readers open with a passage from their current long reading, the senior readers respond in turn, and the conference closes with a brief silence in which the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named. The conference is not a debate, for the doctrine does not maintain that any of its readings is the last word; it is a place of correction rather than advocacy, and the readers attend to one another's readings with the same attention they bring to their own.

The conference is open to visitors of good faith, and the doctrine welcomes those from other traditions who wish to attend. The masters see in the visitor's presence a chance to test their own readings against the attention of someone who does not share the doctrine's habits of thought, and the exchange is regarded as a valuable discipline in itself. The conference is the only occasion upon which the readers of the several cloisters meet in any number, and it is the source of the unity of the doctrine across the river towns and the cloister districts.

## The Kinship with Kindred Traditions

The Unwritten Name maintains correspondence with the [Sunless Veils](/content/religion/sunless-veils) and the Still Flame, the kindred traditions of the continent, and the three traditions have exchanged practitioners and maintained mutual respect for many generations. A senior reader of the Unwritten Name will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the [Veil Cloister](/content/religion/veil-cloister), and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of readers, each welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. The correspondence is preserved in the library of each cloister and is the source of the doctrine's knowledge of the wider world.

The doctrine has been especially close to the hearth-keepers of the Still Flame, whose teaching of the single light echoes the doctrine's own teaching about the attention that must be paid to the difference between the name and the thing named. The two traditions have exchanged senior readers more than once in recent generations, and the exchange has enriched both traditions without diminishing the distinctiveness of either.

## The Quiet Growth of the Doctrine

The Unwritten Name is not a faith that seeks converts or that claims exclusive possession of the truth. The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of the study in their home towns. The cloisters have been the source of the doctrine's slow growth over the centuries, a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the academies.

The doctrine asks of its readers that they attend to the difference between naming and pointing, and it offers in return the long discipline by which the attention is refined and the language is brought, slowly, into better service. The Unwritten Name does not promise salvation or enlightenment or any of the rewards that the temples and the mystery cults offer; it promises only the discipline itself, and the patient work of the long reading, and the silence that follows the conference when the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named.

## The Place of the Unwritten Name Among the Traditions

The Unwritten Name occupies a particular place among the traditions of the continent, distinct from the faiths that centre upon worship and from the philosophies that centre upon argument. The doctrine is a discipline of attention, and its practices are addressed to the cultivation of the attention through the long reading and the conference. The doctrine does not claim to have discovered the truth about the divine, and it does not claim that its methods are the only methods by which the truth may be approached. It claims only that the discipline of nouns is a worthy practice, and that those who undertake it will find their attention refined and their language brought into better service. The doctrine has endured because it asks little and promises little, and because the discipline it offers is accessible to any who are willing to undertake the long reading and to attend to the silence that follows the naming.
