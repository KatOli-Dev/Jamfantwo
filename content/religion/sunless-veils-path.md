## The Stages of the Approach

The path of the Sunless Veils is a discipline of the withdrawal and the silence that is conducted in stages, each stage preparing the practitioner for the next and each stage lifting a veil that the ordinary world places between the soul and the reality that lies beyond the bright surfaces. The path does not promise that the practitioner will reach the Veiled One, for the approach itself is the practice and the arrival is not to be expected in the present life. The practitioner who undertakes the path learns to be content with the approach and to abandon the expectation of the arrival.

The stages of the path are not fixed in the number or the duration, for the progress of the practitioner depends on the temperament and the capacity of the individual. The senior veiler who guides the novice through the stages is the judge of the readiness of the novice to proceed, and the guidance is given without the imposition of a rigid schedule.

## The Stage of the First Withdrawal

The first stage of the path is the withdrawal from the ordinary occupations of the life for a period of the days or the weeks. The novice withdraws from the household and the trade and the company of the other people, taking up the residence in the quiet chamber that the Veil Cloister maintains. The chamber is furnished with a low couch, a small table, a basin of water, and a single lamp, and the curtains are drawn against the brightness of the day.

The first withdrawal is conducted in the silence, and the novice is permitted the company only of the senior veiler who attends to the discipline without the conversation. The novice is instructed to attend to the thoughts and the feelings that arise in the silence, observing them without the attachment and allowing them to pass without the pursuit.

The first withdrawal is the most difficult of the stages, for the novice is accustomed to the noise and the activity of the ordinary life and the silence is a shock to the senses. The novice who persists through the difficulty begins to experience the stillness that lies beneath the surface of the thoughts, and the experience of the stillness is the first lifting of the veil.

## The Stage of the Long Retreat

The second stage of the path is the long retreat, a discipline of the withdrawal that extends for a season of the weeks or the months. The novice who has completed the first withdrawal is admitted to the long retreat, which is conducted in the same chamber and under the same conditions as the first withdrawal. The novice remains within the chamber for the duration of the retreat, and the curtains are not opened until the close.

The long retreat is the central observance of the path, and it is the chief instrument by which the veils are lifted in the order. The novice who undertakes the long retreat learns to distinguish between the veils that are necessary for the ordinary life and the veils that can be lifted, and the discipline of the retreat is the means by which the lifting is learned.

The long retreat is not a punishment, and the path is careful to mark the difference between the discipline and the punishment. The retreat is the means by which the novice is prepared to approach the Veiled One, and the novice who completes the retreat comes forth quieter than the novice who entered.

## The Stage of the Instruction

The third stage of the path is the instruction in the doctrine of the veils, conducted in the seasons that alternate with the seasons of the withdrawal. The novice attends the readings of the texts that the path has preserved, discussing the teachings with the senior veiler and the other novices.

The instruction is not a lecture in the manner of the academies, for the path values the oral tradition above the written and the experience above the theory. The instruction is a guided reading of the texts, and the novice is expected to bring the attention to the texts and to mark the passages that strike the novice as consequential.

The doctrine of the veils is the teaching that the First Veilers developed in the quiet valleys of the central highlands, and the doctrine has been preserved in the small body of the writings that the path maintains. The doctrine teaches the distinction between the lifting of the veils and the breaking of the veils, and the instruction is the means by which the distinction is learned.

## The Stage of the Wandering

The fourth stage of the path is the wandering, in which the practitioner travels between the cloisters and the hill towns, carrying the discipline of the path to the communities that cannot support a full cloister. The wandering veiler is recognised by the small white staff that is carried and by the custom of speaking softly and at length only when the company requires it.

The wandering is a stage that is undertaken by the senior novices who have completed the long retreat and the instruction. The wandering is regarded as a worthy complement to the retreat, for the wanderer is tested by the conditions of the road and the company of the people who do not share the discipline of the path.

The wanderer returns to the cloister at the close of the wandering, and the return is the occasion for the silence in which the labours are observed without the ceremony. The wanderer who has completed the wandering is admitted to the rank of the senior veiler.

## The Stage of the Senior Veiler

The fifth stage of the path is the admission to the rank of the senior veiler, the keeper of the discipline who guides the novices through the stages of the path. The senior veiler is the practitioner who has completed the long retreat and the instruction and the wandering and who has been recognised by the other senior veilers as fit to guide the next generation.

The senior veiler does not hold the authority over the novices in the manner of the master or the superior. The senior veiler is the attendant who guides the novice through the experience of the retreat without the imposition of the interpretation. The senior veiler's role is to be present in the silence, to attend to the novice's need without the conversation, and to provide the counsel when the novice seeks it.

## The Goal of the Path

The goal of the path is not the arrival at the Veiled One but the approach that the practitioner makes through the stages of the discipline. The practitioner who has completed the path does not claim to have understood the Veiled One or to have reached the stillness that is the Veiled One's nature. The practitioner claims only to have come closer, and the coming closer is the purpose of the path.

The path teaches that the Veiled One is approached through the stillness that the stages of the discipline cultivate, and the practitioner who has lifted the veils in the order comes to rest in that stillness without claiming to have understood what the stillness is. The goal of the path is not the knowledge of the Veiled One but the participation in the stillness that the Veiled One offers.
