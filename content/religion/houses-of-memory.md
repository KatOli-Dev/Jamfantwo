## The Establishments of the Song-Half

The Memory Houses are the quiet establishments of the song-half of the Shield and Song, the order's houses where local songs and traditions are maintained and where the singers of the order keep the memory of the communities they serve. The houses are found in the larger market towns and at the more important river crossings of the temperate lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), kept by the order's singers, who are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat. The houses are regarded by the order as the second of the principal institutions of the calling, the seat of the song-half of the order's work in the same measure that the standing of the watch is the seat of the shield-half.

The order teaches that the divine Watcher is revealed in two complementary aspects: the shield-half and the song-half. The shield-half is the bearing of hardship for the protection of others, and the song-half is the keeping of memory, the recitation of the names of the dead, and the maintenance of the meaning of the community's life through the arts of voice and instrument. The houses of memory are the establishments in which the song-half is observed, and the order holds them in greater esteem than any of the meeting-places or chapels found in the towns of the order's following.

## The Border Riders and the First Singers

The Memory Houses trace their foundation to the company of border riders who served the marches of the older Aethelaran kingdoms and who were accustomed, in their off-watch, to maintain the songs of their forebears and of the deeds they had seen. The traditions of the order speak of a woman called Vaelren of the Long Watch, who is held to have first bound the riders to the practice of singing the day's losses and the day's mercies into a single account at evening. Whether Vaelren was a single figure or a composite of several riders is left to the chronicler's discretion, and the order is content that the binding should be remembered whether or not the binder can be named.

The first house of memory was a single room set aside for keeping the songs learned in the field, furnished with a small hearth and a few wooden benches. The community's successors added a second room and then a third, and the house grew in the measure of the songs brought to it and the number of communities served by the singers. The order grew along the marches and the river country, and each new market town that received the teaching required its own house, for the distance between towns is such that a single house could not serve the whole of the faithful. Though each house is modelled upon the first, each is governed by the same discipline the first company established.

## The Keepers of the Order's Living Memory

The houses of memory are kept by the order's singers, the principal officers of the song-half of the calling. A singer is required to learn by heart a substantial body of songs and to maintain the accuracy of their transmission from one generation to the next. The tradition holds that a song learned imperfectly is a song lost, and the singers are trained accordingly. The training of a singer is conducted by a senior singer of the nearest house and is regarded by the order as the chief instrument by which the song-half is preserved.

The singers travel between the houses and between the orders of the faithful, carrying the songs from one district to the next, and they are regarded as the keepers of the order's living memory. The singers are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat, the mark of the office. The order's houses of memory are open to all who seek a quiet place, and the singers are instructed to teach any who ask without charge, for the maintenance of the community's memory is too important a duty to be turned into a trade.

## The Small Library of Preserved Songs

The houses of memory maintain a small library of the songs the order has preserved, housed in a chamber lined with shelves of plain wood. The library is open to the singers who serve the house and to such visitors as the senior singer permits, closed to all others. It is regarded by the order as the heart of the house, the place to which every novice singer aspires and to which every senior singer returns.

The songs held by the library are few in number and well established, including the older songs of the order and the local songs gathered by the singers from the communities they serve. The order does not maintain an extensive library, for it values the song learned by heart above the song written down and has been slow to commit its songs to the page. The songs the order has chosen to write are regarded by the senior singers as the foundation of the tradition, and additions to the library are made with great care, only after the conference of the senior singers has approved the addition.

## The Cycle of Teaching and Travel

The order maintains at the house a schedule of singing observed throughout the year. The schedule is divided into seasons of teaching and seasons of travel, alternating in a cycle refined over generations. The seasons of teaching are set apart for the instruction of novice singers in the songs the order has preserved, and the seasons of travel are set apart for visits the singers make to the communities of the surrounding countryside. The schedule is observed without exception, and breaking it is regarded by the senior singers as a failure of discipline.

A novice singer is admitted to a house after a probation of some seasons, during which the candidate attends the teaching and the singing and contributes to the common maintenance of the house. The probation is conducted under the direction of a senior singer, who reports to the conference of the senior singers upon the candidate's progress at the close of the probation. The probation is the principal means by which the order maintains the standard of the singing, a worthy complement to the long training of the singer.

## The Daily Accounting of Mercies and Losses

The central observance of the order is the evening review, and the houses of memory are the establishments in which the review is kept by the singers and by such of the faithful as are able to attend. The review is conducted in the meeting-hall of the house, a plain room lit by a single window and warmed by a small hearth. At the close of the day, the household or the company recalls together the events of the day, giving thanks for the mercies and observing the losses. A short song of account is sung, naming the day's sick and recovered, the day's fed and unfed, the day's spared and the day's fallen, and the review is the measure of the order's care for the community.

The review is not a confession and not a punishment, and the order is careful to mark the difference. The sins of the day are observed but not adjudicated, and the remedy lies in the change of conduct on the morrow rather than in any further observance at the evening. The order teaches that the review is meant to keep the account honest, so that the community may know what it has done and may be moved, in good time, to mend what has been done amiss. The review is regarded as the chief instrument by which the song-half of the calling is observed in the household.

## The Customary Bond Between House and Community

The house of memory is bound to the community it serves by a customary relation held to be part of the discipline of the order. The community is required to maintain the house in provisions and in the small endowment the singers require, and the house is required to maintain the community in the keeping of its memory and in the instruction of the young in the songs of the order. This mutual obligation has done much to preserve the harmony of the market towns and the river country, where the house and the community regard one another as parts of a single order. The breach of the mutual obligation is visited by the censure of the community, delivered by the elders of the community in the presence of the senior singer of the house.

The house is also the home of the singers who maintain the discipline in the wider community of the surrounding countryside, recruited from among the senior singers of the house. The singers travel between the houses and between the orders of the faithful, the principal point of contact between the house and the wider community. They are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat, the mark of the office. The order regards the house as a worthy complement to the standing of the watch, held in greater esteem than any other of its institutions.

## Kindred Traditions and the Correspondence

The Memory Houses maintain correspondence with the houses of kindred traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), the means by which the order has preserved its kinship with the Still Flame and other contemplative orders of the continent. A singer of the order will sometimes attend the evening review of a hearth-hall of the Still Flame, and a hearth-keeper will sometimes attend the singing at a house of memory, each welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. This correspondence reflects the order's openness to the wider world, a privilege rather than a claim.

The order has also exchanged singers with the [Forged Flame](/content/religion/forged-flame), whose hearth-keepers teach a discipline of the workshop that the order regards as a worthy complement to the singing of the houses of memory. These exchanges are recorded in the register of each house, the source of the order's knowledge of the wider world. The senior singers caution against the temptation to make a display of the exchanges, for the order regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very ornament the order counsels against in the singing.

## The Quiet Establishments of the Song-Half of the Calling

The Memory Houses are the quiet establishments of the song-half of the Shield and Song, the seat of the singers, the library of songs, the schedules of singing, and the evening review. They are quiet establishments in the market towns and the river country of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), supported by the labour of the singers and the small endowment the communities provide. The houses have been the source of the order's slow growth in the market towns and the river country, a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the courts and the temples. The order asks of its practitioners that they bear what they are called to bear and that they sing what they are called to sing, and it promises them, in return, that the bearing and the singing are themselves a share in the Watcher's keeping of the world.
