## The Faith of the Honest Scale

The Silver Mark is the name given to the tradition of commercial faith practised by merchants, money-changers, and assayers in the trading cities of the three continents. The tradition is not a religion in the manner of the temple faiths, for it maintains no priests, sacred texts, or formal doctrines. The Silver Mark is the understanding that honest measure and just weight are the foundation of fair trade and that the merchant who deals honestly is engaged in a practice pleasing to the divine.

The tradition takes its name from the silver mark, the most widely recognised coin of the known world, and from the mark the assayer stamps upon the coin to certify its weight and fineness. The silver mark is the symbol of the tradition, representing the principle that value is determined by honest measure and that the merchant who deals in the silver mark is bound to deal honestly.

## The Truemarker as Witness

The central figure of the Silver Mark tradition is the Truemarker, the divine witness of just measure and honest weight. The Truemarker is not a god worshipped in temples or invoked in the prayers of ordinary people. The Truemarker is the presence the merchant experiences in the weighing of coin and the measuring of goods, the witness that observes every transaction and knows whether the measure is true or false.

The merchant who uses false weight defrauds not only the customer but the Truemarker as well, for the Truemarker is the witness of every transaction and the judge of every measure. The tradition teaches that the Truemarker does not punish the dishonest merchant by supernatural means but leaves the merchant to the natural consequences of dishonesty: loss of reputation, exclusion from the guild, and the ruin of the trade the merchant once conducted.

A merchant known for honest measure is trusted by customers and correspondents, and a reputation for honest measure is the most valuable asset a merchant can possess. The tradition teaches that the trust of the market is the reward of the honest dealer, and that the Truemarker is present in every transaction as the witness of the honesty the merchant brings to the dealing. A merchant who has been found to use false weights may find that even the offer of restitution does not restore the confidence of the trading partners, for the Truemarker's witness is not a matter that can be set aside by a payment or an apology once the trust has been broken.

## The Practices of the Tradition

The practices of the Silver Mark tradition are the practices of honest trade. The merchant who follows the tradition maintains accurate weights and just measures, testing them regularly against the reference standards the guild of money-changers maintains. The merchant who accepts a coin in payment weighs it upon the scale and tests its fineness against the known standard, recording the weight and fineness in the ledger the merchant keeps.

The tradition also includes the practice of the assay, the testing of metal to determine its purity and value. The assayer trained in the tradition performs the assay with the care and attention the work requires, recording the results in the register the guild maintains. An assayer known for honest assay is trusted by the merchants and money-changers of every market, and the reputation of the assayer is the foundation of the trust the market places in the coin.

The tradition further includes the practice of reconciliation, the regular review of accounts and settlement of balances between merchant houses. The reconciliation is conducted with the formality the tradition requires, and the parties acknowledge the presence of the Truemarker in the settlement of accounts.

## The Houses of Account

The houses of account are the institutions that maintain the practice of the Silver Mark tradition in the trading cities of the three continents. They are the places where merchants and money-changers keep their ledgers and scales, where assayers perform their tests, and where reconciliations are conducted. The largest houses of account are found in Eldreth, Khalisar, and Nordhaven, where the volume of trade demands dedicated buildings with strong rooms and multiple assayers. In smaller towns, a room in the guild hall serves the same function, and the practices of the Silver Mark are observed with the same care despite the more modest surroundings.

Houses of account are not temples, for they contain no altars or shrines. They are places of business where honest trade is conducted, and they are regarded by followers of the tradition as the places where the presence of the Truemarker is most directly experienced.

Houses of account are maintained by the guild of money-changers or the guild of merchants, and they are subject to the regulation of the guild and the oversight of the magistrates. A house of account is required to maintain reference weights and measures, to employ assayers trained in the tradition, and to conduct reconciliations at regular intervals.

## The Correspondence with Other Traditions

The Silver Mark tradition has been recognised by the [Still Flame](/content/religion/still-flame), whose teachings on the honest household and just measure are consistent with the practices of the tradition. The Still Flame teaches that the hearth of the honest household is the place where the measure is just, and followers of the Silver Mark recognise in this teaching a kindred understanding.

The [Unwritten Name](/content/religion/unwritten-name) also recognises the Silver Mark tradition, teaching that the honest naming of quantity is the discipline of the noun that the reader must attend to. Followers of the Silver Mark and the Unwritten Name have maintained a correspondence, recognising in each other's traditions the same attention to the precise and the true.

The tradition is also recognised by the [Forged Flame](/content/religion/forged-flame), whose smiths and assayers work together with merchants and money-changers in the certification of the weight and fineness of coin. The smith who forges the weights and measures is engaged in the same work as the merchant who uses them, and the two traditions are complementary.
