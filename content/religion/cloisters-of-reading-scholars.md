## The Scholars of the Unwritten Name

The Cloisters of Reading are the houses of the Unwritten Name, the quiet establishments where the order of the readers maintains the libraries, the schedules of the study, and the conferences of the senior and the junior scholars. The cloisters are found chiefly in the river towns and the older cloister districts of Aethelar, and the scholars who attend the cloisters are the practitioners of the discipline of the nouns, the careful attention to the difference between the naming and the pointing.

The scholars of the cloisters are not monks in the manner of the older traditions, for the doctrine does not require the readers to withdraw from the world. Many of the most senior scholars have kept the households and the trades while pursuing the long study, and the cloisters are the places of the meeting rather than the retreat. The scholar who attends the cloister is a person of the ordinary life who has undertaken the discipline of the long reading and the conference.

## The Admission of the Scholar

The scholar is admitted to the cloister after a probation of some seasons, during which the candidate attends the readings and the conferences and contributes to the common maintenance of the library. The probation is conducted under the direction of a senior reader, who reports to the conference upon the candidate's progress at the close of the probation.

The probation is the principal means by which the doctrine maintains the standard of the reading. It is not a test of the memory but a test of the attention, and the candidate who passes is admitted as a reader. The new reader is given the wooden clasp that is the sign of the calling, and the new reader is permitted to undertake the long reading of a chosen text.

The admission of the scholar is the recognition that the candidate has developed the capacity for the sustained attention that the long reading requires. The scholar who has been admitted is expected to continue the reading through the years, returning to the text again and again and bringing the discoveries to the conference.

## The Long Reading of the Scholar

The principal discipline of the scholar is the long reading, a sustained practice of the attention in which the reader returns again and again to a chosen text over a span of years. The doctrine teaches that the same text, read with the patience over time, becomes a different text to the reader who has grown in the meantime. The contradictions and the gaps in the text are not the obstacles to the understanding but the very places where the understanding must be sought.

The scholar who undertakes the long reading chooses a text from the library of the cloister, with the counsel of a senior reader. The chosen text is taken from the shelf and placed in the scholar's hands, and the senior reader speaks a brief blessing in the ancient tongue preserved by the doctrine. The scholar is permitted to take the text to the reader's chamber and keep it for the duration of the long reading.

The scholar is required to bring the text to the conference at the appointed intervals, and the scholar presents the passages that have struck the attention. The conference discusses the passages, and the senior readers offer the corrections and the insights that the scholar's reading has prompted.

## The Conference of the Scholars

The conference of the readers is the social observance of the doctrine, held at the cloister at the regular intervals throughout the year. The junior readers open with a passage from the current long reading, the senior readers respond in turn, and the conference closes with a brief silence in which the Unspeakable is held in mind without being named.

The conference is not a debate, and the doctrine does not maintain that any of the readings is the last word. The conference is a place of the correction rather than the advocacy, and the readers attend to one another's readings with the same attention that they bring to the own. The scholar who presents a reading at the conference is tested by the attention of the other scholars, and the testing is the refinement of the understanding.

The conference is the occasion on which the solitary reading is brought into the community of the readers. The scholar who has read the text alone through the season of the reading brings the discoveries to the conference, and the discoveries are tested and refined by the attention of the other readers. The conference is the source of the unity of the doctrine across the river towns and the cloister districts.

## The Library of the Cloister

The library is the principal chamber of the cloister, lined with the shelves of the plain wood upon which the copies of the older texts are kept. The library is open to the readers who have been admitted to the long reading and closed to all others. It is regarded by the doctrine as the heart of the cloister, the place to which every novice aspires and to which every senior reader returns.

The texts held by the library are few in number and well established, for the doctrine values the long reading of a few texts above the brief reading of many. The texts include the older scriptures of several of the world's faiths and certain secular works of the careful philosophy. The additions to the library are made with the great care and only after the conference of the senior readers has approved the addition.

## The Scholar and the Wider Community

The scholar of the cloister is bound to the wider community of the river towns by the customary relation that is held to be part of the discipline of the doctrine. The town is required to maintain the cloister in the provisions and in the small endowment that the scholars require, and the cloister is required to maintain the town in the counsel and in the instruction of the young.

The scholar who travels between the cloisters and the towns is the principal point of contact between the cloister and the wider community. The travelling scholar is recognised by the small wooden clasp worn at the throat, and the scholar's arrival in the town is the occasion for the instruction and the counsel.

## The Kinship with the Kindred Traditions

The scholars of the cloisters maintain the correspondence with the kindred traditions, and the practice of the exchange is the means by which the doctrine has preserved the kinship with the Sunless Veils and the Still Flame. A senior reader of the Unwritten Name will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the Veil Cloister of the Sunless Veils, and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of the readers.

The scholars regard the exchange as the enrichment of the own discipline, for the encounter with the different practice reveals the assumptions that the scholar has not examined. The scholar who undertakes the retreat at the Veil Cloister returns to the reading with the fresh attention, and the scholar who attends the conference of the readers from the Still Flame brings the new perspective to the reading.

## The Life of the Scholar

The life of the scholar is the life of the reading and the conference and the instruction. The scholar rises in the morning and reads the text for the hour, attends to the work of the household or the trade, returns to the reading in the evening, and brings the discoveries to the conference at the appointed intervals. The discipline of the doctrine is woven into the fabric of the daily life, and the scholar who follows the discipline is the practitioner of the Unwritten Name.

The scholar does not seek the recognition or the reward, and the doctrine does not offer the advancement or the honour. The scholar seeks the refinement of the attention and the deepening of the understanding, and the discipline of the long reading is the means by which the refinement is achieved.
