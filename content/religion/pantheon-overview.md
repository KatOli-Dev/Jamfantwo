## The Varieties of the Divine Attended

The peoples of the known world attend the divine in many ways, and the traditions that have grown from this attending are as varied as the climates and the languages of the continents. There are faiths that centre upon the temple and the priestly hierarchy, such as the worship of the Sea Mother in the warm coastal cities. There are orders that pursue a discipline of practice rather than a creed, such as the Shield and Song and the Sunless Veils. There are philosophical doctrines that approach the divine through the attention to language and to silence, such as the Unwritten Name. And there are the traditions of the different species, the dwarven reverence for the ancestors and the mountain, the elven veneration of the forests and the living world, the centaur beliefs about the sky and the plains, the halfling hearth traditions of the household, and the gnomish devotion that is expressed through the craft of the hands.

The traditions of the different peoples do not exist in isolation from one another, and the history of the continent is marked by the exchanges and the correspondences that the traditions have maintained. The Still Flame and the Shield and Song and the Unwritten Name have exchanged practitioners for many generations, and the three traditions regard one another as kindred paths that approach the same mystery from different directions. The older temple faiths of the warm coasts have maintained a more distant relationship with the traditions of the interior, but the merchants and the travellers who move along the trade routes carry the knowledge of the different practices from one region to the next.

## The Temple Faiths of the Warm Coasts

The temple faiths of the warm coasts are the most visible of the religious institutions of the known world, centred upon the great temples that dominate the waterfronts and the market squares of the coastal cities. The Sea Mother's Court in Khalisar is the largest and the most influential of the temples of the warm coasts, and the worship of the Sea Mother is the principal faith of the sailors and the fishermen who depend upon the sea for their livelihood. The temple of the Sea Mother maintains the harbour lamps and the records of the voyages that have been undertaken from the port, and the high priest of the temple is a member of the city council.

The temple faiths teach that the deity is a being who can be approached through the prayer and the offering, and the priests of the temples conduct the rites that secure the favour of the deity for the community. The rites of the Sea Mother include the offering of the first catch at the beginning of the fishing season and the lighting of the harbour lamps at the evening for the safe return of the vessels. The temple faiths do not claim that the deity is the only divine reality, and the priests of the Sea Mother acknowledge the existence of the other traditions without asserting the superiority of their own.

## The Contemplative Orders of the Interior

The contemplative orders of the interior are the traditions that pursue the divine through the disciplines of the withdrawal and the attention and the silence. The Sunless Veils teach the discipline of lifting the veils that the world places between the soul and the reality that lies beyond the bright surfaces. The Unwritten Name teaches the discipline of the nouns, the careful attention to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it. The Still Flame teaches the tending of the hearth-fire as the means by which the one Flame that underlies all the fires is honoured.

These orders do not maintain the temples in the manner of the coastal faiths, and they do not employ the priests who conduct the rites for the community. The contemplative orders are organised around the cloisters and the houses of the reading and the retreat, and the practitioners are the readers and the veilers and the hearth-keepers who maintain the discipline of the order in the communities where they live.

## The Species Traditions

The different species of the known world have developed the traditions that reflect their different natures and their different relationships to the world. The dwarven peoples of the mountains practise the reverence for the ancestors and the mountain, maintaining the halls of the memory in which the names of the forebears are preserved and the rites of the deep earth are observed. The elven peoples of the forests practise the veneration of the living world, honouring the trees and the waters and the creatures of the woodland as the manifestations of the divine.

The centaur kindreds of the plains practise the beliefs that are centred upon the sky and the stars and the open grasslands. The centaurs read the movements of the celestial bodies as the signs of the order that governs the plains, and the seasonal gatherings of the kindreds are the occasions for the observation of the sky. The halfling communities of the valleys practise the hearth traditions that are centred upon the household and the garden and the table, honouring the spirits of the home and the land through the daily rituals of the preparation of the food and the tending of the fire.

The gnomish peoples of the woodland hills practise the devotion that is expressed through the craft of the making, teaching that the work of the hands is a form of the prayer and that the tool that is well made is an honour to the divine. The gnomish craft devotion is not a faith of the temple or the creed, but a practice of the attention and the skill that the maker brings to the work.

## The Kindred Traditions and the Exchanges

The traditions of the continent maintain the correspondences that connect them to one another, and the exchanges of the practitioners between the traditions are the means by which the different paths enrich one another without diminishing the distinctiveness of each. A senior reader of the Unwritten Name will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the Veil Cloister of the Sunless Veils, and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of the readers. A singer of the Shield and Song will sometimes attend the evening review of a hearth-hall of the Still Flame, and a hearth-keeper will sometimes attend the singing at a house of memory.

The correspondences are preserved in the libraries of the cloisters and the houses, and the letters that the senior practitioners exchange are among the most valued texts in the possession of the traditions. The correspondences are the source of the unity that the traditions recognise among themselves, and the openness that the traditions maintain toward one another is a privilege rather than a claim.

## The Rites of the Passage and the Season

The rites that mark the passages of the life and the turning of the seasons are observed in every tradition of the known world, though the forms of the rites and the meanings that they carry vary with the traditions. The birth of the child, the coming of the age, the joining of the marriage, and the death of the person are the occasions on which the traditions conduct the rites that acknowledge the significance of the transition.

The seasonal rites mark the turning of the year and the cycles of the agricultural and the pastoral life. The planting and the harvest, the coming of the winter and the return of the spring, the longest day and the longest night are the occasions on which the communities gather to observe the rites that the tradition prescribes. The rites of the passage and the season are the most widely observed of the religious practices, and the participation in the rites is the most common form of the engagement that the people have with the traditions.

## The Diversity and the Unity of the Traditions

The religious traditions of the known world are diverse in the forms and the teachings and the practices, and there is no single institution that claims the authority over all the traditions. The diversity is the result of the different histories and the different conditions of the regions in which the traditions have developed, and the diversity is recognised by the traditions themselves as a condition that is not to be overcome but to be respected.

The unity that the traditions recognise among themselves is not the unity of the doctrine or the hierarchy but the unity of the shared concern for the divine that the traditions attend in their different ways. The Still Flame and the Unwritten Name and the Sunless Veils and the Shield and Song are kindred traditions that recognise the kinship of their paths. The temple faiths and the contemplative orders and the species traditions are the different forms that the attending of the divine takes in the different regions and among the different peoples, and the diversity is the richness of the religious life of the known world.
