## The Faith That Keeps the Hearth

The Still Flame is the faith that maintains the [hearth-halls](/content/religion/hearth-halls), a tradition centred upon the teaching that the flame upon the hearth, the warmth of the body, and the light of the sun are all of one nature. The faith is among the older traditions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and its practitioners are found throughout the temperate river valleys of the continent and in scattered communities along the eastern coasts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The Still Flame is neither a revealed religion in the manner of the great temple faiths nor a philosophical system of the kind the academies cultivate; it is a practical tradition of tending the fire, keeping the household, and extending the warmth of the hall to those who come to it in need.

The faith holds that the single Flame underlies every particular fire that burns upon a hearth, every warmth that stirs in a living body, and every light that falls from the sun upon the fields and the rivers. The distinction between these three is a distinction of manifestation and not of substance, and the faithful are taught to recognise the one Flame in the many fires. The doctrine is not elaborated into a theology of the kind that the temples maintain, for the Still Flame teaches that the Flame is to be honoured by tending rather than by explaining, and the tradition has resisted the temptation to produce a formal creed.

## The Teaching of the Threefold Flame

The Still Flame teaches that the flame upon the hearth is the most immediate and accessible manifestation of the one Flame, the fire that warms the household and cooks the food and lights the room at night. The tending of the hearth-fire is the first discipline of the faith, and every household that keeps the morning kindling and the evening banking is regarded as a participant in the work of the faith. The second manifestation is the warmth of the body, the inner fire that sustains life and that returns to the Flame at death. The third manifestation is the light of the sun, the great fire that warms the world and that the faithful honour as the source of the cycle of the seasons and the growth of the crops.

The Threefold Flame is not a mystery to be contemplated in the manner of the doctrines of the Unwritten Name; it is a practical teaching that the faithful apply in the daily conduct of the household. The morning kindling is the renewal of the household's share in the Flame, and the evening banking is the trust that the Flame will return with the morning light. The teaching is simple, and the faith is content that it should be simple, for the Still Flame holds that the truth of the Flame is accessible to every person who tends a fire and not only to those who have mastered the disciplines of reading or retreat.

## The Spread Along the River Valleys

The Still Flame spread from the river valleys of central Aethelar along the trade routes that connected the early human kingdoms, carried by settlers and merchants who brought the custom of the hearth with them rather than by missionaries or conquest. The faith appears in the chronicles of the river towns already well established by the time the first great human kingdoms arose, and the earliest written references to the Still Flame are found in the records of the grain trade, where the hearth-halls are mentioned as landmarks and as places of shelter for travellers.

The spread of the faith along the river systems gave the Still Flame its characteristic geography: the hearth-halls are concentrated in the temperate river valleys and the coastal plains where the human populations first settled, and they are scarce or absent in the highlands, the deep forests, and the colder northern regions. The faith has never attempted to convert the peoples of the regions beyond the river valleys, and its slow growth along the trade routes has been the result of the steady movement of peoples and the quiet persuasion of the hearth rather than any program of expansion.

## The Relation to the Culture of the Hearth

The Still Flame has given rise to a body of cultural practices associated with [the Still Flame](/content/culture/still-flame) that extend beyond the strictly religious into the daily life of the communities that observe the faith. The morning kindling and the evening banking are the principal observances of the household, and the seasonal gatherings at the hearth-halls mark the turning of the year and the important passages of life. The faith does not prescribe a detailed code of conduct, and its teachings on marriage, hospitality, and the duties of the household are customary rather than doctrinal.

The Still Flame has been especially close to the communities of the Shield and Song and the Unwritten Name, and the three traditions have exchanged practitioners and maintained correspondence for many generations. The faith regards these exchanges as a sign of the unity that underlies the different traditions of the continent, and the hearth-keepers are instructed to welcome the practitioners of kindred traditions with the same warmth that they extend to the faithful of the Still Flame.

## The Hearth-Halls and the Hearth-Keepers

The [hearth-halls](/content/religion/hearth-halls) are the houses of worship of the Still Flame, plain structures of stone and timber in which the central fire is kept burning without ceasing. The hearth-halls are the most visible sign of the faith and are found in every town and village that observes the Still Flame. The hearth-keepers are the men and women appointed to tend the central fire and to instruct the young in the doctrine of the faith, and they hold no worldly authority beyond the respect their service earns them.

The hearth-keepers are drawn from the communities they serve and are appointed by the elders of the community after a period of training and probation. The keepers are expected to labour with their hands as do other folk, and the faith counsels them against the temptation to seek governance or influence beyond the tending of the fire and the instruction of the young. The keepers are the principal point of contact between the hearth-hall and the wider community, and their reputation for hospitality is the measure of the hall's standing in the surrounding country.

## The Rites of the Flame

The Still Flame observes four principal rites conducted at the hearth-halls: the gathering at the turning of the seasons, the presentation of children to the light, the solemnisation of marriage before the shared hearth, and the returning of the body to the fire at death. The seasonal gatherings are the chief communal observances of the faith, and the faithful gather at the hearth-halls from the surrounding country to attend the rites and to bear portions of the central fire home to relight their own hearths.

The rites of the flame are simple in form and are conducted by the hearth-keepers without elaboration. The faith teaches that the Flame needs no adornment to be honoured, and the rites are conducted with the same plainness that characterises the hearth-halls themselves. The rites are open to all who wish to attend, and the Still Flame does not restrict participation in its observances to those who profess the faith.

## The Duty of Hospitality

The Still Flame teaches that none who come to a flame in need should be turned away, and the duty of hospitality is the most visible expression of the faith in the wider community. The hearth-halls maintain a store of fuel and food against the winter, and the hearth-keepers are required to extend the warmth of the fire to all who come, regardless of their creed or origin. The duty of hospitality is the third of the principal observances of the faith, and its discharge is the measure of the hall's reputation among the travellers of the road.

The hospitality of the hearth-halls has earned the Still Flame the respect even of those who hold to other traditions, and many who do not observe the faith speak well of the hearth-halls and the warmth they extend without condition. The faith asks little of those who come seeking shelter, and it promises little beyond the warmth of the fire and the hospitality of the hall, and it is perhaps for this reason that the Still Flame has endured through the centuries.

## The Life of the Still Flame

The Still Flame is a faith of the household and the hearth, and its teachings are addressed to the practical conduct of daily life rather than to the contemplation of the divine. The faith has no formal hierarchy, no extensive scripture, and no elaborate doctrine. It asks of its practitioners that they keep the fire burning, that they extend warmth to those in need, and that they recognise the one Flame in the fires of the hearth, the warmth of the body, and the light of the sun. The faith has outlasted grander and more demanding creeds, and its endurance is a testimony to the power of the simple teaching that the Still Flame preserves: that the flame upon the hearth is a fragment of the one Flame that sustains the world, and that the tending of the fire is the tending of the faith.
