## The Faith of the Hearth and the Temple

The Still Flame is a religious tradition of the central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), a faith centred upon the teaching that the flame upon the hearth, the warmth of the body, and the light of the sun are all of one nature. The tradition is at once a simple practice of the household and a formal religion with temples, clergy, and doctrine. The relationship between these two forms is the central tension of the faith, for the Still Flame has never been of a single character throughout the lands where it is observed.

The faith is found throughout the temperate river valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and in scattered communities along the eastern coasts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). It is neither a revealed religion in the manner of the great temple faiths nor a philosophical system of the kind the academies cultivate; it is a practical tradition of tending the fire, keeping the household, and extending the warmth of the hall to those who come to it in need. Yet in the kingdoms where the faith has become established as the principal religion, it has developed a formal church with scripture, hierarchy, and doctrine that the older practice lacks.

## The Teaching of the Threefold Flame

The Still Flame holds that a single Flame underlies every particular fire that burns upon a hearth, every warmth that stirs in a living body, and every light that falls from the sun upon the fields and the rivers. The distinction between these three is a distinction of manifestation and not of substance, and the faithful are taught to recognise the one Flame in the many fires. The doctrine is not elaborated into a formal theology of the kind that the temple faiths maintain, for the Still Flame teaches that the Flame is to be honoured by tending rather than by explaining.

The first and most immediate manifestation is the flame upon the hearth, the fire that warms the household and cooks the food and lights the room at night. The tending of the hearth-fire is the first discipline of the faith, and every household that keeps the morning kindling and the evening banking is regarded as a participant in the work of the faith. The second manifestation is the warmth of the body, the inner fire that sustains life and that returns to the Flame at death. The third manifestation is the light of the sun, the great fire that warms the world and that the faithful honour as the source of the cycle of the seasons and the growth of the crops.

The teaching of the threefold flame is not a mystery to be contemplated in the manner of the doctrines of the [Unwritten Name](/content/religion/unwritten-name); it is a practical teaching that the faithful apply in the daily conduct of the household. The morning kindling is the renewal of the household's share in the Flame, and the evening banking is the trust that the Flame will return with the morning light. The teaching is simple, and the faith is content that it should be simple, for the Still Flame holds that the truth of the Flame is accessible to every person who tends a fire and not only to those who have mastered the disciplines of reading or retreat.

## The Hearth Tradition

The older and more widespread form of the Still Flame is the hearth tradition, the practice of the household and the community that has been observed since before the first great human kingdoms arose. The hearth tradition has no formal hierarchy, no extensive scripture, and no elaborate doctrine. It asks of its practitioners that they keep the fire burning, that they extend warmth to those in need, and that they recognise the one Flame in the fires of the hearth, the warmth of the body, and the light of the sun.

The hearth tradition is observed through the daily disciplines of the household: the morning kindling, the evening banking, and the seasonal gatherings at the hearth-halls where the central fire is kept burning throughout the year. The tradition has given rise to a body of cultural practices that extend beyond the strictly religious into the daily life of the communities that observe the faith. The morning kindling and the evening banking are the principal observances of the household, and the seasonal gatherings mark the turning of the year and the important passages of life.

The hearth tradition does not prescribe a detailed code of conduct, and its teachings on marriage, hospitality, and the duties of the household are customary rather than doctrinal. The naming of children, the joining of hands in marriage, and the returning of the body to the fire at death are conducted according to the customs of the valley and the parish, with variations from one region to another that the tradition accommodates without difficulty.

### The Hearth-Halls and the Hearth-Keepers

The [hearth-halls](/content/religion/hearth-halls) are the houses of worship of the Still Flame, plain structures of stone and timber in which the central fire is kept burning without ceasing. The hearth-halls are the most visible sign of the faith and are found in every town and village that observes the Still Flame. They are built to hold a central fire tended by the hearth-keepers, and they lack the towers or ornamentation favoured by some other traditions, for the faith teaches that the Flame needs no adornment to be honoured.

The hearth-keepers are the men and women appointed to tend the central fire and to instruct the young in the doctrine of the faith. They are drawn from the communities they serve and are appointed by the elders of the community after a period of training and probation. The keepers are expected to labour with their hands as do other folk, and the faith counsels them against the temptation to seek governance or influence beyond the tending of the fire and the instruction of the young. The keepers hold no worldly authority beyond the respect their service earns them, and they are the principal point of contact between the hearth-hall and the wider community.

### The Rites of the Flame

The Still Flame of the hearth tradition observes four principal rites conducted at the hearth-halls: the gathering at the turning of the seasons, the presentation of children to the light, the solemnisation of marriage before the shared hearth, and the returning of the body to the fire at death. The seasonal gatherings are the chief communal observances of the faith, and the faithful gather at the hearth-halls from the surrounding country to attend the rites and to bear portions of the central fire home to relight their own hearths.

The rites are simple in form and are conducted by the hearth-keepers without elaboration. The faith teaches that the Flame needs no adornment to be honoured, and the rites are conducted with the same plainness that characterises the hearth-halls themselves. The rites are open to all who wish to attend, and the Still Flame does not restrict participation in its observances to those who profess the faith.

### The Duty of Hospitality

The Still Flame teaches that none who come to a flame in need should be turned away, and the duty of hospitality is the most visible expression of the faith in the wider community. The hearth-halls maintain a store of fuel and food against the winter, and the hearth-keepers are required to extend the warmth of the fire to all who come, regardless of their creed or origin. The duty of hospitality is the third of the principal observances of the faith, and its discharge is the measure of the hall's reputation among the travellers of the road.

The hospitality of the hearth-halls has earned the Still Flame the respect even of those who hold to other traditions, and many who do not observe the faith speak well of the hearth-halls and the warmth they extend without condition. The faith asks little of those who come seeking shelter, and it promises little beyond the warmth of the fire and the hospitality of the hall, and it is perhaps for this reason that the Still Flame has endured through the centuries.

## The Temple Faith

In the kingdoms where the Still Flame became the principal religion, particularly in [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) and the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) river valley, the faith developed into an institutional church with formal hierarchy, scripture, and doctrine. This form of the Still Flame is known as the temple faith, and it preserves the core teachings of the threefold flame while adding layers of organisation and interpretation that the older hearth tradition does not possess.

### The Prophet of the Lowlands

The founder of the temple faith is known in its scriptures as the Prophet of the Lowlands, a figure of obscure origin whose teachings were first recorded in the market towns of the central river valleys. The traditional account holds that the Prophet was a scribe of modest station who received a vision of the eternal flame while copying the chronicles of a minor temple of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/culture/sky-walkers-way). The vision revealed that the gods of the polytheist pantheon were not separate beings but emanations of a single divine principle, and that the worship of many gods was a misunderstanding of the unity that underlay all things.

The Prophet began to preach in the market towns, drawing small crowds of listeners who were attracted by the clarity of the message and the simplicity of its demands. The faith required no sacrifices, no pilgrimages, and no elaborate rituals, only the acknowledgement of the single flame and the conduct of a life in accordance with the order the flame sustained. The simplicity of the teaching was the foundation of its appeal, and the faith spread rapidly through the towns of the river valleys and along the trade routes connecting the interior to the coast.

The Prophet was opposed by the priests of the polytheist temples, who regarded the teaching as a threat to their authority. The Prophet was arrested and brought before the magistrates of one of the principal towns of the lowlands, charged with disturbing the peace and undermining the public cult. The trial is recorded in the scriptures of the church with considerable detail. The Prophet defended the teaching with arguments that the magistrates found persuasive, and the charges were dismissed.

### The Temples and the Hierarchy of the Church

The places of worship of the temple faith are called temples, and they differ substantially from the hearth-halls of the older tradition. A temple is a simple hall oriented toward the east, with an altar at the eastern end upon which a flame is kept burning continuously, symbolising the eternal flame that is the object of the faith. The flame is kindled from the embers of the previous year's flame, and the continuity of the flame from one year to the next is a matter of considerable importance. A temple whose flame has been extinguished is regarded as having lost its sanctity until the flame is rekindled by a priest of appropriate rank.

The clergy of the temple faith are organised into a hierarchy consisting of deacons, priests, and bishops. Deacons are the junior clergy who assist in the conduct of services and the administration of the temple. Priests are the clergy who conduct the principal services of the faith, including the kindling of the flame, the blessing of the faithful, and the administration of the rites. Bishops are the senior clergy who oversee the temples of a region and who are responsible for the ordination of priests and deacons. The head of the church is the First Warder, a title that reflects the understanding of the bishop's role as the guardian of the flame rather than its master.

The hierarchy is governed by a council of bishops that meets at intervals to deliberate upon matters of doctrine and discipline. The council is the highest authority of the church, and its decisions are binding upon the faithful. The council has no authority over secular rulers, and the relationship between the church and the kingdoms in which it is established is governed by agreements that vary from one kingdom to another. In the [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth), the church and the crown maintain a close relationship, and the king is crowned by the First Warder in a ceremony that acknowledges the divine origin of royal authority.

### The Spread of the Temple Faith

The expansion of the temple faith from its place of origin in the central lowlands was accomplished through the activity of missionaries who travelled along the trade routes and through the patronage of rulers who adopted the faith for their kingdoms. The pattern of expansion was consistent: missionaries arrived in a town, preached the faith to the inhabitants, and established a small community of believers. The community grew gradually as the inhabitants found the teaching persuasive and as the moral conduct of the believers attracted favourable notice.

The adoption of the faith by the rulers of the Aldreth was the decisive event in its spread through the eastern lowlands. The king of the Aldreth was converted by a missionary who had been sent to the court following a period of illness that the court physicians had been unable to cure. The king recovered after the missionary prayed over him, and the recovery was attributed to the intercession of the flame. The king ordered the establishment of the faith throughout the kingdom, and the temples of the polytheist religions were gradually converted into churches of the Still Flame.

## The Two Forms in Relation

The hearth tradition and the temple faith are not separate religions but two expressions of the same underlying teaching, and the relationship between them is a matter of ongoing negotiation. The hearth tradition regards the temple faith as a legitimate but elaborated development of the core teaching, while the temple faith regards the hearth tradition as the uncultivated soil from which the church grew. In practice, the two forms coexist in most regions where the Still Flame is observed, and the faithful move between them according to the occasion: the household observes the morning kindling and the evening banking in the manner of the hearth tradition, while the community gathers at the temple for the principal festivals under the direction of the priest.

The coexistence is not without tension. The hearth-keepers of the older tradition regard the hierarchy of the temple faith with a certain distance, holding that the simplicity of the Flame is obscured by the elaboration of the church. The clergy of the temple faith regard the hearth-keepers with a tolerance that is not entirely free of condescension, holding that the simple practice is incomplete without the guidance of the church. Yet the two forms have learned to accommodate one another, and the friction between them is a sign of the vitality of the faith rather than a threat to its survival.

## Relations with Other Traditions

The Still Flame has maintained correspondence with the other traditions of the continent for many generations. The faith has been especially close to the [Shield and Song](/content/religion/shield-and-song) and the [Unwritten Name](/content/religion/unwritten-name), and the three traditions have exchanged practitioners and maintained mutual respect for many generations. The hearth-keepers of the Still Flame are instructed to welcome the practitioners of kindred traditions with the same warmth that they extend to the faithful.

The relationship between the Still Flame and the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/culture/sky-walkers-way), the older polytheist religion of the central grasslands, has been marked by periods of conflict and periods of accommodation. The earliest encounters between the two religions were characterised by mutual suspicion. The priests of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/culture/sky-walkers-way) regarded the monotheistic teaching as a threat to the social order, and the missionaries of the Still Flame regarded the polytheist temples as obstacles to the spread of the true faith. The reconciliation achieved between the two religions in the present age is a measure of the maturity both traditions have attained.

The Still Flame has also acknowledged the [Forged Flame](/content/religion/forged-flame) as a kindred tradition, recognising in the forge fire a reflection of the Still Flame that burns in the hearth-halls. The dwarven communities of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) have adopted the [First Kindling](/content/culture/first-kindling) as a complement to their own traditions of the forge fire.

## The Faith in the Present Age

The Still Flame is the principal religion of the central and southern regions of Aethelar and has made some progress in the coastal regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), though the older animistic traditions of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) have proved more resistant to the teachings of the flame. The faith has outlasted grander and more demanding creeds, and its endurance is a testimony to the power of the simple teaching that the Still Flame preserves: that the flame upon the hearth is a fragment of the one Flame that sustains the world, and that the tending of the fire is the tending of the faith.
