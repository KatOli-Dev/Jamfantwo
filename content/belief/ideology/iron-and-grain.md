## Overview

The Iron-and-Grain is a teaching of civic duty that holds the warrior and the husbandman to be the two halves of a single polity, neither sufficient without the other and each bound to honour the other's office. It is not a religion and claims no temple or priest, but a discipline of governance and household taught in the marcher lands, where the sword and the sickle are both daily necessities and the ruin of either is held to be the ruin of the commonweal. The central precept of the doctrine is that the iron which defends the field is fed by the grain which the field yields, and that the husbandman who forgets the iron and the warrior who forgets the grain are alike in fault. The doctrine has been refined over generations into a comprehensive ethic of mutual dependence that shapes the laws, the households, and the councils of the communities where it is observed.

## Origins in the Marcher Settlements

The Iron-and-Grain is generally held to have arisen in the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches), where the settlements along the northern frontier were required to maintain both a militia and a harvest, and where the neglect of either left the community exposed to raid or to famine. The chroniclers of the marches preserve accounts of an older compact by which the warriors of the watch shared in the labours of the field at the season of planting and of harvest, and the husbandmen took their turn at the watch when the labour of the field was slack. From this practical compact the present doctrine is held to descend, refined by the elders and the captains into the formal teaching now observed throughout the marches and in the polities that have taken it up.

As the safety of the marches permitted the growth of larger settlements and the rise of towns, the doctrine was carried into the new communities by the officers who had learned it on the frontier, and the towns of the marches came to require of their leading citizens a competence in both the military and the agricultural arts. The doctrine spread southward into the [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) and into the towns of the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league), where the coastal communities had long experience of the same complementarity between the fishery, the field, and the defence of the shore. From the northern kingdoms the doctrine entered the councils of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), where it was adapted to the complementary arts of the horseman and the sower, and the chronicler notes that the confederation has given the doctrine one of its fullest practical expressions.

## The Two Offices

The doctrine sets forth two offices as the foundation of the polity: the office of iron, which is the defence of the community by arms, and the office of grain, which is the sustenance of the community by the labour of the field. The doctrine holds that neither office may claim precedence over the other, for the polity defended but unfed is no polity at all, and the polity fed but undefended is no polity for long. The two offices are held to be complementary rather than rival, and the practitioner of one is required to honour the practitioner of the other. The chronicler notes that this teaching has done much to soften the customary contempt of the warrior for the husbandman and of the husbandman for the warrior, and that the communities where the doctrine is observed are marked by a mutuality of respect that other communities sometimes lack.

The doctrine is careful that the offices should not be confounded. The warrior is not to leave his post for the field save at the appointed seasons, and the husbandman is not to quit his field for the watch save when the watch requires him. The teaching holds that the division of offices is itself a discipline, and that the competence of each is preserved by the proper separation. The chronicler notes that this discipline has given to the communities of the marches a steadiness in both their military and their agricultural practice that the communities where the offices are confused do not possess, for the warrior who is half a husbandman is neither, and the husbandman who is half a warrior is likewise neither.

## The Tribute of the Field

The first article of the doctrine is the tribute of the field, by which the husbandman acknowledges the necessity of the iron and contributes of his store to the maintenance of the warrior. The tribute is not a tax in the ordinary sense, for the doctrine holds that the husbandman contributes freely to the defence of the field that feeds him, and that the contribution so given is a part of the husbandman's office rather than an imposition. The teaching is careful that the tribute should be measured, so that the husbandman is not impoverished by his contribution and the warrior is not enriched beyond his station. The chronicler notes that this measure has been a continual subject of deliberation in the communities where the doctrine is observed, and that the prudent magistrates are known by their attention to it.

The tribute of the field is observed in its most elaborate form in the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), where the horsemen and the sowers maintain a careful balance of contribution and service, and where the customary rates are revised by the councils of the plains at each generation. The confederation is held by the chroniclers to be a polity in which the doctrine of iron and grain has been carried to its highest practical refinement, and the chronicler notes that the confederation's endurance through many seasons of hardship is attributable in no small part to the steadiness of the tribute.

## The Guardianship of the Watch

The second article of the doctrine is the guardianship of the watch, by which the warrior acknowledges the necessity of the grain and contributes of his vigilance to the protection of the husbandman's labour. The teaching holds that the warrior who defends the field without honouring the field is a hireling and not a guardian, and that the office of iron is fulfilled only when the warrior recognises that his own sustenance depends upon the labour he defends. The warrior is required to know something of the field, that he may understand what he defends, and the husbandman is required to know something of the watch, that he may honour what defends him. The chronicler notes that this mutual knowledge has done much to soften the customary estrangement of the two offices in other communities.

The guardianship of the watch is observed most strictly upon the frontier, where the settlements are exposed to the raids of the wild beasts and of the unsubdued clans beyond the marches. The watchmen of the [Northern Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches) are required to maintain a familiarity with the fields and the herds within their ward, and the husbandmen are required to take their turn at the watch in the season of slack labour. The chronicler notes that the doctrine has produced in the marches a population in which the military and the agricultural arts are both widely shared, and that the marches are accordingly both well defended and well tilled.

## The Common Hearth and the Common Wage

The third article of the doctrine is the common hearth and the common wage, by which the two offices are bound into a single polity. The doctrine holds that the household which contains both the warrior and the husbandman is the foundation of the commonweal, and that the polity which honours the household is the polity which endures. The teaching is careful that the household should not be impoverished by the maintenance of either office, and that the wage of the warrior and the wage of the husbandman should be such as to permit the continuance of both. The chronicler notes that this teaching has produced a moderation in the affairs of the marches that the wealthier polities to the south do not always observe, and that the marches are marked by an absence of the extremes of wealth and want that disfigure other communities.

The common hearth is observed in its most ordinary form in the villages of the marches, where the household of the captain contains also the labour of the field, and the household of the husbandman contributes also to the watch. The chronicler notes that the village headman is required by the doctrine to maintain both offices in his own person so far as he is able, and that the village which fails of this is held to be in decline. The doctrine has been criticised by the philosophers of the southern cities as too narrow a foundation for a polity, and the chronicler notes that the criticism is not without force, yet the marches endure by it and the philosophy of the cities has not always preserved the cities.

## Application in the Greater Polities

Beyond its origins in the marches, the Iron-and-Grain has shaped the conduct of the greater polities that have taken it up, and the [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) is governed by a council in which the marshals of the watch and the reeves of the field sit together. The doctrine has been adapted to the conditions of the kingdom with care, and the chronicler notes that the adaptation has not been without controversy, for the greater polity requires a division of offices that the village does not, and the doctrine has been required to yield a little of its simplicity to the complexity of the realm. Yet the central teaching is preserved, and the king is required by the doctrine to know both offices in some measure and to honour both.

The doctrine has also shaped the conduct of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), whose [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) are both warriors and miners by long tradition, and whose polity is held by the chroniclers to be a natural expression of the doctrine. The thanes of Stoneheim govern their holds by the twin obligations of the forge and the watch, and the chronicler notes that the holds have endured by this governance through many seasons in which other communities have failed. The teaching holds that the polity which honours both offices will endure where one which honours only one will fail, and the holds are held to be a proof of the teaching.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

The Iron-and-Grain is, like the other teachings of the world, indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the two offices be honoured and that the polity be sustained by their joint labour, and it binds the followers of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and of the smaller local rites alike where they labour in the field or march upon the watch. The polities of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose life is governed by the sea rather than by the field, have sometimes found the doctrine ill-suited to their condition, and the doctrine is in fact less generally observed among the islanders than among the mainlanders. Yet the islanders have their own expression of the complementarity in the arts of the fishery and the marine defence, and the chronicler notes that the doctrine has influenced even those communities that do not observe it in its plain form.

The doctrine is distinct from the doctrines of worship, though it shares with them the language of duty and station. The doctrine does not require of its followers any belief in the divine, and the practitioners of the field and the watch are found among the faithful of every rite. The chronicler notes that this indifference to creed has given the doctrine a breadth of acceptance that the more particular teachings have not enjoyed, and that it is found in settlements of every confession where the practical conditions of the marcher life have called it forth.

## Significance

The Iron-and-Grain endures as one of the foundational teachings of the marcher polities, a discipline of mutual dependence that has preserved the northern settlements through every extremity of season and history. It is a teaching without temple or priesthood, carried in the custom of the household and the watch, and its strength lies in the demonstrable fact that the communities which observe it endure while those which neglect it decline. A village that honours both offices is a village that survives the winter and the raid, and a polity whose governors remember the field and the watch is a polity that is fed and defended alike. In the account of the chronicler, the Iron-and-Grain is among the chief supports of the northern order, and the world is the more secure for the labour of those who keep both the iron and the grain in their proper stations.
