## Overview

The Iron-and-Grain is a teaching of civic duty that holds the warrior and the husbandman to be the two halves of a single polity, neither sufficient without the other and each bound to honour the other's office. It is not a religion and claims no temple or priest, but a discipline of governance and household taught in the marcher lands where the sword and the sickle are both daily necessities. The central precept is that the iron which defends the field is fed by the grain which the field yields; the husbandman who forgets the iron and the warrior who forgets the grain are alike in fault. The doctrine has been refined over generations into a comprehensive ethic of mutual dependence that shapes the laws, households, and councils of the communities where it is observed.

## Origins

The Iron-and-Grain arose in the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches), where settlements along the northern frontier required both a militia and a harvest, and where neglect of either left the community exposed to raid or famine. The warriors of the watch shared in the labours of the field at planting and harvest, and the husbandmen took their turn at the watch when field labour was slack. This practical compact was refined by the elders and captains into the formal teaching now observed throughout the marches.

As the safety of the marches permitted larger settlements, the doctrine was carried into new communities by officers who had learned it on the frontier. The towns of the marches came to require of their leading citizens competence in both military and agricultural arts. The doctrine spread southward into [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) and the towns of the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league), where coastal communities experienced the same complementarity between fishery, field, and shore defence. From the northern kingdoms the doctrine entered the councils of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), where it was adapted to the arts of the horseman and the sower.

## The Two Offices

The doctrine sets forth two offices as the foundation of the polity: the office of iron, which is the defence of the community by arms, and the office of grain, which is its sustenance by the labour of the field. Neither office may claim precedence over the other — the polity defended but unfed is no polity at all, and the polity fed but undefended is no polity for long. The two offices are complementary rather than rival; the practitioner of one must honour the practitioner of the other. This teaching has softened the customary contempt between warrior and husbandman; communities where the doctrine is observed show a mutuality of respect that other communities lack.

The offices should not be confounded. The warrior does not leave his post for the field save at appointed seasons, and the husbandman does not quit his field for the watch save when the watch requires him. The division of offices is itself a discipline; the competence of each is preserved by proper separation.

## The Tribute of the Field

The first article is the tribute of the field, by which the husbandman acknowledges the necessity of the iron and contributes of his store to the warrior's maintenance. The tribute is not a tax in the ordinary sense — the husbandman contributes freely to the defence of the field that feeds him, and the contribution is part of the husbandman's office rather than an imposition. The tribute must be measured so that the husbandman is not impoverished and the warrior is not enriched beyond his station. This measure has been a continual subject of deliberation in communities where the doctrine is observed; prudent magistrates are known by their attention to it.

The tribute is observed in its most elaborate form in the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), where horsemen and sowers maintain a careful balance of contribution and service, and customary rates are revised by the councils of the plains at each generation. The confederation's endurance through many seasons of hardship is attributable in no small part to the steadiness of the tribute.

## The Guardianship of the Watch

The second article is the guardianship of the watch, by which the warrior acknowledges the necessity of the grain and contributes his vigilance to the protection of the husbandman's labour. The warrior who defends the field without honouring the field is a hireling, not a guardian; the office of iron is fulfilled only when the warrior recognises that his own sustenance depends upon the labour he defends. The warrior must know something of the field to understand what he defends, and the husbandman must know something of the watch to honour what defends him. This mutual knowledge has softened the customary estrangement between the two offices.

The guardianship is observed most strictly on the frontier, where settlements are exposed to raids from wild beasts and unsubdued clans beyond the marches. The watchmen of the [Northern Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches) maintain familiarity with the fields and herds within their ward; the husbandmen take their turn at the watch in the season of slack labour. The doctrine has produced a population in which military and agricultural arts are both widely shared.

## The Common Hearth and the Common Wage

The third article binds the two offices into a single polity. The household that contains both warrior and husbandman is the foundation of the commonweal; the polity that honours the household endures. The household must not be impoverished by maintenance of either office, and the wage of warrior and husbandman must permit the continuance of both. This teaching has produced a moderation in the affairs of the marches that the wealthier southern polities do not always observe; the marches lack the extremes of wealth and want that disfigure other communities.

In the villages of the marches, the household of the captain contains also the labour of the field, and the household of the husbandman contributes also to the watch. The village headman must maintain both offices in his own person so far as he is able; the village that fails in this is held to be in decline. The doctrine has been criticised by the philosophers of the southern cities as too narrow a foundation for a polity, yet the marches endure by it while the philosophy of the cities has not always preserved the cities.

## Application in the Greater Polities

Beyond its origins in the marches, the Iron-and-Grain has shaped the conduct of greater polities. [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) is governed by a council in which the marshals of the watch and the reeves of the field sit together. The adaptation has not been without controversy — the greater polity requires a division of offices that the village does not — but the central teaching is preserved: the king must know both offices in some measure and honour both.

The doctrine has also shaped the conduct of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), whose [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) are both warriors and miners by long tradition. The thanes govern their holds by the twin obligations of the forge and the watch; the holds have endured by this governance through many seasons in which other communities have failed.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

The Iron-and-Grain is indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the two offices be honoured and the polity be sustained by their joint labour, and it binds followers of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and smaller local rites alike where they labour in the field or march upon the watch. The polities of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose life is governed by the sea rather than the field, have sometimes found the doctrine ill-suited to their condition; yet they have their own expression of the complementarity in the arts of the fishery and marine defence.

The doctrine does not require belief in the divine. Practitioners of the field and the watch are found among the faithful of every rite. This indifference to creed has given the doctrine a breadth of acceptance that more particular teachings have not enjoyed.

## Significance

The Iron-and-Grain endures as a foundational teaching of the marcher polities — a discipline of mutual dependence that has preserved the northern settlements through every extremity of season and history. It has no temple or priesthood; it is carried in the custom of the household and the watch, and its strength lies in the demonstrable fact that the communities which observe it endure while those which neglect it decline. A village that honours both offices survives the winter and the raid; a polity whose governors remember the field and the watch is fed and defended alike. The Iron-and-Grain is among the chief supports of the northern order, and the world is the more secure for the labour of those who keep both the iron and the grain in their proper stations.
