## Overview

The Doctrine of the Open Hand is an ethical and social teaching concerned above all with the obligations of hospitality and the bonds of mutual aid that hold a community together. It is not a religion, for it makes no claim upon the divine, but a school of conduct, and its influence is felt most strongly among the trading and caravan peoples of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) and the maritime communities of the narrow seas. The central precept of the doctrine is that the traveller and the stranger are owed shelter and sustenance without reckoning of price, and that the health of any people is measured by the openness of its hand to those who pass through it. The doctrine has been refined over generations into a comprehensive account of debt, gift, and obligation that reaches far beyond the tent door or the inn yard.

## Origins among the Desert Peoples

The roots of the Open Hand lie in the caravan life of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), the great desert of southern Sahrana, where the survival of the traveller has always depended upon the generosity of those who hold the water and the shade. The nomadic confederations of the desert, among them the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), preserved from ancient times a code of hospitality so strict that the refusal of shelter to a stranger was accounted the gravest of offences, for in the desert the withholding of water is the withholding of life. From this practical necessity grew a broader ethic, taught by the elders and the storytellers of the tribes, that the open hand is the foundation upon which all society rests.

As the caravan routes of the Kharesh carried goods and peoples between the Sahrani coast and the interior, the doctrine travelled with them, and the settled merchants of the northern Sahrani cities adopted its principles as the basis of their own commercial honour. The coastal city-states of Sahrana, prosperous and cultured, came to regard the Open Hand as a mark of civilisation, and the ruin of a merchant's name through a breach of its obligations was held to be a worse misfortune than the loss of a caravan. From Sahrana the teaching passed along the trade routes to the straits and coasts where many peoples met, and it is in these cosmopolitan waters that the doctrine found its fullest development as a philosophy binding strangers of every people.

## The Precept of Shelter

The first article of the Open Hand is the duty of shelter, which holds that any who come to a household or a settlement in need of rest, water, or food are to be received without question of their people, their faith, or their errand. The doctrine teaches that the stranger at the door is under the protection of the household for the span of his need, and that to harm a guest is to break the order of the world. This precept is observed most strictly in the desert and among the caravans, where the guest may be a rival trader or a stranger of unknown kind, yet is fed and sheltered alike, for the doctrine holds that the test of the open hand is precisely its extension to those from whom no return is expected.

The chronicler may observe that this precept accords with the practical wisdom of the arid lands, where a traveller turned away may perish and a traveller succoured may one day succour the giver in turn. The doctrine clothes this wisdom in the language of honour rather than of calculation, and it is this framing that has given the Open Hand its durability, for men will keep a code of honour where they might neglect a mere prudence.

## The Ledger of Obligation

Beyond the simple shelter of the stranger, the Doctrine of the Open Hand sets forth a detailed accounting of gift and debt that governs the relations of those who deal with one another over time. The doctrine holds that every kindness given freely creates an obligation in the receiver, not a debt to be paid in coin but a bond to be honoured when the occasion arises. This ledger is kept not in writing but in memory, for the doctrine teaches that the obligation truly felt is worth more than the obligation recorded, and that the keeping of the account in the mind binds the giver and the receiver in a continuing relation of mutual care.

The merchants of the Sahrani coast are noted for the diligence with which they maintain this ledger of obligation, and a trader of standing is known as much by the number of bonds he honours as by the weight of his purse. The doctrine holds further that an obligation discharged too quickly, or repaid with exact measure, is a slight, for it implies that the original kindness was a transaction rather than a gift. The open hand gives without counting, and it receives in the same spirit, and the chronicler notes that this teaching has produced among the caravan peoples a web of trust that spans great distances and many peoples, sustaining commerce where law and enforcement are thin.

## Application among the Maritime Peoples

The Doctrine of the Open Hand found a second home among the maritime communities of the narrow seas, where the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) bring together the peoples of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) in close and constant exchange. The shipwrecked sailor, the stranded passenger, and the vessel in want of provision are all reckoned within the shelter of the open hand, and the port towns of the strait are known for a tolerance and an openness to strangers that the doctrine has done much to shape. Marriages and partnerships across the shores of the Stormfall, already common, are further eased by a shared ethic that holds the stranger to be a guest before he is a foreigner.

The [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose scattered communities depend upon the goodwill of the sea and the ports, have likewise taken up the Open Hand as a principle of their confederation, and the islanders are reputed among the most hospitable of the ocean peoples. The doctrine suits the island life, for a community surrounded by water has frequent need of the passing ship and equally frequent occasion to be the passing ship's benefactor.

## Relation to Realm and Creed

The Open Hand is notable among the teachings of the world for its indifference to the boundaries of realm and creed. It asks of those who would follow it only that they keep the shelter and honour the bond, and it binds [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves), [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves), and the lesser peoples alike where they meet in trade and travel. The great kingdoms of the continents have at times found the doctrine inconvenient, for it sets the obligation of the guest above the claims of the tax collector and the magistrate, yet none has succeeded in suppressing it, for it rests upon customs too deeply rooted in the caravan and the port to be rooted out by edict.

The doctrine stands apart from the faiths of the world, including the monotheistic [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame) and the polytheistic [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-pantheon-of-the-plains), neither contradicting their teachings nor depending upon them, and many of the faithful of those creeds are also followers of the Open Hand, seeing in its precepts a complement to the duties of their religion rather than a rival to it.

## Significance

The Doctrine of the Open Hand endures as one of the most socially consequential of the world's teachings, for it supplies the trust upon which the long-distance commerce of the known world is built. From the caravan wells of the Kharesh to the harbours of the strait, the open hand is the quiet engine of exchange between peoples who might otherwise regard one another with suspicion, and its precept of shelter has preserved the lives of countless travellers and the honour of countless hosts. It is a teaching of no temple and no priesthood, carried in the memory and the practice of those who keep it, and its strength lies precisely in this diffuseness, for a doctrine held by all is harder to destroy than one held by a few. In the account of the chronicler, the Open Hand is among the chief bonds that hold the diverse peoples of the world in a single, if loose, fellowship of mutual obligation.
