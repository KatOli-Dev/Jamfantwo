## Overview

Deep Grove is a teaching of nature and conduct that holds the great forests of the world to be the elder kindred of all growing things, and the human dwellers of the forest to be the guests and the wardens of a household not their own. It is not a religion in the ordinary sense, for it makes no claim upon the divine and is not centred upon any temple, but a discipline of forest life taught by the elders of the woodland peoples and by the silent company of those who have chosen the grove over the town. The central teaching of the philosophy is that the grove is a polity of its own, older and more patient than any realm of [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), and that the dwellers of the grove are bound to the polity of the grove by duties that precede the claims of any human lord. The philosophy has been refined over generations into a comprehensive ethic of forest conduct that has shaped the customs of the woodland communities from the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) to the sylvan outposts of the northern marches.

## Origins among the Sylvan Peoples

Deep Grove is generally held to have arisen among the sylvan [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), whose life within the great forest had long taught them a regard for the polity of the trees that the dwellers of the open plains did not possess. The chroniclers of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) preserve accounts of an older compact by which the sylvan communities regulated their cutting of the timber and their hunting of the deer so that the forest was not impoverished by their use. From this practical compact the present philosophy is held to descend, refined by the elders of the sylvan communities into the formal teaching now observed throughout the woodland and in the outposts that have grown up upon its borders.

As the trade of the lowland kingdoms extended and the sylvan outposts came into closer contact with the towns and the markets, the philosophy was carried into the new communities by the elders who had been trained in the heartwood, and the woodland towns of the marches took up its precepts as the foundation of their forest conduct. The philosophy found a ready welcome among the half-elven communities of the forest fringe, whose life in the grove and in the town required of them a regard for both jurisdictions. The chronicler notes that the philosophy has been of especial service to the smaller communities of the forest, where the pressure of the market might otherwise have tempted the dwellers to a cutting of the timber that the grove could not sustain, and that the discipline of the philosophy has preserved many a tract of forest that the unregulated market would have stripped.

## The Two Polities

The philosophy begins with the recognition that the grove is a polity of its own, and that the dwellers of the grove are members of two polities at once: the human polity of their village, town, or court, and the sylvan polity of the grove. The teaching holds that the two polities are not rivals but companions, and that the human polity which disregards the polity of the grove is held to have overstepped the proper limit of its office. The dwellers of the grove are required to honour both polities and to refuse the command of either when the command would work harm to the other. The chronicler notes that this teaching has done much to preserve the harmony of the sylvan communities, where the dwellers are accustomed to the discipline of the double membership.

The two polities are observed in their most distinct form in the sylvan communities of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), where the life of the community is ordered by the sylvan court and the elders of the court are responsible to both polities. The chronicler notes that the sylvan court has refined the philosophy into a fine discipline of the double membership, and that the officers of the court are trained from youth in the duties of both polities. The teaching holds that the double membership is itself a discipline, and that the sylvan who neglects either polity is held to have failed in his office. The chronicler notes that this discipline has preserved the harmony of the heartwood through many seasons in which the harmony of other forests has been disturbed.

## The Cutting of the Timber

The first article of the philosophy is the cutting of the timber, by which the dwellers of the grove are required to take only so much of the forest as their need requires and to refrain from the wasteful cutting that the philosophy holds to be a breach of the polity of the grove. The teaching holds that the forest is a household that grows slowly and that the cutting which exceeds the growth is held to be a cutting that consumes the household, and the cutter who exceeds his need is held to have offended against the polity of the grove. The discipline is enforced by the customary law of the sylvan communities and by the watchfulness of the elders, who are required to observe the cutting and to correct the cutter who exceeds his need. The chronicler notes that this discipline has done much to preserve the timber of the heartwood and the marches, and that the forests where the philosophy is observed are marked by a steadiness of growth that the unregulated forests do not possess.

The cutting of the timber is observed in its most formal form in the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court), where the customary law of the court prescribes the amount of the cutting and the seasons of the felling, and the forester of the court is required to render an account of the cutting at the appointed times. The chronicler notes that the court has maintained a careful record of the timber for as long as any chronicler can attest, and that the record shows a balance between the cutting and the growth that the unregulated forests do not show. The teaching holds that the balance between the cutting and the growth is itself a discipline, and that the community which maintains it is the community which preserves the grove.

## The Hunting of the Deer

The second article of the philosophy is the hunting of the deer, by which the dwellers of the grove are required to take only such game as the forest can sustain and to refrain from the slaughter that would diminish the herd below the number that the grove can feed. The teaching holds that the deer are a part of the polity of the grove and that the hunter who exceeds the proper number is held to have offended against the polity of the grove, and the elders of the sylvan communities are required to watch the hunt and to correct the hunter who exceeds his need. The discipline is enforced by the customary law of the sylvan communities and by the watchfulness of the elders, and the chronicler notes that the forests where the philosophy is observed are marked by a steadiness of the herd that the unregulated forests do not possess.

The hunting of the deer is observed in its most characteristic form in the smaller sylvan communities of the marches, where the hunt is a part of the seasonal round and the elders of the community regulate the taking of the deer. The chronicler notes that the custom of the marches requires the hunter to give account of the game he has taken and to submit to the correction of the elders if his taking has exceeded the proper number. The teaching holds that the regulation of the hunt is itself a discipline, and that the community which maintains it is the community which preserves the deer. The chronicler notes that this discipline has done much to preserve the deer of the marches, and that the hunters of the regulated forests are held in good repute throughout the woodland for the moderation of their taking.

## The Keeping of the Water

The third article of the philosophy is the keeping of the water, by which the dwellers of the grove are required to maintain the springs and the streams of the forest so that the water is not fouled by the settlement or wasted by the cutting. The teaching holds that the water is a part of the polity of the grove and that the dweller who fouls the spring or wastes the stream is held to have offended against the polity of the grove, and the elders of the sylvan communities are required to watch the water and to correct the offender. The discipline is enforced by the customary law of the sylvan communities and by the watchfulness of the elders, and the chronicler notes that the forests where the philosophy is observed are marked by a cleanness of the water that the unregulated forests do not possess.

The keeping of the water is observed in its most rigorous form in the sylvan outposts of the heartwood, where the springs are the source of the larger streams and the fouling of a single spring may be felt in the lower country. The chronicler notes that the elders of the outposts are required to inspect the springs at the appointed seasons and to correct any fouling that they observe. The teaching holds that the cleanness of the water is itself a discipline, and that the community which maintains it is the community which preserves the springs. The chronicler notes that this discipline has done much to preserve the streams of the heartwood, and that the dwellers of the lower country are accustomed to regard the water of the sylvan outposts with particular regard.

## The Hospitality of the Grove

The fourth article of the philosophy is the hospitality of the grove, by which the dwellers of the forest are required to receive the traveller who enters the woodland and to give him such guidance and shelter as the grove affords. The teaching holds that the grove is a place of refuge as well as a place of polity, and that the traveller who enters the woodland in good faith is under the protection of the sylvan community for so long as his need requires. The discipline is observed with a particular rigour among the sylvan communities of the heartwood, where the custom of the woodland requires the dweller to guide the traveller to the nearest settlement and to supply him with such provision as the season permits. The chronicler notes that this custom has done much to make the forest a place of safe passage, and that the traveller who enters the regulated woodland is held to be under the protection of the grove.

The hospitality of the grove is distinct from the doctrines of hospitality that are observed by the other teachings of the world, for the philosophy does not bind the host to the elaborate courtesies of the town. The hospitality of the grove is a simpler practice, requiring only the guidance of the traveller and the sharing of such provision as the forest affords, and the grove does not maintain the formal courtesies of the hearth. The chronicler notes that this simplicity has given the philosophy a particular character that the doctrines of hospitality do not possess, and that the traveller of the forest is held to a simpler expectation than the traveller of the town. The teaching holds that the hospitality of the grove is the forest expression of the human duty of shelter, and that the sylvan who neglects it has forgotten the meaning of the polity of the grove.

## The Patient Listening

The fifth article of the philosophy is the patient listening, by which the dwellers of the grove are required to attend to the sounds of the forest and to read the signs of the woodland as the officers of the marches read the signs of the frontier. The teaching holds that the grove speaks to those who will hear it, and that the dweller who cannot read the signs is a dweller in name only. The discipline is taught by a graduated series of exercises, beginning with the recognition of the songs of the common birds and rising to the recognition of the warnings of the rarer beasts and the silences that precede the storm. The chronicler notes that this art requires a long apprenticeship and a certain natural quietness of mind, and that not every settler of the forest fringe is suited to the patient listening.

The patient listening is observed in its most characteristic form among the elders of the sylvan communities, who are said to maintain a watchfulness for the signs of the grove through every season of the year. The chronicler notes that the elders of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) are required to give account of the signs at the appointed seasons, and that the record of the signs is preserved in the archives of the court. The teaching holds that the patient listening is itself a discipline, and that the community which maintains it is the community which reads the polity of the grove. The chronicler notes that this discipline has done much to preserve the harmony of the sylvan communities, where the dwellers are accustomed to the reading of the signs and to the regulation of their conduct by what they hear.

## The Right of the Lone Tree

Beyond the formal articles of the philosophy, the customary practice of the right of the lone tree is observed by many of its practitioners, particularly in the sylvan communities of the heartwood. The right of the lone tree is the right of any tree of remarkable age or size to stand undisturbed by the cutter, and the teaching holds that the lone tree is a part of the polity of the grove that may not be cut without the consent of the elders. The chronicler notes that this right has preserved many a remarkable tree that the unregulated market would have felled, and that the sylvan communities regard the lone trees with a reverence that the townsmen sometimes find excessive. The teaching holds that the lone tree is the elder of the grove and that the elder may not be cut without the consent of the community, and the chronicler notes that this discipline has done much to preserve the character of the heartwood.

The right of the lone tree is observed in its most characteristic form in the older forests of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), where the remarkable trees are marked by the elders and the cutters are required to leave them standing. The chronicler notes that the custom of the heartwood is enforced by the regard of the sylvan community and by the watchfulness of the elders, and that the cutter who fells a marked tree is held to have offended against the polity of the grove. The teaching holds that the preservation of the lone tree is itself a discipline, and that the community which maintains it is the community which preserves the character of the grove.

## Application in the Sylvan Outposts

Deep Grove has been of especial service to the sylvan outposts that stand at the meeting of the forest and the settled land, where the discipline of the grove is required to moderate the pressure of the market. The chronicler notes that the outposts of the marches are held by small garrisons of the philosophy, and that the elders of the outposts are responsible to both the sylvan court and the marcher captain. The teaching holds that the outpost which maintains the discipline of the grove is the outpost which preserves its forest, and the chronicler notes that the outposts where the philosophy is observed are marked by a steadiness of the timber and the deer that the outposts where it is neglected do not possess. The chronicler further notes that the discipline of the philosophy has done much to preserve the harmony of the forest fringe, where the settler and the forester are accustomed to regard one another as members of the same polity of the grove.

The philosophy has also shaped the conduct of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court), whose officers are trained in the disciplines of the grove as well as in the customary law of the court. The chronicler notes that the court has refined the philosophy into a comprehensive discipline of forest governance, and that the officers of the court are required to render an account of their stewardship at the appointed seasons. The teaching holds that the discipline of the forest is itself an excellence, and that the court which maintains it is the court which preserves the grove. The chronicler notes that this discipline has preserved the heartwood through many seasons in which the unregulated forests have been diminished, and that the sylvan communities regard the court with the particular regard that is owed to a faithful steward.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

Deep Grove is, like the other teachings of the world, indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the polity of the grove be honoured and that the duties of the dwellers be discharged, and it binds the followers of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and of the smaller local rites alike where they dwell within the woodland. The polities of the open plains, with their wide horizons and their settled towns, have sometimes viewed the philosophy as a peculiarity of the sylvan peoples, and the philosophy is in fact less generally observed in the open country than in the woodland. Yet the philosophy has been taken up by the foresters of the royal domains and by the officers of the greater polities where their jurisdiction extends into the woodland, and the chronicler notes that the teaching has proved itself capable of crossing the customary frontiers.

The philosophy is distinct from the doctrines of worship, though it shares with them the language of the elder kindred and the patient regard. The philosophy does not require of its practitioners any belief in the divine nor any participation in the rites of the temples; it asks only the observance of the grove and the discharge of the duties of the dwellers. The chronicler notes that this distinction has made the philosophy acceptable to the sylvan peoples and to the half-elven communities of the forest fringe, who hold their faith lightly and their grove firmly, and that it has been adopted in the outposts precisely because it makes no demands beyond its own observance.

## Significance

Deep Grove endures as one of the most quietly efficacious of the world's teachings, a discipline of forest conduct that has preserved the great woodlands through every extremity of season and history. It is a teaching without temple or priesthood, carried in the custom of the sylvan communities and in the patient practice of the elders, and its strength lies in the daily observance of the dwellers who maintain it. A grove that is observed by the philosophy is a grove that endures, and a community that observes the philosophy is a community that is fed by the forest without diminishing it. In the account of the chronicler, the Deep Grove is among the chief supports of the sylvan order, and the world is the more kindly for the patient regard of those who keep the polity of the grove.
