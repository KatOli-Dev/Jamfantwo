## Overview

Deep Grove is a teaching of nature and conduct that holds the great forests to be the elder kindred of all growing things and their human dwellers to be guests and wardens of a household not their own. It is not a religion — it makes no claim upon the divine and has no temple — but a discipline of forest life taught by the elders of the woodland peoples and by those who have chosen the grove over the town. The central teaching is that the grove is a polity of its own, older and more patient than any realm of [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), and that its dwellers are bound to it by duties that precede the claims of any human lord. The philosophy has shaped the customs of woodland communities from the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) to the sylvan outposts of the northern marches.

## Origins

Deep Grove arose among the sylvan [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), whose life in the great forest taught them a regard for the polity of the trees that dwellers of the open plains did not possess. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) preserves accounts of an older compact by which sylvan communities regulated timber cutting and deer hunting so that the forest was not impoverished by their use. The elders refined this compact into the formal teaching now observed throughout the woodland and its outposts.

As trade with lowland kingdoms extended, the philosophy was carried to new communities by elders trained in the heartwood. The woodland towns of the marches took up its precepts as the foundation of their forest conduct. It found a welcome among the half-elven communities of the forest fringe, whose life in both grove and town required regard for both jurisdictions. The philosophy has been of especial service to smaller forest communities, where market pressure might otherwise have tempted dwellers to unsustainable cutting — the discipline has preserved many tracts of forest that the unregulated market would have stripped.

## The Two Polities

The philosophy recognises that the grove is a polity of its own and that its dwellers are members of two polities simultaneously: the human polity of their village, town, or court, and the sylvan polity of the grove. The two are not rivals but companions; a human polity that disregards the grove has overstepped the proper limit of its office. Dwellers must honour both polities and refuse the command of either when it would harm the other.

The two polities are observed in their most distinct form in the sylvan communities of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), where community life is ordered by the sylvan court and the elders are responsible to both polities. The sylvan court has refined the philosophy into a discipline of double membership; officers are trained from youth in the duties of both polities. The sylvan who neglects either polity has failed in his office. This discipline has preserved the harmony of the heartwood through many seasons in which other forests suffered disturbance.

## The Cutting of Timber

The first article of the philosophy requires dwellers to take only as much timber as their need requires and to refrain from wasteful cutting, which breaches the polity of the grove. The forest grows slowly; cutting that exceeds growth consumes the household itself. The discipline is enforced by the customary law of sylvan communities and by the elders, who observe the cutting and correct the cutter who exceeds his need.

This is observed in its most formal form in the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court), where customary law prescribes the amount and seasons of felling, and the forester renders an account at appointed times. The court has maintained a careful record of timber for as long as any account attests, showing a balance between cutting and growth that unregulated forests do not show.

## The Hunting of Deer

The second article requires dwellers to take only such game as the forest can sustain and to refrain from slaughter that would diminish the herd below what the grove can feed. The deer are part of the polity of the grove; the hunter who exceeds the proper number has offended against it. The elders watch the hunt and correct the hunter who exceeds his need.

This is observed most characteristically in the smaller sylvan communities of the marches, where the hunt is part of the seasonal round. The hunter must give account of the game taken and submit to correction if his taking exceeded the proper number. The forests where the philosophy is observed show a steadiness of the herd that unregulated forests do not possess. Hunters of the regulated forests are held in good repute throughout the woodland for their moderation.

## The Keeping of Water

The third article requires dwellers to maintain the springs and streams so that the water is not fouled by settlement or wasted by cutting. The water is part of the polity of the grove; the dweller who fouls a spring or wastes a stream has offended against it. The elders watch the water and correct the offender.

This is observed most rigorously in the sylvan outposts of the heartwood, where springs feed larger streams and the fouling of a single spring may be felt downstream. The elders inspect the springs at appointed seasons and correct any fouling they observe. Dwellers of the lower country regard the water of the sylvan outposts with particular regard.

## Hospitality of the Grove

The fourth article requires dwellers to receive the traveller who enters the woodland and to give him such guidance and shelter as the grove affords. The grove is a place of refuge as well as polity; the traveller who enters in good faith is under the protection of the sylvan community for as long as his need requires. This is observed with particular rigour among the sylvan communities of the heartwood, where custom requires the dweller to guide the traveller to the nearest settlement and supply him with such provision as the season permits.

The hospitality of the grove is distinct from doctrines of hospitality observed by other teachings. It does not bind the host to the elaborate courtesies of the town — it is a simpler practice requiring only guidance and the sharing of such provision as the forest affords. This simplicity gives the philosophy a particular character; the traveller of the forest is held to a simpler expectation than the traveller of the town. The hospitality of the grove is the forest expression of the human duty of shelter; the sylvan who neglects it has forgotten the meaning of the polity of the grove.

## The Patient Listening

The fifth article requires dwellers to attend to the sounds of the forest and read the signs of the woodland, as officers of the marches read the signs of the frontier. The grove speaks to those who will hear it; the dweller who cannot read the signs is a dweller in name only. The discipline is taught through a graduated series of exercises, from recognition of common bird songs to recognition of the warnings of rarer beasts and the silences that precede the storm. This art requires a long apprenticeship and a certain natural quietness of mind; not every settler of the forest fringe is suited to it.

The patient listening is observed most characteristically among the elders of the sylvan communities, who maintain a watchfulness for the signs of the grove through every season. The elders of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) give account of the signs at appointed seasons, and the record is preserved in the archives. This discipline has preserved the harmony of sylvan communities where dwellers are accustomed to reading the signs and regulating their conduct by what they hear.

## The Right of the Lone Tree

Many practitioners observe the right of the lone tree — the right of any tree of remarkable age or size to stand undisturbed by the cutter. Such a tree is part of the polity of the grove and may not be cut without the consent of the elders. This right has preserved many remarkable trees that the unregulated market would have felled; the sylvan communities regard lone trees with a reverence that townsmen sometimes find excessive. The lone tree is the elder of the grove and may not be cut without community consent.

This right is most characteristic of the older forests of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), where remarkable trees are marked by the elders and cutters must leave them standing. The custom is enforced by the regard of the sylvan community; the cutter who fells a marked tree has offended against the polity of the grove.

## Application in the Sylvan Outposts

Deep Grove has been of especial service to sylvan outposts at the meeting of forest and settled land, where the discipline moderates market pressure. The outposts of the marches are held by small garrisons of the philosophy; elders are responsible to both the sylvan court and the marcher captain. Outposts where the philosophy is observed show steadiness of timber and deer that those neglecting it do not possess. The discipline has preserved the harmony of the forest fringe, where settler and forester regard one another as members of the same polity of the grove.

The philosophy has also shaped the conduct of the [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court), whose officers are trained in the disciplines of the grove as well as in customary law. The court has refined the philosophy into a comprehensive discipline of forest governance; officers render account of their stewardship at appointed seasons. This discipline has preserved the heartwood through many seasons in which unregulated forests have been diminished.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

Deep Grove is indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the polity of the grove be honoured, and it binds followers of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and smaller local rites alike where they dwell within the woodland. The polities of the open plains have sometimes viewed the philosophy as a peculiarity of the sylvan peoples. Yet foresters of the royal domains and officers of greater polities have taken it up where their jurisdiction extends into woodland, and the teaching has proved itself capable of crossing customary frontiers.

The philosophy is distinct from doctrines of worship. It does not require belief in the divine or participation in temple rites — it asks only observance of the grove and the duties of the dwellers. This distinction has made it acceptable to sylvan peoples and half-elven communities who hold their faith lightly and their grove firmly.

## Significance

Deep Grove endures as one of the most quietly efficacious of the world's teachings — a discipline of forest conduct that has preserved the great woodlands through every extremity of season and history. It has no temple or priesthood; it is carried in the custom of sylvan communities and the patient practice of the elders. Its strength lies in daily observance: a grove observed by the philosophy endures, and a community that observes it is fed by the forest without diminishing it. Deep Grove is among the chief supports of the sylvan order, and the world is the more kindly for the patient regard of those who keep the polity of the grove.
