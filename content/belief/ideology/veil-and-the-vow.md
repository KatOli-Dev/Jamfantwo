## Overview

The Veil and the Vow is a teaching of conduct that governs the bearing of secrets, the giving of oaths, and the conduct of those whose lives are spent in the spaces between courts, caravans, and kindreds. It is not a religion and claims no temple, priest, or revealed text. It is a discipline learned in the household, the embassy, and the merchant house, and its followers are bound to one another less by creed than by the careful observance of the vow once given. The teaching takes its name from the two halves of its central practice: the veil, which conceals what must be concealed, and the vow, which binds the speaker to the truth of what is uttered when the veil is lifted. Together they form an ethic of measured speech and honoured commitment that has shaped the conduct of envoys, factors, and quiet servants of the great houses for as long as any account attests.

## Origins

The Veil and the Vow took its present form among the envoys and factors of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), where the courts of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) have long maintained embassies in one another's capitals and where the smallest indiscretion may cost a treaty or trade charter. An older compact among the guild of translators and ledger keepers, who served the embassies without oath to any crown and were trusted by all because they were bound to none, formed the basis of the present teaching. The household masters and senior factors refined this compact into the formal discipline now observed.

The discipline spread inland along the trade roads, where the factors of the great houses carried its precepts into the counting rooms of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar), and the cities of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles). It found an especially ready welcome in the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), whose member cities had long experience of treaty and counter-treaty and whose merchants depended upon the stability of bargains made across linguistic and customary frontiers. From these cosmopolitan centres the teaching entered the service of the greater kingdoms; the envoys of [Valdmark](/content/government/national/valdmark), the [Northern Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches), and the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) are among its more notable practitioners.

## The Veil: The Law of Concealment

The first half of the teaching is the veil: what is entrusted in confidence must remain concealed so long as the confidence stands, and the concealment must be entire rather than partial. The teaching distinguishes three classes of secret. The private concerns a single household or person. The official concerns the affairs of a court, guild, or polity. The mortal concerns a matter of life and death, such as a place of refuge or the identity of one who would be hunted if known. Each class is bound by its own degree of concealment; the bearer of a mortal secret is held to a stricter silence than the bearer of a private one, for the consequence of breach is graver.

Concealment is not a licence for evasion but a positive duty. The one who has accepted a confidence has taken on a burden that may not be set down at convenience. Factors of the Veldenwatch guild have refused the direct questioning of their own lords rather than betray the secrets of a foreign court; these refusals, once notorious, came to be regarded as the mark of a practitioner of the discipline rather than as an act of defiance. The veil places the obligation above the inclination of the moment.

## The Vow: The Law of Spoken Word

The second half of the teaching is the vow, which governs what is uttered when the veil is lifted and the speaker must declare openly. The spoken word, once given in a binding form, is to be honoured even at cost to the speaker; the lightness with which a vow is given is itself a breach of the discipline. The teaching distinguishes several forms of vow. The most solemn is the named vow, by which the speaker binds his own name and the name of his house to the truth of what he says. Next is the conditional vow, by which the speaker binds himself to a stated consequence should the assertion prove false. Finally the plain vow, which is the everyday undertaking of merchants and envoys in the ordinary course of business.

The vow is the visible half of an otherwise hidden compact; the honour of the veil is preserved precisely because the vow is feared to be true. A practitioner of the discipline speaks but rarely, and when he speaks he is listened to with great care, for it is understood that he has weighed the words against the cost of their falsehood. This practice has given the senior factors of the great houses a peculiar authority in councils where louder voices prevail — an authority that rests not upon office but upon the demonstrated willingness to be bound.

## The Forms of Binding

The teaching sets forth formal modes by which the veil and the vow are entered, each suited to the gravity of the matter. The lightest is the spoken undertaking, a promise made before witnesses sufficient to recall it. The next is the written undertaking, sealed and delivered, which is the common form among merchants and preserved in the archives of counting houses. The third is the sworn undertaking, made upon a token of office or kindred and accompanied by the calling of witnesses. The fourth and gravest is the bound undertaking, by which the speaker pledges his own person, his household, or his honour as surety, and from which there is no release save by the discharge of the matter pledged.

The form of binding must match the matter in hand: an excessive form constrains unjustly, while an insufficient form dishonours both parties. The masters of the discipline in the embassies of the strait keep a mental catalogue of forms and match them to occasions with a precision that the uninitiated find inscrutable. This precision has made the embassies of the strait places of slow but reliable negotiation, where neither side expects to be deceived in a matter of declared word.

## Conduct of the Bearer

Beyond the formal practice of veil and vow, the teaching sets forth an ethic of conduct for the bearer in ordinary life. The bearer is sparing of speech, neither forward in council nor silent in counsel, and weighs each utterance for its consequence before it passes the lips. The one who has learned to conceal well has also learned to speak well; the two disciplines support one another. The bearer is further enjoined against bearing two confidences that conflict and is taught to refuse one rather than to carry both, for the bearer of conflicting secrets is already compromised.

The teaching is not ascetic. It does not require withdrawal from the world, renunciation of pleasure, or peculiar dress. It is satisfied with the observance of its two halves in the commerce and counsel of ordinary life. This modesty of aim, as much as the rigour of its precepts, has given the teaching its long durability.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

The Veil and the Vow is indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the veil be kept and the vow honoured, and it binds followers of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and the [Kharesh Spirits](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/kharesh-spirits) alike where they meet in the conduct of office. The kings of [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/nordhaven) and [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) have at times viewed the discipline with suspicion, for it sets the obligation of the spoken vow above the command of the throne, yet none has succeeded in suppressing it, for the commerce of the world depends upon it too deeply.

The discipline sits easily with the [Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/open-hand), for both honour the bond given and the confidence received; the factors of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast are often practitioners of both. It stands apart from the codes of the warrior and the husbandman, which govern the field and the household rather than the council and the counting house. This separation is where its usefulness lies: a teaching confined to its proper sphere does not collide with the teachings of other spheres.

## Significance

The Veil and the Vow endures as one of the quiet authorities of the world — an unwritten compact that holds the embassies and counting houses to their word and protects the confidences upon which treaty and trade depend. It has no temple or priesthood; it is preserved by example and by the careful training of the household, and its strength lies in the long memory of those who practise it. A factor who has once broken a vow is a factor no longer; a translator who has once betrayed a confidence is a translator no longer, for the discipline is enforced not by any court but by the severer court of professional reputation. The Veil and the Vow is among the chief supports of the diplomatic and commercial order, and the world is the more orderly for the silent observance of those who keep it.
