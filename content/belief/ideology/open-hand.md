## Overview

Open Hand is an ethical and social teaching concerned with the obligations of hospitality and the bonds of mutual aid that hold a community together. It is not a religion, for it makes no claim upon the divine, but a school of conduct whose influence is felt most strongly among the trading and caravan peoples of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) and the maritime communities of the narrow seas. The central precept is that the traveller and the stranger are owed shelter and sustenance without reckoning of price, and that the health of any people is measured by the openness of its hand to those who pass through it. The doctrine has been refined over generations into a comprehensive account of debt, gift, and obligation that reaches far beyond the tent door or the inn yard.

## Origins

The roots of Open Hand lie in the caravan life of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), the great desert of southern Sahrana, where survival has always depended upon the generosity of those who hold the water and the shade. The nomadic confederations of the desert, among them the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), preserved an ancient code of hospitality so strict that the refusal of shelter to a stranger was accounted the gravest of offences, for in the desert the withholding of water is the withholding of life. From this practical necessity grew a broader ethic that the open hand is the foundation upon which all society rests.

As the caravan routes of the Kharesh carried goods and peoples between the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast and the interior, the doctrine travelled with them. The settled merchants of the northern Sahrani cities adopted its principles as the basis of their commercial honour. The coastal city-states of Sahrana came to regard the Open Hand as a mark of civilisation; the ruin of a merchant's name through a breach of its obligations was held to be a worse misfortune than the loss of a caravan. From Sahrana the teaching passed along the trade routes to the straits and coasts where many peoples met, and it is in these cosmopolitan waters that the doctrine found its fullest development as a philosophy binding strangers of every people.

## The Precept of Shelter

The first article of the Open Hand is the duty of shelter: any who come to a household or settlement in need of rest, water, or food are to be received without question of their people, faith, or errand. The stranger at the door is under the protection of the household for the span of his need; to harm a guest is to break the order of the world. This precept is observed most strictly in the desert and among the caravans, where the guest may be a rival trader or a stranger of unknown kind yet is fed and sheltered alike, for the test of the open hand is precisely its extension to those from whom no return is expected.

This precept accords with the practical wisdom of the arid lands: a traveller turned away may perish, and a traveller succoured may one day succour the giver in turn. The doctrine clothes this wisdom in the language of honour rather than calculation, and this framing has given the Open Hand its durability — men will keep a code of honour where they might neglect a mere prudence.

## The Ledger of Obligation

Beyond the simple shelter of the stranger, the Open Hand sets forth a detailed accounting of gift and debt that governs relations between those who deal with one another over time. Every kindness given freely creates an obligation in the receiver — not a debt to be paid in coin but a bond to be honoured when the occasion arises. This ledger is kept not in writing but in memory, for the obligation truly felt is worth more than the obligation recorded, and the keeping of the account in the mind binds giver and receiver in a continuing relation of mutual care.

The merchants of the Sahrani coast are noted for the diligence with which they maintain this ledger. A trader of standing is known as much by the number of bonds he honours as by the weight of his purse. An obligation discharged too quickly, or repaid with exact measure, is a slight, for it implies that the original kindness was a transaction rather than a gift. The open hand gives without counting and receives in the same spirit. This teaching has produced among the caravan peoples a web of trust that spans great distances and many peoples, sustaining commerce where law and enforcement are thin.

## Application among the Maritime Peoples

Open Hand found a second home among the maritime communities of the narrow seas, where the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) bring together the peoples of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) in close and constant exchange. The shipwrecked sailor, the stranded passenger, and the vessel in want of provision are all reckoned within the shelter of the open hand. The port towns of the strait are known for a tolerance and openness to strangers that the doctrine has done much to shape. Marriages and partnerships across the shores of the Stormfall, already common, are further eased by a shared ethic that holds the stranger to be a guest before he is a foreigner.

The [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose scattered communities depend upon the goodwill of the sea and the ports, have likewise taken up the Open Hand as a principle of their confederation. The islanders are reputed among the most hospitable of the ocean peoples: a community surrounded by water has frequent need of the passing ship and equally frequent occasion to be the passing ship's benefactor.

## Relation to Realm and Creed

Open Hand is notable among the teachings for its indifference to the boundaries of realm and creed. It asks only that the shelter be kept and the bond honoured, and it binds [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves), [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves), and the lesser peoples alike where they meet in trade and travel. The great kingdoms have at times found the doctrine inconvenient, for it sets the obligation of the guest above the claims of the tax collector and the magistrate, yet none has succeeded in suppressing it, for it rests upon customs too deeply rooted in the caravan and the port to be rooted out by edict.

The doctrine stands apart from the faiths of the world, including the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame) and the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), neither contradicting their teachings nor depending upon them. Many of the faithful of those creeds also follow the Open Hand, seeing its precepts as a complement to the duties of their religion rather than a rival.

## Significance

Open Hand endures as one of the most socially consequential of the world's teachings, for it supplies the trust upon which the long-distance commerce of the known world is built. From the caravan wells of the Kharesh to the harbours of the strait, the open hand is the quiet engine of exchange between peoples who might otherwise regard one another with suspicion. Its precept of shelter has preserved the lives of countless travellers and the honour of countless hosts. It is a teaching of no temple and no priesthood, carried in the memory and practice of those who keep it, and its strength lies in this diffuseness — a doctrine held by all is harder to destroy than one held by a few. Open Hand is among the chief bonds that hold the diverse peoples of the world in a single, if loose, fellowship of mutual obligation.
