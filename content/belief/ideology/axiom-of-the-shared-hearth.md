## Overview

The Axiom of the Shared Hearth is a teaching of household and community that takes as its emblem the single hearth about which the members of a household gather, and by extension the single hearth about which the households of a community gather in the ordinary passages of life. It is not a religion and claims no temple or priest, but a discipline of domestic conduct taught by the matrons and the elders of the household throughout the settled lands where the household is held to be the foundation of the polity. The central axiom of the teaching is that the hearth once kindled is the hearth of all who sit beside it, and that the master of the house who would deny the warmth of the fire to any of those who gather is held to have denied the meaning of the hearth itself. The teaching has been refined over generations into a comprehensive ethic of household order, mutual dependence, and the proper governance of the shared life.

## Origins in the Settled Communities

The Axiom of the Shared Hearth is generally held to have arisen in the settled farming communities of the river valleys, where the household was the fundamental unit of production and the hearth was the centre about which the labours of the household were ordered. The chroniclers of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) preserve accounts of an older custom by which the harvest was brought to a common hearth and divided among the households of the village, and by which the matrons of the village met at a single hearth to order the affairs of the community. From this practical custom the present axiom is held to descend, refined by the matrons and the elders into the formal teaching now observed throughout the settled lands and in the towns that have grown up from them.

As the trade of the settled lands grew and the towns increased in size and complexity, the axiom was carried into the new communities by the matrons who had been trained in the older villages, and the households of the towns took up the axiom as the foundation of their domestic order. The axiom found a ready welcome in the households of the [Kingdom of Aldreth](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-aldreth), whose settled agrarian communities had long experience of the village hearth, and in the households of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), whose member cities maintained the custom of the shared hearth as a foundation of the urban order. The chronicler notes that the axiom has been of especial service to the larger households, in which the management of the common life requires a discipline that the informal custom of the small village does not supply.

## The Hearth as Foundation

The first article of the axiom is the hearth as foundation, by which the household is held to be the unit of the polity and the hearth the centre of the household. The teaching holds that the polity is an aggregate of households and that the polity which disorders its households is itself disordered, and that the master and the matron of the household are therefore the first officers of the commonweal. The hearth is not merely the place where the pot is hung, for the teaching holds that the hearth is the place where the household gathers in counsel and in rest, and that the order of the household is to be read in the order of the fire. The chronicler notes that this teaching has given to the matrons of the settled communities a recognised authority that the matrons of the more mobile communities do not always possess, and that the axiom is held in particular esteem by the households of the river valleys.

The hearth as foundation is observed in its most characteristic form in the villages of the river valleys, where the household is large enough to require the ordering of many labours and where the hearth is the centre of the common life. The chronicler notes that the villages where the axiom is observed are marked by a tidiness of the household and a steadiness of the domestic routine that the villages where it is neglected do not possess, and that the customary law of the village is itself a development of the axiom. The teaching holds that the orderly hearth is the foundation of the orderly village, and the orderly village is the foundation of the orderly realm.

## The Membership of the Fire

The second article of the axiom is the membership of the fire, by which all who sit beside the hearth are held to be within the protection of the household for so long as they sit there. The teaching holds that the hearth is a place of refuge as much as a place of cooking, and that the one who is admitted to the fire is admitted to the household for the duration of his sitting. The membership is not a formality, for the axiom holds that the master of the house who would deny the protection of the hearth to a guest is held to have denied the meaning of the fire, and that the matron of the house who would set a stranger from the door has forgotten the membership that her own position confers. The chronicler notes that this teaching has done much to soften the boundaries between household and stranger, and that the households where the axiom is observed are held in good repute for their welcome of the traveller.

The membership of the fire is observed in its most elaborate form in the households of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), where the formal courtesies of the hearth are prescribed by the customary law of the cities and where the master and the matron of the household are required to maintain a watchfulness for the stranger at the door. The chronicler notes that the custom of the pact is more elaborate than the custom of the villages, and that the formal courtesies of the hearth have been developed by the pact into a fine discipline that the simpler villages do not possess. The teaching holds that the discipline of the hearth is itself an excellence, and that the household which maintains it is the household which fulfils the axiom.

## The Labours of the Household

The third article of the axiom is the labours of the household, by which the work of the common life is ordered and apportioned among the members of the household. The teaching holds that the household is a polity in miniature, and that the labour of the household is to be ordered by the same justice as the labour of the realm, and that the master of the house who would lay all the heavy work upon one member of the household is held to have broken the axiom. The teaching further holds that the labour of the household is to be apportioned with attention to the capacity of each member, so that the strong bear the heavier part and the weak are not overburdened, and that the master and the matron are required to set their own hands to the work that they require of others. The chronicler notes that this teaching has given to the households where the axiom is observed a fair apportionment of labour that the households where the axiom is neglected do not possess.

The labours of the household are observed in their most characteristic form in the larger households of the towns and the manors, where the variety of the work requires a deliberate apportionment and where the matron is required to maintain a watchfulness for the proper ordering of the common labour. The chronicler notes that the custom of the apportionment is enforced by the regard of the household and by the periodic reckoning of the matron, and that the household which fails of the proper apportionment is held to be in disorder. The teaching holds that the orderly apportionment of the household labour is the foundation of the household's prosperity, and that the household which observes it is the household which endures.

## The Keeping of the Common Store

The fourth article of the axiom is the keeping of the common store, by which the resources of the household are gathered, husbanded, and distributed according to the need of the household. The teaching holds that the store of the household is a common trust and that the master and the matron are required to administer it for the benefit of all the members, and that the master who would appropriate the store to his own pleasure is held to have broken the axiom. The teaching is careful that the store should be husbanded with attention to the season and to the need, so that the household is neither impoverished by the failure to lay up nor starved by the failure to distribute. The chronicler notes that this teaching has produced in the households where the axiom is observed a steadiness of provision that the households where it is neglected do not possess.

The keeping of the common store is observed in its most formal form in the larger households of the manors and the patrician houses of the towns, where the variety of the resources and the length of the reckoning require a precise administration. The chronicler notes that the matrons of the patrician houses of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) are required to render an account of the common store at stated intervals, and that the custom of the reckoning is held to be a foundation of the trust between the household and its officers. The teaching holds that the trustful keeping of the store is itself a discipline, and that the household which maintains it is the household which preserves the axiom.

## The Custom of the Common Meal

Beyond the formal articles of the axiom, the customary practice of the common meal is observed by many of its practitioners, particularly in the households of the villages and the smaller towns. The common meal is the meal at which all the members of the household sit down together, and the teaching holds that the master and the matron who would eat apart from the household have broken the fellowship of the fire. The custom is observed with a particular rigour in the households of the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach), where the common meal is held to be the foundation of the household's daily life, and the chronicler notes that the reach is marked by a custom of the table that the more hurried polities of the north do not always possess. The teaching holds that the common meal is the household expression of the axiom, and that the household which neglects it has lost the meaning of the hearth.

The custom of the common meal is distinct from the doctrines of hospitality that are observed by the other teachings of the world, for the axiom does not bind the host to the formal reception of the stranger; it binds the household to the common sitting of its own members. The chronicler notes that this distinction has given the axiom a particular character that the doctrines of hospitality do not possess, and that the household where the common meal is observed is held to be a household of a particular kind. The teaching holds that the common meal is the seal of the household's fellowship, and that the household which maintains it is the household which best fulfils the axiom.

## Application in the Towns and Manors

The Axiom of the Shared Hearth has been of especial service to the larger households of the towns and the manors, where the management of the common life requires a discipline that the simpler custom of the village does not supply. The chronicler notes that the patrician houses of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) have refined the axiom into a fine art of household management, and that the matrons of the pact are held in repute throughout the lands where the axiom is observed for the precision of their domestic administration. The teaching holds that the discipline of the household is itself an excellence, and that the patrician house which maintains it is the patrician house which preserves the axiom in its fullest form.

The axiom has also shaped the conduct of the town councils of the smaller polities, where the affairs of the town are ordered by the matrons and the elders of the leading households. The chronicler notes that the [Council of Khalisar](/content/government/local/council-of-khalisar) draws upon the axiom in its domestic ordinances, and that the custom of the shared hearth has been of service to the council in the ordering of the town's common life. The teaching holds that the polity is an aggregate of households and that the polity which observes the axiom in its own domestic ordering will be a polity whose households are themselves well ordered. The chronicler notes that this teaching has done much to preserve the steadiness of the smaller polities, where the disorder of the household is quickly felt in the disorder of the town.

## Relation to Crown and Creed

The Axiom of the Shared Hearth is, like the other teachings of the world, indifferent to the boundaries of crown and creed. It asks only that the hearth be shared and the common store be kept, and it binds the followers of the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame), of the [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-pantheon-of-the-plains), and of the smaller local rites alike where they keep the household. The greater polities have sometimes viewed the axiom as a teaching suited only to the settled communities, and the axiom is in fact less generally observed among the mobile peoples of the plains and the desert than among the settled peoples of the river valleys. Yet the axiom has been taken up by the settled communities of the desert oases and the plain towns, and the chronicler notes that the teaching has proved itself capable of crossing the customary frontiers.

The axiom is distinct from the doctrines of worship, though it shares with them the language of the hearth and the household. The axiom does not require of its practitioners any belief in the divine nor any participation in the rites of the temples; it asks only the observance of the household and the fellowship of the fire. The chronicler notes that this distinction has made the axiom acceptable to the practical folk of the villages and the towns, who hold their faith lightly and their household firmly, and that it has been adopted in the settled communities precisely because it makes no demands beyond its own observance.

## Significance

The Axiom of the Shared Hearth endures as one of the most quietly influential of the domestic teachings of the world, a discipline of household order and mutual dependence that has preserved the settled communities through every extremity of season and history. It is a teaching without temple or priesthood, carried in the custom of the household and the village, and its strength lies in the daily observance of the matrons and the elders who maintain it. A household that observes the axiom is a household that endures, and a village whose households observe it is a village that is steady in its domestic order. In the account of the chronicler, the Axiom of the Shared Hearth is among the chief supports of the settled order, and the world is the more orderly for the discipline of those who keep the common fire.
