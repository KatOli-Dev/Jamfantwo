## Overview

Flood-Bringer is the third of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), the divine of the high water that descends from the high country in the spring and that can either fertilise or destroy the fields of the river valleys. Flood-Bringer is the brown water that the snowmelt sends down the tributaries in the late spring, the slow rise of the river that spreads across the floodplain and deposits the silt that renews the soil, the long high water that the river holds through the early summer, and the falling of the water in the late summer that returns the river to its ordinary channel. The river communities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) invoke Flood-Bringer with a combination of gratitude and caution, for the flood is the giver of the fertility of the fields but also the destroyer of the embankments and the villages that lie too near the water. The chronicler of the river-mouth records has set down that the figure is older than the first dyke and the first irrigation channel, and that the [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers have honoured the figure since before the first human settlement was founded upon the riverbank.

The figure of Flood-Bringer is generous and dangerous, and the rites that attend the figure are marked by the same duality that marks the flood itself. The figure is invoked at the first rise of the water in the spring, at the height of the flood when the fields are watered, and at the falling of the water in the late summer when the harvest begins. The river communities do not ask the figure for blessings in the abstract; they ask the figure for a gentle flood that will water the fields without destroying them, for warning of the excessive floods that would break the embankments, and for the wisdom to manage the water that the snowmelt sends down. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of stewardship of the flood, and that the enduring fertility of the river valleys is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Flood-Bringer is conceived as the living presence of the high water that descends from the high country in the spring, and the figure's domain extends from the snowfields of the mountains to the broad floodplains of the lower rivers. She is the brown water that the snowmelt sends down the tributaries, the slow rise of the river that spreads across the floodplain and deposits the silt that renews the soil, the long high water that the river holds through the early summer, and the falling of the water in the late summer that returns the river to its ordinary channel. The chronicler of the river-mouth records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the water and the management of the embankments as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Flood-Bringer is the floodplain itself, the broad belt of low ground that borders the lower rivers and that the annual flood covers with the silt that renews the soil. The figure is invoked at the customary shrines of the river, the marked places at the principal fords and the principal reaches where the river communities have long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrines are not buildings in the formal sense; they are marked places, usually a standing stone or a sacred tree or a natural rise above the ordinary flood level, and the tending of the shrines is a responsibility shared among the households of the river communities. Flood-Bringer is present in the shrines as the spirit of the high water that the shrines acknowledge, and her presence is renewed each year by the first rise of the water in the spring.

The figure is also invoked in the work of the dyke-keepers, who maintain the embankments that the river communities have raised over the centuries to control the distribution of the flood water. The dyke-keepers are the principal labourers of the river communities in the season of the flood, and the chronicler records that the maintenance of the embankments is a communal labour that the families of the river perform together at the customary season. Flood-Bringer is present in the work of the dyke-keepers as the spirit of the high water that the embankments contain, and the dyke-keepers are held to be the human instruments of the figure's care for the floodplain. The chronicler notes that the work of the dyke-keepers is held to be a form of devotion, and the dyke-keepers speak the customary words of acknowledgement as each section of the embankment is renewed.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Flood-Bringer falls at the height of the spring runoff, when the rivers are at their fullest and the flood water is spreading across the fields of the floodplain. The festival is the principal observance of the figure among the river communities, and the rites are conducted at the principal ford of each valley by the elder of the community. The elder stands at the ford and speaks the traditional words, asking Flood-Bringer to carry the flood gently and to water the fields without destroying them. The chronicler records that the spring festival is observed with great solemnity in every river community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the river year. Flood-Bringer's day at the height of the spring runoff is the most universal of the observances of the high water.

The rite of the first rise is conducted at the opening of the flood season, when the elder of the community stands at the principal ford and speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, asking Flood-Bringer to grant a gentle rise and a fair distribution of the water across the fields. The rite of the falling follows at the close of the summer, when the elder speaks the traditional words of thanksgiving for the fertility that the flood has bestowed and for the safe return of the water to its ordinary channel. The chronicler notes that the two rites of the flood - the first rise and the falling - form the pivot of the river year, and the observance of the rites with strict regularity is held to be the foundation of the continuity of the river communities through the seasons.

A distinctive observance of Flood-Bringer is the rite of the safe banks, conducted by the watchkeepers of the river at the height of the flood. The watchkeepers are charged with observing the conditions of the embankments and the weather, and the rite of the safe banks is a form of devotion that is practised by every household of the river communities. The chronicler records that the watchkeepers of the river valleys have developed a refined understanding of the behaviour of the flood, and that their predictions are relied upon by every farmer and every fisher who works upon the floodplain. The rite of the safe banks is held to be a form of communion with Flood-Bringer, and the watchkeepers who have mastered the reading are regarded with a respect that is matched by no other accomplishment of the river life.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Flood-Bringer is the third of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and the figure's position in the order of the greater powers is the testing of the river year. The [River-Mother](/content/belief/deity/river-mother) is the first of the greater powers, the goddess of the moving water in whom the great rivers and their tributaries are embodied. [Estuary-Walker](/content/belief/deity/estuary-walker) is the second, the divine of the brackish water where the river meets the sea. Flood-Bringer is the third, the power of the high water that descends in the spring. The three are acknowledged collectively at the seasonal gatherings of the river-mouth communities, but each also has her own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the river-mouth cycle, and the figure of Flood-Bringer is honoured last because the flood is the last of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the river communities before the summer.

The three greater powers are understood to be a single order of divine care for the moving water, and the figures are invoked together at the customary shrines of the river and the estuary. The River-Mother is the moving water that carries the silt that fertilises the fields, Estuary-Walker is the brackish water where the river meets the sea, and Flood-Bringer is the high water that descends in the spring. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in a fixed sequence at the seasonal gatherings, and the sequence is observed with great strictness in every river-mouth community. The figure of Flood-Bringer is present in the sequence as the spirit of the high water that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is the foundation of the unity of the observance.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the spirits of the individual rivers, the tributaries, and the principal species of fish. The river of a given valley has its own spirit, and the communities of that valley maintain the name and the customary offerings in small books kept by the elder. Flood-Bringer is present in the local gods as the spirit of the high water that the local rivers bring in the spring, and her presence is renewed each year by the first rise of the water at the opening of the flood season. The chronicler records that the local catalogue is held in particular esteem by the river communities, and the local spirits are honoured at the customary shrines alongside the greater powers.

## The River Vael and the Annual Flood

The [River Vael](/content/location/natural/feature/river-vael) is the greatest waterway of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the annual flood of the Vael is the most celebrated of the floods in the known world. The flood of the Vael begins in the late spring, when the snowmelt from the high country swells the upper tributaries, and the rise of the water moves downstream over a period of several weeks. The floodwaters spill over the low banks of the river and spread across the fields of the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale), depositing a layer of fine silt that renews the fertility of the soil. The chronicler records that the flood of the Vael is not regarded by the valley communities as a danger but as a gift, and the customary law of the Amber Vale gives the maintenance of the dykes, the channels, and the bridges precedence over every private claim.

Flood-Bringer is present in the flood of the Vael as the spirit of the high water that the snowmelt sends down the valley, and the figure is honoured in the customary observances of the river communities with a strictness that the chronicler has described as the most attentive of the river year. The chronicler records that the farmers of the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale) have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the management of the flood, and the management is conducted through a system of dykes and channels that control the distribution of the water. The figure is honoured in the management of the flood as the spirit of the high water that the figure provides, and the chronicler notes that the careful husbandry of the floodwater is the foundation of the prosperity of the valley.

The flood is also a hazard when it is excessive, and the chronicles of the valley record several occasions upon which the river has burst its banks and destroyed entire villages. The flood of the third year of the reign of [Queen Sera](/content/people/historical/queen-sera-of-the-amber-vale) was the most severe of recorded history, and the queen is remembered for her personal supervision of the repair of the embankments in the year that followed. The chronicler notes that the memory of this flood is preserved in the customary law of the valley, which requires that each household set aside a portion of the harvest in a common store against the recurrence of such a calamity, and that the store is administered by the village elder under the oversight of the royal governor. Flood-Bringer is honoured in the memory of the great flood as the spirit of the high water whose power the valley has learned to respect.

## The Naga and the Warm Rivers

The [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers maintain the oldest continuous form of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and their observance of Flood-Bringer is the model upon which the human communities of the central rivers have patterned their own practices. The naga are themselves river-dwellers, inhabiting the water rather than the banks, and their understanding of the movements of the flood is regarded by the human communities with a mixture of respect and dependence. The chronicler records that the naga observe Flood-Bringer at the customary seasons of the warm river, and that they maintain the customary cautions that govern the conduct of the fishers in the flood season.

The relationship between the naga and the human river communities is one of mutual dependence, and the warning of the excessive floods is one of the most valuable of the courtesies that the naga extend. The naga know the deep waters and the behaviour of the flood, and the humans control the embankments and the harvesting of the floodplain. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements that govern the conduct of the fishers in the flood season, and the chronicler records that the system has generally worked well, with occasional disputes over the boundaries of the fishing grounds but no sustained conflict. Flood-Bringer is honoured in the cooperation as the spirit of the high water that the two peoples share, and the chronicler notes that the cooperation has done much to maintain the unity of the observance across the wide extent of the warm rivers.

The chronicler notes that the naga observance of Flood-Bringer takes a form that the human communities do not always witness, for the naga gather at the deep pools of the warm rivers during the height of the flood, and the naga singing can sometimes be heard from the banks when the water is high and the wind is in the right quarter. The naga do not build shrines in the manner of the human communities, for the river itself is their temple, and the figure of Flood-Bringer is invoked by the naga in the waters where the flood is most fully felt. The chronicler notes that the naga understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and that the human faith has borrowed many of its cautions from the naga practice of the warm rivers.

## Significance

Flood-Bringer is the third of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the flood-season life of the river communities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the warm southern rivers of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the high water that renews the soil, the formalisation of the management of the embankments through religious obligation, and the integration of the naga knowledge into the everyday practice of the river communities have all contributed to the prosperity of the river valleys of the known world. The chronicler finds in Flood-Bringer one of the most generous and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the dyke-keepers and the watchkeepers who depend upon the flood for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the flood remaining gentle. The figure endures, in the embankment and at the height of the spring runoff, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the high water that descends from the mountains in the spring.
