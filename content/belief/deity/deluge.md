## Overview

Deluge is the third of the greater powers of the [River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/river-mouths), the divine of the high water that descends from the high country in the spring and can either fertilise or destroy the fields of the river valleys. Deluge is the brown water that snowmelt sends down the tributaries in the late spring, the slow rise of the river that spreads across the floodplain and deposits silt that renews the soil, the long high water that the river holds through the early summer, and the falling of the water in the late summer that returns the river to its ordinary channel. The river communities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) invoke Deluge with a combination of gratitude and caution, for the flood is the giver of the fertility of the fields but also the destroyer of embankments and villages that lie too near the water. The figure is older than the first dyke and the first irrigation channel, and the [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers have honoured the figure since before the first human settlement was founded upon the riverbank.

Deluge is generous and dangerous, and the rites that attend the figure are marked by the same duality as the flood itself. The figure is invoked at the first rise of the water in the spring, at the height of the flood when the fields are watered, and at the falling of the water in the late summer when the harvest begins. River communities ask for a gentle flood that will water the fields without destroying them, for warning of excessive floods that would break the embankments, and for wisdom to manage the water. The figure is honoured by every act of stewardship of the flood, and the enduring fertility of the river valleys is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Deluge is conceived as the living presence of the high water that descends from the high country in the spring. Her domain extends from the snowfields of the mountains to the broad floodplains of the lower rivers. She is the brown water that snowmelt sends down the tributaries, the slow rise of the river that spreads across the floodplain, the long high water through the early summer, and the falling of the water in the late summer. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by reading the water and managing the embankments as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Deluge is the floodplain itself, the broad belt of low ground bordering the lower rivers that the annual flood covers with silt. She is invoked at customary shrines of the river, marked places at principal fords and reaches where river communities have long gathered for seasonal rites. The shrines are not buildings but marked places, usually a standing stone, a sacred tree, or a natural rise above the ordinary flood level. The tending of the shrines is a responsibility shared among households of the river communities. Her presence is renewed each year by the first rise of the water in the spring.

Deluge is also invoked in the work of the dyke-keepers, who maintain the embankments raised over centuries to control the distribution of flood water. The dyke-keepers are the principal labourers of the river communities in the season of the flood, and the maintenance of the embankments is a communal labour performed together at the customary season. Dyke-keepers speak the customary words of acknowledgement as each section of the embankment is renewed, and their work is held to be a form of devotion.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Deluge falls at the height of the spring runoff, when the rivers are at their fullest and flood water is spreading across the fields of the floodplain. The rites are conducted at the principal ford of each valley by the elder of the community, who speaks the traditional words asking Deluge to carry the flood gently and to water the fields without destroying them. The spring festival is observed with great solemnity in every river community and is one of the most important ceremonial events of the river year.

The rite of the first rise is conducted at the opening of the flood season, when the elder stands at the principal ford and speaks the customary words, asking for a gentle rise and a fair distribution of water. The rite of the falling follows at the close of summer, when the elder speaks the traditional words of thanksgiving for the fertility the flood has bestowed and for the safe return of the water to its ordinary channel. These two rites form the pivot of the river year.

A distinctive observance is the rite of the safe banks, conducted by the watchkeepers of the river at the height of the flood. Watchkeepers observe the conditions of the embankments and the weather, and this rite is a form of devotion practised by every household. Watchkeepers of the river valleys have developed a refined understanding of the behaviour of the flood, and their predictions are relied upon by every farmer and fisher who works upon the floodplain.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Deluge is the third of the greater powers of the [River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/river-mouths). [Potamia](/content/belief/deity/potamia) is the first, goddess of the moving water in whom the great rivers and their tributaries are embodied. [Estuary Walker](/content/belief/deity/estuary-walker) is the second, divine of the brackish water where the river meets the sea. Deluge is the third, power of the high water that descends in the spring. The three are acknowledged collectively at the seasonal gatherings of the river-mouth communities, but each has her own day of particular observance. The order of the three is fixed by the river-mouth cycle, and Deluge is honoured last because the flood is the last of the gifts the gods bestow upon the river communities before the summer.

Beneath the three greater powers are the spirits of individual rivers, tributaries, and principal species of fish. The river of a given valley has its own spirit, and communities maintain the name and customary offerings in small books kept by the elder. The local catalogue is held in particular esteem, and local spirits are honoured at the customary shrines alongside the greater powers.

## The River Vael and the Annual Flood

The [River Vael](/content/location/natural/feature/river-vael) is the greatest waterway of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and its annual flood is the most celebrated in the known world. The flood begins in the late spring when snowmelt from the high country swells the upper tributaries, and the rise moves downstream over several weeks. The floodwaters spill over the low banks and spread across the fields of the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale), depositing fine silt that renews the fertility of the soil. The flood is not regarded as a danger but as a gift, and the customary law of the Amber Vale gives maintenance of dykes, channels, and bridges precedence over every private claim.

Farmers of the Amber Vale have developed a body of customary knowledge governing the management of the flood through a system of dykes and channels that control the distribution of water. The careful husbandry of the floodwater is the foundation of the prosperity of the valley.

The flood is also a hazard when excessive. The flood of the third year of the reign of [Sera](/content/people/historical/sera-ambervale) was the most severe in recorded history, and the queen is remembered for her personal supervision of the repair of the embankments in the year that followed. The memory of this flood is preserved in the customary law of the valley, which requires each household to set aside a portion of the harvest in a common store against the recurrence of such a calamity. The store is administered by the village elder under the oversight of the royal governor.

## The Naga and the Warm Rivers

The [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers maintain the oldest continuous form of the [River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/river-mouths), and their observance of Deluge is the model upon which human communities of the central rivers have patterned their practices. The naga are river-dwellers, inhabiting the water rather than the banks, and their understanding of the movements of the flood is regarded by human communities with respect and dependence. They observe Deluge at the customary seasons and maintain the customary cautions governing the conduct of fishers in the flood season.

The relationship between naga and human river communities is one of mutual dependence. Warning of excessive floods is one of the most valuable courtesies naga extend. Naga know the deep waters and the behaviour of the flood; humans control the embankments and the harvesting of the floodplain. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements governing the conduct of fishers in the flood season. The naga gather at deep pools during the height of the flood, and their singing can sometimes be heard from the banks. Naga do not build shrines in the manner of human communities, for the river itself is their temple. The naga understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and the human faith has borrowed many cautions from naga practice.

## Significance

Deluge is the third of the greater powers of the [River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/river-mouths), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the flood-season life of the river communities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the warm southern rivers of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The reverence for the high water that renews the soil, the formalisation of the management of the embankments through religious obligation, and the integration of naga knowledge into the everyday practice of the river communities have contributed to the prosperity of the river valleys. Deluge is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the dyke-keepers and watchkeepers who depend upon the flood for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the flood remaining gentle.
