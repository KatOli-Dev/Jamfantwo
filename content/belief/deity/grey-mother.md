## Overview

The Grey Mother is the principal power of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), the goddess in whom the cold western waters and the long swell of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are embodied. The fishers, the whalers, and the cliff communities of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) invoke her at the calm of the fog and at the breaking of the storm, and her presence is understood as the encompassing veil through which the cold sea reveals itself to those who know how to listen. She is conceived as the grey sea itself, the fog that rises from it, and the long swell that moves through the deep water, and the chronicler of the western records has set down that the figure is older than the cliff towns that honour her, and that the merfolk of the cold water have invoked her since before the first human boat was launched from the western headlands.

The figure of the Grey Mother is austere, and her festival is the most austere of the year's observances. She is invoked in silence rather than ceremony, and the rites that are conducted in her honour are marked by a quietness that the chronicler of the coastal shrines has described as the deepest piety he has witnessed. The fishers of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) do not ask her for favours in the manner of the more elaborate faiths; they ask her for clear water, for steady hands, and for the wisdom to read the signs of the sea. The chronicler records that the faith that surrounds her is less a matter of doctrine than of practice, and that her figure is honoured by every act of seamanship that the western communities perform.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Grey Mother is conceived as the encompassing presence of the grey sea, and her domain extends from the surf of the cliffs to the deep water of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) and the colder waters beyond. She is the fog that shrouds the coast and conceals the horizon, the long swell that moves through the deep water and reaches the cliffs in a steady rhythm, and the cold current that brings the fish and the whales to the western shores. The chronicler of the coastal shrines has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the fog and the reading of the swell as much as by any formal rite.

The fog is the veil of the Grey Mother, and the fog-readers of the cliff towns are charged with observing the conditions of the fog and the swell and with relaying their assessments to the boats and the harbours. The chronicler records that the fog-readers are the most respected of the coastal practitioners, and that their work is considered a form of devotion. The fishers who enter the fog without the customary acknowledgement are believed to be at greater risk of becoming lost, both literally and spiritually, and the custom of speaking the words before entering the fog is held to be as important as the skill of reading the conditions. The chronicler has no way of assessing the spiritual dimension of this belief, but the practical fact remains that the fishers who observe the custom have lower rates of reported accidents in fog-bound conditions than those who neglect it.

The long swell is the breath of the Grey Mother, and the swell is held to move the water with a force that the individual fisher cannot measure but can feel in the hull of his boat. The chronicler of the western records has set down that the fishers of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) have developed a refined understanding of the conditions of the western waters through their observation of the swell, and that their predictions are relied upon by every fisher and every whaler who operates in the region. The reading of the swell is held to be a form of communion with the Grey Mother, and the fishers who have mastered the reading are regarded with a respect that is matched by no other accomplishment of the coastal life.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Grey Mother falls at the midwinter, when the storms of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are at their most frequent and the fisherfolk are most dependent upon her protection. The festival is the most austere of the year's observances, and the rites are conducted in silence at the headland shrine of each cliff town. The elder of the community stands at the highest point of the headland and speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, and the words are not prayers in the conventional sense but a formal recognition that the sea has entered its most dangerous season and that the community places itself under the protection of the Grey Mother for the duration of the winter. The chronicler records that the words have changed little over the generations, and that the form of the festival is the same in every cliff town from the northern headlands to the southern estuaries.

The midwinter festival is the occasion of the storm-cantors, the singers who perform the old songs at the height of the winter gales. The songs are not prayers but memorials of the past storms and the fishermen who have been lost to them, and the performance of the songs is held to be a way of keeping the dead present in the life of the community. The chronicler notes that the storm-cantors are usually women, the widows of fishermen who have been lost at sea, and that the songs are sung at the principal headland shrine during the worst storms of the winter, when the wind is so loud that the voices of the cantors can be heard only by those who stand close. The belief is held that the songs carry across the water to the spirits of the lost, and that the spirits then guide the surviving vessels safely back to harbour.

The customary observances of the Grey Mother are not confined to the midwinter festival. The fishers speak the customary words before they put to sea in any season, and the words are a brief acknowledgement of the figure of the Grey Mother and a request for clear water and steady hands. The whalers speak the customary words before the hunt, and the first blubber of the kill is offered at the headland shrine by the elder of the whaling community. The lighthouses and the signal towers are maintained by the watchkeepers of the cliff towns, and the chronicler records that the duty of the watch is considered a religious obligation, and the neglect of the lighthouse by its keeper is considered a betrayal of the Grey Mother and the fishing community alike.

## The Merfolk of the Cold Water

The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters are the principal non-human adherents of the Grey Mother, and their observance is adapted to the life of the deep sea rather than the cliff town. The merfolk communities are dwellers of the cold water itself, and their understanding of the movements of the whales and the currents of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) is deep. The merfolk observe the Grey Mother at the customary seasons, and they maintain the traditional quotas that govern the whaling hunt. The chronicler records that the merfolk have acted as the primary intermediaries between the whaling communities and the whale pods in recent centuries, warning the whalers of approaching pods and advising them on the conditions of the hunt.

The relationship between the merfolk and the human cliff communities is one of practical reciprocity. The humans provide the boats, the nets, and the technology of the hunt, while the merfolk provide the knowledge of the whales and the deep water. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements that govern the fishing grounds and the conduct of the hunt, and the chronicler notes that the system has generally worked well, with occasional friction but no sustained conflict. The Grey Mother has not required formalisation of this relationship, as the daily cooperation between the two peoples has made elaborate treaties unnecessary.

The merfolk observance of the Grey Mother takes a form that the cliff communities do not always witness. The merfolk gather at the offshore rocks during the midwinter festival, and the chronicler records that the merfolk singing can sometimes be heard from the cliffs when the sea is calm and the wind is in the right quarter. The merfolk do not build shrines in the manner of the cliff towns, for the sea itself is their temple, and the figure of the Grey Mother is invoked by the merfolk in the waters where she is most present. The chronicler notes that the merfolk understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and that the human faith has borrowed many of its observances from the merfolk practice of the cold water.

## The Whales and the Hunt

The great whales of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are honoured as the eldest of the sea's creatures, and the [Greyring whale](/content/species/beasts/greyring-whale) in particular is understood to be a creature that the Grey Mother has placed under the protection of the whalers. The figure of the Whale-Walker, the second of the greater powers of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), is the elder of the sea, and the whalers honour the Whale-Walker before the hunt by speaking the customary words and by setting aside the first blubber of the kill as an offering. The chronicler records that the figure of the Whale-Walker is not worshipped by the whalers in the sense of a deity to be placated but is regarded as the elder of the sea, a creature to be honoured and respected before the hunt and a being whose bounty is earned rather than given.

The quotas that govern the whaling hunt are among the strictest in the known world, and the chronicler records that the whaling communities that have adhered to the quotas have maintained stable whale populations over generations, while those that have expanded their catches during years of abundance have often found the populations depleted in subsequent seasons. The faith teaches that the sea is a common inheritance of the present and the future generations, and that the whaler who would take the last breeding stock has denied his grandchildren the bounty of the sea. The chronicler observes that this principle of restraint, while framed in religious terms, represents one of the earliest known formal management systems for marine megafauna in the known world.

The Grey Mother is understood to be the source of the whales' abundance, and the figure is honoured in the hunt as the giver of the catch. The first blubber is offered at the headland shrine by the elder of the whaling community, and the offering is a formal acknowledgement that the whale is not the property of the hunter but the gift of the Grey Mother, lent for the keeping of the community. The chronicler notes that the practice has done much to give the whaling communities a sense of stewardship over the fishery, and that the whales' continued presence in the waters of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) is the clearest evidence of the practice's success.

## The Coast and the Communities

The Grey Mother is honoured by the cliff communities, the whalers, the fishers, and the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold water, and the chronicler of the western records has set down that the figure unites the disparate communities of the western coast in a single observance. The cliff towns maintain their own headland shrines, and the shrines are not connected by any central authority, but the rites that are conducted at them are recognised by every community as the same observance. The chronicler notes that the absence of hierarchy has prevented the kind of religious disputes that have afflicted more elaborate faiths, and that the unity of the observance is the foundation of the quiet strength of the western communities.

The watch is the central duty of the cliff communities, and the watchkeepers of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) are responsible for the maintenance of the lighthouses, the signal towers, and the storm warnings that protect the fishing fleet and the merchant traffic that passes along the coast. The watchkeepers are drawn from the fisherfolk, and their role is not separate from their daily labour but an extension of it. The chronicler records that the watchkeepers of the western coast maintain a level of vigilance that is exceptional, and that the lighthouses and signal towers of the western coast are among the most densely maintained in the known world. The duty of the watch is considered a religious obligation, and the neglect of the lighthouse by its keeper is considered a betrayal of the Grey Mother and the fishing community alike.

The Grey Mother is understood to be present in every part of the western sea, from the surf of the cliffs to the deepest trenches of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), and the chronicler of the western records has set down that the figure is honoured by every act of seamanship that the western communities perform. The reading of the fog, the reading of the swell, the maintenance of the lighthouses, the conduct of the hunt, and the singing of the storm-cantors are all understood to be observances of the Grey Mother, and the figure is present in the daily life of the coast in a manner that no other figure of the western faiths quite matches.

## Significance

The Grey Mother is the principal power of the cold western sea, and her observance is the foundation of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the whales, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) knowledge into the everyday practice of the whalers have all contributed to the prosperity of the western coast communities. The chronicler finds in the Grey Mother one of the most austere and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the fishers and the whalers who depend upon the grey sea for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the sea remaining healthy. The figure endures, in the fog and in the long swell, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the cold waters of the western deep.
