## Overview

Fury is the third of the greater powers in the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way), the divine of the long gales that sweep in from the open [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) and break against the cliff towns of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) of western [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Fury is the heavy weather that builds in the west, the dark line of cloud upon the horizon, the long roll of thunder across the water, and the bright sheets of rain that fall upon the cliffs before the gale. Fishers, whalers, and cliff communities invoke Fury with reverence and caution, for the storms are necessary to the fertility of the sea but dangerous to boats and nets. The figure is older than the first cliff town and the first whaling camp, and the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters have honoured the figure since before the first human boat was launched from the western headlands.

Fury is austere and unpredictable, and the rites attending the figure are marked by the same watchfulness that marks the fisher's reading of the weather. The figure is invoked at the first sign of the building of the storm, at the height of the gale when boats are safe in harbour, and at the passing of the weather when boats put to sea again. Cliff communities ask for warning of the approaching storm, for the strength to ride out the gale in harbour, and for wisdom to read the signs of the weather. Fury is honoured by every act of seamanship the western communities perform, and her blessing upon the sea is held to be the source of the fertility that the fishers draw from the cold water.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Fury is conceived as the living presence of the long gales that move across the cold water of the west. Her domain extends from the first line of building cloud on the western horizon to the last squall that breaks against the cliffs after the gale has passed. She is the darkening of the sky in the west, the rising of the long swell before the wind arrives, the hammering of rain upon cliffs and boats, and the long clearing that follows the passing of the weather. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by reading the weather and managing the boats as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Fury is the open water of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), the cold expanse across which the gales build before they reach the coast. She is invoked at customary headland shrines of the cliff towns, marked places at the highest points of the headlands. The shrines are not formal buildings but marked spaces upon natural rock, tended by the households of the cliff communities. Her presence is renewed each year by the first gale of autumn and the last gale of spring.

Fury is also invoked in the watch upon the headlands, maintained by watchkeepers through the storm season. Watchkeepers observe the conditions of the weather and the sea, a form of devotion practised by every household of the cliff towns. They have developed a refined understanding of the gales of the Greyring, and their predictions are relied upon by every fisher and whaler in the region. Watchkeepers who have served through the worst of the gales are honoured as the human instruments of the figure's care for the cold water.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Fury falls at the height of the storm season in late autumn, when the gales are most frequent and fisherfolk are most dependent upon the figure's protection. The rites are conducted at the principal headland shrine of each cliff town by the elder, who speaks the customary words asking Fury to grant warning of approaching storms and to keep the boats safe in harbour. The festival is observed with great solemnity in every cliff town from the northern headlands to the southern estuaries.

The rite of the first gale is conducted at the first named gale of autumn, when the elder asks Fury to grant that the boats be safe in harbour and nets stored against the coming weather. The rite of the clearing follows the last named gale of spring, with words of thanksgiving for the passing of the storm season and the opening of calmer waters. These two rites form the pivot of the western year.

A distinctive observance is the rite of the warning, conducted by watchkeepers at the first sign of a building storm. Watchkeepers light warning fires upon the headlands, signalling boats to make for the nearest harbour. The lighting is accompanied by a brief invocation of Fury, a formal acknowledgement that the storm is approaching and the community places itself under the figure's protection.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Fury is the third of the greater powers of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way), her position being the testing of the western year. [Cana](/content/belief/deity/cana) is the first, goddess of the cold western waters, the fog, and the long swell. [Cetus](/content/belief/deity/cetus) is the second, the elder of the sea and the spirit of the great whales. Fury is the third, the power of the long gales. The three are acknowledged collectively at the headland shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each has her own day of particular observance. The order is fixed by the western year, and Fury is honoured last because the storms are the last of the three gifts the gods bestow upon the cliff communities before the spring.

Beneath the three greater powers are the spirits of the particular rocks and headlands of the western coast. Each cliff town maintains the names of the spirits of nearby headlands and rocks in small books kept by the elder, updated only when a spirit has been known across several generations.

## The Storm and the Fertility of the Sea

Fury is honoured not only in the danger of the gales but also in the recognition that the storms are necessary to the fertility of the cold water. The gales of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) bring upwelling of cold nutrient-laden water from the deep, the foundation of the abundance of fish and whales. The figure is invoked at the first gale of autumn with a word of welcome as well as caution, the formal acknowledgement that the storm is the giver of the fertility of the sea. Fishers who observe this dual observance have maintained richer fisheries over generations than those who regard the storms as a threat alone.

Fury is also honoured in the long calm following the passing of the gale, when the sea is at its most bountiful and boats can put to sea in safety. Fishers set out upon the clearing with a customary word of thanks to Fury, acknowledging that the figure's passing has released the bounty of the sea.

This dual understanding of the storm is one of the most distinctive features of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way). Fishers who fear the storm alone stay in harbour and miss the catch. Fishers who welcome the storm alone put to sea in weather that destroys their boats. Fishers who honour Fury in both aspects read the weather with the attention the sea requires.

## The Merfolk and the Deep Water

The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters are the principal non-human adherents of Fury, and their observance is adapted to the life of the deep sea. Merfolk communities are dwellers of the cold water itself, with deep understanding of the gales of the Greyring. They observe Fury at the customary seasons and maintain the traditional cautions governing the conduct of boats in the storm season. In recent centuries, merfolk have acted as primary intermediaries between cliff communities and the weather of the deep water, warning fishers of approaching gales.

The relationship between merfolk and human cliff communities is one of practical reciprocity. Humans provide boats, nets, and technology of the fishery; merfolk provide knowledge of the weather and deep water. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements governing fishing grounds and the conduct of boats in the storm season.

Merfolk observance of Fury takes a form the cliff communities do not always witness. Merfolk gather at offshore rocks during the height of the gales, and their singing can sometimes be heard from the cliffs. Merfolk do not build shrines in the manner of cliff towns, for the sea itself is their temple. The merfolk understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and the human faith has borrowed many cautions from merfolk practice.

## Significance

Fury is the third of the greater powers of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the storm-season life of the cliff communities of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) and the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold water. The reverence for the gales that bring the upwelling of the deep water, the formalisation of the warning of the storms through religious obligation, and the integration of merfolk knowledge into the everyday practice of the fishers have contributed to the prosperity of the western coast. Fury is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the fishers and watchkeepers who depend upon the cold water for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the gales remaining true to their season.
