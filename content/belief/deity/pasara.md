## The Spirit of the Grazing Beasts

The Pasara is the second of the greater powers in the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), the spirit of the grazing beasts and of the fertility of the herds that the pastoral peoples of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) of central [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) depend upon for their livelihood. She is conceived as the spirit that quickens the unborn calf in the body of the cow, that brings the lamb into the world in the early spring, that sustains the mare through the gestation, and that keeps the herd healthy through the lean months of the winter. The pastoralists of the southern grasslands and the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) of the open country invoke her at the birthing of the calves and the lambs, at the founding of a new herd, and at the seasonal movement of the herds between the summer pastures and the winter shelter. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the figure is older than the first herd and the first camp, and that the pastoral peoples of the grasslands have honoured her since before the first boundary stone was set upon the open country.

The figure of the Pasara is patient and abundant, and her observance is among the most enduring of the pastoral year. The pastoral communities do not ask her for blessings in the abstract; they ask her for healthy calves, for a fair yield of milk, for the preservation of the herd through the winter, and for the wisdom to manage the grazing lands without diminishing them. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of pastoral husbandry that the grassland communities perform, and that the enduring fertility of the herds is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Pasara is conceived as the living presence of the herd animals in their passage across the open country, and her domain extends from the rich pastures of the eastern grasslands to the drier uplands of the western margins. She is the cow that drops the calf in the early spring, the ewe that brings forth the lamb in the shelter of the rocks, the mare that foals upon the open grass, and the herd that moves across the country in a single great body under the guidance of the older animals. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the management of the grazing and the care of the breeding animals as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Pasara is the standing stone, the ancient marker that the pastoralists have set upon the open country to mark the boundaries of the grazing lands and the places where the seasonal rites are conducted. The standing stones are not built in the manner of the temples of the lowland faiths, for they are natural stones that the pastoralists have marked with the customary symbols, and the stones are maintained by the elders of the kindreds in accordance with the customary law that the founding of the pastoral order established. The Pasara is present in the standing stones as the spirit of the herd that the stones commemorate, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the pastoral year.

## The Greater Spirits of the Plains

The Pasara is the second of the greater spirits of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and her position in the pantheon is the honouring of the grazing beasts and the fertility of the herds. The [Sky Walker](/content/belief/deity/sky-walker) is the first, the spirit of the open heavens and the weather, who sends the rains that green the grass and the winds that sweep the long grass flat. The [Stone Watcher](/content/belief/deity/stone-watcher) is the third, the spirit of the standing stones and the buried dead, who keeps the boundaries of the land and the memory of the ancestors. To these three are reckoned a fourth in some traditions, the [River Speaker](/content/belief/deity/river-speaker), spirit of the waters that cut the plains, honoured by those kindreds whose grounds lie along the great rivers that thread the grasslands from north to south.

Beneath the greater spirits are counted a multitude of lesser powers, many of them local to a single hill, spring, or stand of trees. The pastoralists hold that to know the name of the spirit of a place is to hold a claim upon its favour, and the elders of a kindred are charged with the remembering of these names and the teaching of them to the young. The Pasara is honoured in the catalogue of the lesser powers as the spirit whose name the catalogue preserves, and the figure is present in the maintenance of the lists as the spirit of the herd that the lists commemorate.

## The Rites of the Pastoral Year

The rites of the pastoral year are bound up with the management of the grazing lands, for the spirits are held to be pleased by the careful tending of the earth and angered by its waste. The seasonal movement of the herds is accompanied by offerings at the standing stones that mark the old boundaries, and the first greening of the grass after the cold months is greeted with a song of thanksgiving to the Sky Walker and the Pasara alike. Births, marriages, and the passage of the dead are each marked by rites addressed to the appropriate spirit, and the elders who lead these rites are likewise the keepers of the kindred's knowledge of the land and its waters.

A distinctive teaching of the pantheon is the duty of restraint in the use of the plains, founded upon the belief that the spirits lend the grass and the beasts to the people only for their keeping. The pastoralists hold that to overgraze a ground is to offend the Pasara and to invite the thinning of the herds, and the management of grazing by rotation, observed among the centaur kindreds and the human pastoralists alike, is defended not merely as good husbandry but as a religious obligation. In this the plains faith is unusual among the beliefs of the world, for it joins devotion directly to the stewardship of the land.

## The Centaur Kindreds and the Herd

The [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) are the dominant sapient inhabitants of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains), and their presence has shaped the ecology of the region for centuries beyond reckoning. The centaur kindreds move across the plains in a seasonal cycle that mirrors the movements of the herds, following the grass as it greens and fades and moving their encampments according to patterns that have been established over countless generations. The centaurs do not build permanent settlements upon the plains, for their way of life demands mobility, and their dwellings are tents of hide and woven grass that can be dismantled and packed within the hour, leaving no trace upon the land beyond the flattened circle of the camp.

The relationship between the centaurs and the grasslands is one of active stewardship, for the centaurs manage the grazing of the wild herds through their own movements and through the controlled burning of the grass in the dry season, a practice that rejuvenates the sward and prevents the encroachment of woody plants. The Pasara is honoured in the management of the herds as the spirit whose presence the management acknowledges, and the figure is present in the burning of the grass as the spirit of the renewal that the burning accomplishes.

## The Plains Cattle and the Principal Herds

The plains cattle, a large humped animal that roams the grasslands in herds of hundreds or thousands, are the most conspicuous of the grazing animals of the plains, and the herds of the plains cattle are the principal expression of the Pasara's domain. The cattle move in a constant cycle of grazing and migration, following the growth of the grass as it advances northward through the spring and retreats southward through the autumn, and their hooves and their dung are essential elements of the grassland ecosystem. The Pasara is honoured in the cattle as the spirit of the herd that the cattle embody, and the figure is present in the migration as the spirit of the movement that the cattle perform.

The swift antelope, the shaggy-maned horse, and the smaller creatures of the plains are also within the domain of the Pasara, for the figure is the spirit of all the grazing animals of the open country. The centaur kindreds manage the herds of the wild horses as well as the cattle, and the management of the horses is a particular art that the centaurs have refined over many generations. The Pasara is honoured in the management of the horses as the spirit of the herd that the horses embody, and the figure is present in the breeding and the training of the horses as the spirit of the fertility that the horses represent.

## The Boar of the Open Country

The [plains thunder boar](/content/species/beasts/plains-thunder-boar) is the largest of the wild swine of the grasslands, a great and shaggy beast of formidable temper whose range extends across the plains from the foothills of the western mountains to the river valleys of the interior. The boar is the quarry of the great mounted hunt of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), and the hunt of the thunder boar is a ceremonial pursuit that is conducted according to traditions that are among the most ancient of the plains peoples. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the boar is not the creature of the Pasara but a being of the wild country that the Pasara's domain does not encompass, for the boar is a solitary and a fierce creature that lives apart from the herds.

The hunt of the thunder boar is honoured by the centaurs and the human pastoralists alike as a test of skill and a ritual of the open country. The hunters ride out from the encampment in the early morning and spread across the grasslands in search of a herd of boars, working the herd to a single boar of mature size and great tusks, and the chosen boar is then driven from the herd by the beaters and pursued across the open country. The Pasara is not invoked in the hunt, for the hunt is of the wild country and not of the managed herd, but the figure is honoured in the managed herds that the hunt protects from the rooting of the boar.

## The Calving Season and the First Milk

The calving season is the principal observance of the Pasara, and the season falls in the early spring when the cattle and the horses of the plains bring forth their young. The pastoralists gather at the customary standing stone of the kindred, and the elder of the kindred leads the rite of the first calving. The first milk of the season is offered at the stone, and the offering is a formal acknowledgement that the Pasara has granted the fertility of the herd for the year to come. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the rite of the first calving is observed with great solemnity in every kindred of the grasslands, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the pastoral year.

The first milk is not consumed by the kindred but is reserved for the calves and the foals that the season has brought forth. The pastoralists hold that the first milk carries the blessing of the Pasara, and the blessing is passed to the young animals through the milk that they receive from the herd. The chronicler notes that the custom of the first milk is one of the most distinctive of the pastoral observances, and the custom is observed in every kindred of the grasslands without exception.

## The Movement of the Herds

The seasonal movement of the herds is the central practical labour of the pastoral year, and the movement is governed by a body of customary law that the elders of the kindreds administer. The pastoralists move their herds northward in the spring to follow the growth of the grass, and they move them southward in the autumn to escape the cold of the winter. The movements are conducted in a prescribed order that the customary law has refined over many generations, and the order ensures that the herds do not crowd one another upon the limited grazing lands.

The Pasara is honoured in the movement as the spirit of the herd that the movement sustains, and the figure is present in the customary law as the spirit of the fertility that the law protects. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the customary law of the movement is one of the most elaborate of the customary laws of the pastoral peoples, and the law has been the foundation of the careful management of the grazing lands for several generations.

## The Mortality of the Herd

The mortality of the herd is a constant concern of the pastoral year, and the deaths of the animals are marked by rites that acknowledge the figure of the Pasara. The pastoralists hold that the deaths are not the will of the figure but the natural consequence of the conditions that the herds endure, and the deaths are recorded in the small books that the elders of the kindreds maintain. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the recording of the deaths is one of the most careful of the pastoral observances, and the records are the foundation of the management of the herd through the seasons.

The mortality of the herd is greatest in the winter, when the cold and the shortage of grass combine to thin the weakest animals. The pastoralists maintain the winter shelters at the customary grounds of the kindred, and the shelters are stocked with the hay and the fodder that the herd requires through the cold months. The Pasara is honoured in the shelters as the spirit of the herd that the shelters protect, and the figure is present in the maintenance of the shelters as the spirit of the fertility that the maintenance preserves.

## The Wandering Singers and the Memory

The wandering singers carry the songs of the pantheon between the kindreds, and it is through their travelling that the names of the spirits are kept consistent across the broad expanses of the grasslands. The singers are the living memory of the faith, and a kindred that has lost its singer is held to be in danger of forgetting the spirits of its own ground. The chronicler of the plains records has set down that the wandering singers are held in particular respect by the pastoral communities, and the singers are received with hospitality at every encampment that they visit.

The Pasara is honoured in the songs of the wandering singers as the spirit whose name the songs preserve, and the figure is present in the travelling of the singers as the spirit of the herd that the songs commemorate. The chronicler notes that the songs of the Pasara are among the most enduring of the plains songs, and the songs are passed from singer to singer with a care that the chronicler has described as the foundation of the unity of the pastoral observance across the breadth of the grasslands.

## Significance

The Pasara is the second of the greater powers of the [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), and her observance is the foundation of the pastoral life of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) of central [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the herd animals, the formalisation of the management of the grazing lands through religious obligation, and the integration of the centaur kindreds' knowledge into the everyday practice of the pastoral year have all contributed to the enduring fertility of the grasslands. The chronicler finds in the Pasara one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the pastoralists who depend upon the herd for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the herd remaining healthy. The figure endures, in the calving season and at the standing stone, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the grazing beasts of the open country.
