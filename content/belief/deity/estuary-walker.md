## Overview

Estuary-Walker is the second of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), the divine of the brackish water where the great rivers of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) meet the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea). Estuary-Walker is the slow mixing of the fresh and the salt in the wide mouth of the river, the turning of the tide that pushes the salt water upstream and draws it back again twice in the day, the silt that settles in the shallows where the two waters mingle, and the fish that move between the river and the sea according to the season of their spawning. The fisherfolk who operate in the shallows of the estuaries invoke the figure at the turning of the tide, and the chronicler of the river-mouth records has set down that the figure is older than the first weir and the first fish-trap, and that the [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers have honoured the figure since before the first human settlement was founded upon the estuary.

The figure of Estuary-Walker is patient and observant, and the rites that attend the figure are marked by the same steady quality that marks the turning of the tide. The figure is invoked at the flood tide when the salt water pushes upstream, at the ebb tide when the fresh water flows back to the sea, at the setting of the nets in the shallows, and at the drawing of the nets at the turning of the season. The fisherfolk of the estuaries do not ask the figure for blessings in the abstract; they ask the figure for a fair tide, for an abundance of fish in the shallows, and for the wisdom to read the patterns of the brackish water. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of stewardship of the estuaries, and that the enduring productivity of the estuary fisheries is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Estuary-Walker is conceived as the living presence of the brackish water, and the figure's domain extends from the upper reach of the tide to the mouth of the river where the fresh water meets the open sea. She is the slow mixing of the fresh and the salt in the wide mouth of the river, the long push of the salt water upstream at the flood, the long draw of the fresh water downstream at the ebb, the silt that settles in the shallows where the two waters mingle, and the fish that move between the river and the sea according to the season. The chronicler of the river-mouth records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the tide and the setting of the nets as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Estuary-Walker is the estuary itself, the broad mouth of the river where the fresh and the salt mingle. The figure is invoked at the customary shrines of the estuary, the marked places at the principal shore and the principal sandbanks where the river communities and the sea communities have long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrines are not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for the shrines are understood as natural dwellings of the divine, and the rites are conducted at the shore and in the shallow water of the estuary with the customary observances. Estuary-Walker is present in the shrines as the spirit of the brackish water that the shrines acknowledge, and her presence is renewed each year by the first turning of the tide at the opening of the fishing season.

The figure is also invoked in the work of the net-setters, who set the nets in the shallows of the estuary at the turning of the tide. The net-setters are the principal labourers of the estuary communities, and the chronicler records that the setting of the nets is a communal labour that the families of the estuary perform together at the customary season. Estuary-Walker is present in the work of the net-setters as the spirit of the brackish water that the work serves, and the net-setters are held to be the human instruments of the figure's care for the estuary. The chronicler notes that the work of the net-setters is held to be a form of devotion, and the net-setters speak the customary words of acknowledgement as each net is set in the shallows.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Estuary-Walker falls at the height of the fishing season, in the late summer when the fish are at their most abundant in the shallows and the tides are at their most regular. The festival is the principal observance of the figure among the estuary communities, and the rites are conducted at the principal shore of the estuary by the elder of the community. The elder stands at the shore and speaks the traditional words, asking Estuary-Walker to grant a fair tide and an abundance of fish in the shallows. The chronicler records that the late-summer festival is observed with great solemnity in every estuary community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the river-mouth year. Estuary-Walker's day at the height of the fishing season is the most universal of the observances of the brackish water.

The rite of the first turning is conducted at the opening of the fishing season, when the elder of the community stands at the principal shore and speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, asking Estuary-Walker to grant that the fish enter the estuary in their season and that the nets be set in the customary places. The rite of the last turning follows at the close of the autumn, when the elder speaks the traditional words of thanksgiving for the catch of the season and the closing of the fishing grounds. The chronicler notes that the two rites of the turning - the first and the last - form the pivot of the estuary year, and the observance of the rites with strict regularity is held to be the foundation of the continuity of the estuary communities through the seasons.

A distinctive observance of Estuary-Walker is the rite of the closed estuary, observed during the spawning season of the principal fish species when the fishing in the shallows is suspended to protect the breeding stock. The closure is enforced by the customary law of the estuary communities as a religious obligation, and the chronicler records that the estuary communities that have adhered to the closure have maintained richer fish stocks over generations than those that have abandoned it. Estuary-Walker is present in the closure as the spirit of the brackish water whose care the closure serves, and the neglect of the closure is regarded as a breach of duty to both the figure and the community. The chronicler notes that the closure is not absolute, for the elders may grant permits for the taking of fish from certain species during the closure period if the community faces hunger, but the granting of such permits is rare.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Estuary-Walker is the second of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and the figure's position in the order of the greater powers is the meeting of the river and the sea. The [River-Mother](/content/belief/deity/river-mother) is the first of the greater powers, the goddess of the moving water in whom the great rivers and their tributaries are embodied. The Flood-Bringer is the third, the power of the high water that descends in the spring. Estuary-Walker is the second, the divine of the brackish water where the river meets the sea. The three are acknowledged collectively at the seasonal gatherings of the river-mouth communities, but each also has her own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the river-mouth cycle, and the figure of Estuary-Walker is honoured second because the estuary is the second of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the river communities after the river itself.

The three greater powers are understood to be a single order of divine care for the moving water, and the figures are invoked together at the customary shrines of the river and the estuary. The River-Mother is the moving water that carries the silt that fertilises the fields, Estuary-Walker is the brackish water where the river meets the sea, and the Flood-Bringer is the high water that descends in the spring. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in a fixed sequence at the seasonal gatherings, and the sequence is observed with great strictness in every river-mouth community. The figure of Estuary-Walker is present in the sequence as the spirit of the brackish water that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is the foundation of the unity of the observance.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the spirits of the individual rivers, the tributaries, and the principal species of fish. The river of a given valley has its own spirit, and the communities of that valley maintain the name and the customary offerings in small books kept by the elder. The spirits of the fish are localised to the species that are most important for the diet of the community, and the chronicles note that this localisation is both a strength and a source of occasional conflict between neighbouring communities that share an estuary but differ in the dominant species. Estuary-Walker is present in the local gods as the spirit of the brackish water that the local estuaries inhabit, and her presence is renewed each year by the first turning of the tide at the opening of the fishing season.

## The Meeting of the River and the Sea

The estuary is the principal temple of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and the estuaries of the great rivers are the gathering points of the river communities at the turning of the season. The principal estuaries of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the coastal regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) serve as the centres of the joint observance between the river peoples and the seafaring communities, and the estuary is understood as the dwelling of Estuary-Walker. The chronicler notes that the estuary is not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for it is the natural dwelling of the divine, and the rites are conducted upon the shore and in the shallow water of the estuary with the customary observances.

The estuary is also the meeting-place of the river peoples and the seafaring peoples, and the exchange that takes place there is the foundation of the wider trade of the region. The fisherfolk of the river meet the fisherfolk of the sea at the estuary, and the meeting is the occasion of the exchange of the harvest of the river for the harvest of the sea. The chronicler observes that the joint management of the estuary fishing grounds is one of the most practical applications of the faith, for it requires the river communities and the sea communities to share knowledge about the habits of the fish, the patterns of the tides, and the seasonal movements of the species that inhabit both the fresh and the salt water. Estuary-Walker is honoured in the cooperation of the two communities, and the prosperity of the estuary fisheries is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

The chronicler records that the estuaries of the great rivers are among the most productive of the fishing grounds in the known world, and the productivity is held to be the gift of Estuary-Walker. The mixing of the fresh and the salt water brings the nutrients that the fish require, and the figure is honoured at the principal shore of the estuary with a customary word of welcome for the returning fish and a customary word of thanks for the catch. The figure is present in the estuaries as the spirit of the brackish water that the communities share, and the chronicler notes that the estuaries have been the foundation of the quiet cooperation between the river and the sea communities for as many generations as the chronicles record.

## The Auric and the Stormfall

The estuaries of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) are the principal dwelling of Estuary-Walker, and the figure is honoured in the great river mouths that open upon these waters. The chronicler records that the principal estuaries of the [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) coast are the gathering points of the river communities at the turning of the season, and the estuaries that open upon the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) are the busiest of the river-mouths, for they serve the traffic that passes between the two oceans. Estuary-Walker is present in the great river mouths as the spirit of the brackish water that the figure inhabits, and the figure is honoured in the customary observances of the estuary communities with a strictness that the chronicler has described as the most attentive of the river-mouth year.

The figure is also honoured in the small estuaries of the tributary rivers, the broad shallow mouths where the smaller streams enter the sea. The chronicler records that the small estuaries are the principal fishing grounds of the coastal villages, and the figure is invoked at the customary shrines of the small estuaries with the same observance that the great river mouths receive. The figure is present in the small estuaries as the spirit of the brackish water that the small streams provide, and the chronicler notes that the small estuaries have been the foundation of the prosperity of the coastal villages for as many generations as the chronicles record. Estuary-Walker is honoured in the small estuaries as the spirit of the brackish water that the figure provides, and the figure is present in the small estuaries in a manner that no other figure of the river-mouth year quite matches.

The chronicler notes that the estuaries of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) are subject to the rhythms of the tides that the figure governs, and the tides are read by the watchkeepers of the estuary communities as a form of devotion. The tides vary with the season and with the phase of the moon, and the watchkeepers have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the setting of the nets and the drawing of the catch. Estuary-Walker is honoured in the reading of the tides as the spirit of the brackish water that the watchkeepers observe, and the figure is present in the tides in a manner that no other figure of the river-mouth year quite matches. The chronicler observes that the reading of the tides is among the most attentive of the devotions of the estuary communities, and the prosperity of the estuary fisheries is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## Significance

Estuary-Walker is the second of the greater powers of the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the estuary life of the river-mouth communities of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the coastal regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the fish that move between the river and the sea, the formalisation of the closure of the estuary through religious obligation, and the integration of the river and sea knowledge into the everyday practice of the estuary communities have all contributed to the prosperity of the river-mouth regions of the known world. The chronicler finds in Estuary-Walker one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the net-setters and the watchkeepers who depend upon the estuary for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the brackish water remaining patient. The figure endures, in the tide and at the setting of the nets, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the meeting of the river and the sea.
