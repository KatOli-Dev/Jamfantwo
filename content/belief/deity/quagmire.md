## Overview

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), the divine of the wetland that borders the still water of the interior lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Quagmire is the reed, the mud, the still back-channel where the water moves so slowly that the surface seems to sleep, the croak of the frogs in the dusk, and the slow migration of the waterfowl that arrive at the lake in the season of their going. The lake-shore communities and the dwellers of the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) invoke the figure at the season of the migration, when the waterfowl settle in the marsh and the hunters set out upon the still water, and the chronicler of the lake records has set down that the figure is older than the first net and the first reed-cutters' camp, and that the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes have honoured the figure since before the first human settlement was founded upon the shore.

The figure of Quagmire is patient and watchful, and the rites that attend the figure are marked by the same stillness that marks the marsh itself. The figure is invoked at the standing of the reeds in the spring, at the cutting of the reeds in the late summer, at the catching of the waterfowl in the season of their coming, and at the long quiet evenings when the marsh is given over to the frogs and the herons. The lake communities do not ask the figure for blessings in the abstract; they ask the figure for the preservation of the reed beds, for the return of the waterfowl, and for the wisdom to manage the marsh that sustains them. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of marsh husbandry, and that the enduring fertility of the wetlands is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Quagmire is conceived as the living presence of the wetland, and the figure's domain extends from the saturated margins of the lake to the deeper reed beds that border the back-channels and the slow-moving streams. She is the dense stand of the common reed that grows in the brackish water, the shallow pond in which the larvae of the marsh creatures develop, the mud in which the waterfowl find their food, and the long evening in which the heron stands motionless above the shallows. The chronicler of the lake records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the observation of the marsh and the management of the reed beds as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Quagmire is the wetland itself, the broad belt of shallow water and saturated ground that lies between the lake and the higher ground of the surrounding country. The figure is invoked at the customary shrines of the marsh, the marked places at the edge of the reed beds where the lake communities have long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrines are not buildings in the formal sense; they are marked places, usually a stake of weathered wood or a cairn of stones raised at the margin of the standing water, and the tending of the shrines is a responsibility shared among the households of the marsh communities. Quagmire is present in the shrines as the spirit of the wetland that the shrines acknowledge, and her presence is renewed each year by the first cutting of the reeds and the first catch of the waterfowl.

The figure is also invoked in the songs of the reed-cutters, who work the marsh in the late summer and who preserve the names of the channels and the pools in the verses they sing to keep the rhythm of the cutting. The reed-cutters are the principal labourers of the marsh communities, and the chronicler records that the cutting of the reeds is a communal labour that the families of the marsh perform together at the customary season. Quagmire is present in the songs of the reed-cutters as the spirit of the marsh that the songs acknowledge, and the singers are held to be the human instruments of the figure's care for the wetland. The chronicler notes that the songs are passed from parent to child in a manner similar to the transmission of the faith itself, and the songs preserve the names of the spirits of the channels and the pools.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Quagmire falls at the season of the migration, when the waterfowl arrive at the lake and the marsh in large numbers and the hunters set out upon the still water. The festival is the principal observance of the figure among the lake communities, and the rites are conducted at the customary shrine of the marsh by the elder of the community. The elder speaks the customary words of invocation, asking Quagmire to grant the return of the waterfowl and to bless the hunters in their pursuit. The chronicler records that the festival of the migration is observed with great solemnity in every lake community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the marsh year. Quagmire's day at the season of the migration is the most universal of the observances of the wetland.

The rite of the first cutting is conducted at the opening of the reed-cutting season, when the reed-cutters of the community take their hooks and their cutting knives to the first stand of the mature reeds. The elder of the community speaks the traditional words, asking Quagmire to grant a fair cutting and to preserve the roots of the reeds for the next year's growth. The rite of the first catch is a distinctive observance, performed at the first catching of the waterfowl of the season. The first bird taken is offered at the customary shrine by the elder, and the offering is a formal acknowledgement that the waterfowl are not the property of the hunter but the gift of Quagmire, lent for the keeping of the community. The chronicler records that the first bird of the catch is honoured by the community in a brief ceremony, and the ceremony is the formal opening of the hunting season.

The rite of the closed marsh is observed during the nesting season, when the cutting of the reeds and the hunting of the waterfowl are suspended to protect the breeding stock of the species that depend upon the marsh. The closure is enforced by the customary law of the lake communities as a religious obligation, and the chronicler records that the lake communities that have adhered to the closure have maintained richer stocks of waterfowl and a more abundant growth of reeds over generations than those that have abandoned it. Quagmire is present in the closure as the spirit of the marsh whose care the closure serves, and the neglect of the closure is regarded as a breach of duty to both the figure and the community.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), and the figure's position in the order of the greater powers is the sustaining of the lake cycle. The [Limna](/content/belief/deity/limna) is the first of the greater powers, governing the still water, the reflection, and the depth, and the [Ice Breaker](/content/belief/deity/ice-breaker) is the third, governing the ice that forms upon the lake in the cold months and the breaking of which in the spring releases the lake for the fishing season. Quagmire is the second, governing the wetland that borders the lake and the creatures that inhabit the reed and the mud. The three are acknowledged collectively at the seasonal gatherings of the lake communities, but each also has her own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the lake cycle, and the figure of Quagmire is honoured second because the marsh is the second of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the lake communities.

The three greater powers are understood to be a single order of divine care for the still water, and the figures are invoked together at the customary shrines of the lake and the marsh. The Limna is the still water that mirrors the sky, Quagmire is the wetland that borders the lake and the creatures that inhabit the reed, and the Ice Breaker is the ice that forms upon the lake in the winter and the breaking of which releases the lake for the fishing season. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in a fixed sequence at the seasonal gatherings, and the sequence is observed with great strictness in every lake community. The figure of Quagmire is present in the sequence as the spirit of the wetland that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is the foundation of the unity of the observance.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the gods of the particular lakes, islands, inlets, and species of fish and waterfowl. The community of each lake maintains the names of its local powers in small books kept by the elder, and the names are updated only when a power has been known across several generations. The chronicler records that the catalogue of local gods is long, with distinct entries for the marshes of the eastern valley, the marshes of the northern basin, and the smaller ponds and back-channels that feed into the larger waters. Quagmire is present in the local gods as the spirit of the wetland that the local marshes inhabit, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the lake year.

## The Anurans and the Marsh

The [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes are the principal non-human adherents of Quagmire, and their observance is adapted to the wetland environment that borders the lake. The anuran communities are dwellers of the marsh and the still water, and the marsh is held to be the inheritance of the anuran communities. The chronicler records that the anuran communities honour Quagmire at the customary seasons, and the anuran communities are required to offer the first fish of the catch at the customary shrine of the community. The marsh is not the exclusive domain of any single people; rather, it is understood as a shared space in which each people occupies its own niche, and the faith teaches that the marsh, like the lake, is a trust that is shared among the peoples who depend upon it.

The anuran observance of Quagmire takes a distinctive form, for the anuran elders conduct the customary rites at the edge of the still water in the dusk, when the marsh is given over to the chorus of the frogs and the movement of the herons. The chronicler records that the anuran understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and that the human observance has borrowed many of its customs from the anuran practice of the marsh. The anuran communities have a particular knowledge of the channels and the pools that the human reed-cutters do not share, and the anurans have been known to warn the humans of the approach of storms and the changes in the water level that precede them. The exchange is the foundation of a quiet reciprocity that has sustained the marsh communities through difficult seasons.

The relationship between the anurans and the human lake communities is generally peaceful, and the chronicler records that the cooperation of the two peoples is one of the most successful examples of cross-species governance in the known world. The anurans and the humans maintain the customary shrines of the marsh in common, and the maintenance of the shrines is considered the responsibility of all the marsh peoples, regardless of their species or their place of origin. The figure of Quagmire is present in the cooperation as the spirit of the wetland that the two peoples share, and the chronicler notes that the cooperation has done much to maintain the unity of the observance across the wide extent of the interior waters.

## The Greywash and the Reed Marshes

The [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) are among the most celebrated of the wetlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the marsh communities of the Greywash have maintained an observance of Quagmire that the chronicler has described as the most refined of the inland wetlands. The Greywash descends from the [Vintner's Vale](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/vintners-vale) to the lower reaches of the river, and the marshes cover several days of travel from east to west along the lower course. The chronicler records that the marshes are bounded on the west by the estuary that opens to the sea at [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara), and that the brackish water of the estuary mingles with the fresh water of the river to create the conditions in which the reed grows most densely.

The reed-cutters of the Greywash have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the cutting of the reeds, the catching of the waterfowl, and the maintenance of the channels that the river has cut through the marsh. The chronicler records that the regulations are administered by the magistrates of Nessara in consultation with the elders of the villages, and the regulations set the seasons of the cutting, the methods that the cutters must use, and the price that the cutters may charge. Quagmire is present in the regulations as the spirit of the marsh whose care the regulations serve, and the chronicler notes that the regulations have been the foundation of the careful management of the reed beds and the fish stocks. The [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) is the principal formal agreement that governs the use of the marsh, and the treaty is renewed at the seasonal gatherings of the marsh communities at the customary shrines.

The figure is also honoured in the cutting of the reeds, which the marsh communities perform at the customary season in the late summer, after the nesting season of the birds that breed in the marsh has ended. The chronicler records that the cutting is a communal labour that the families of the marsh perform together, and the bundles of cut reeds are taken by boat to the towns of the coast and the inland valleys, where the reeds are used for thatching, for bedding, and for the small baskets that the fishermen and the farmers require. Quagmire is present in the cutting as the spirit of the marsh whose bounty the cutting gathers, and the reed-cutters are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of the figure's care for the wetland. The figure is present in the bundles of reeds and in the boats that carry them, and the chronicler notes that the cutting is among the most distinctive of the seasonal labours of the inland year.

## Significance

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the wetland life of the interior lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the waterfowl, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) and the other marsh peoples into the everyday practice of the lake year have all contributed to the prosperity of the inland wetlands of the known world. The chronicler finds in Quagmire one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the reed-cutters and the fishers who depend upon the marsh for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the marsh remaining healthy. The figure endures, in the reed bed and at the cutting of the season, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the wetland that borders the still water.
