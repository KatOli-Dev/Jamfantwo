## Overview

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), the divine of the wetland that borders the still water of the interior lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Quagmire is the reed, the mud, the still back-channel where the water moves so slowly the surface seems to sleep, the croak of the frogs in the dusk, and the slow migration of the waterfowl that arrive at the lake in the season of their going. The lake-shore communities and the dwellers of the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) invoke the figure at the season of the migration, when the waterfowl settle in the marsh and the hunters set out upon the still water. The figure is older than the first net and the first reed-cutters' camp, and the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes have honoured the figure since before the first human settlement was founded upon the shore.

Quagmire is patient and watchful, and the rites attending the figure are marked by the same stillness as the marsh itself. The figure is invoked at the standing of the reeds in spring, at the cutting of the reeds in late summer, at the catching of the waterfowl in the season of their coming, and at the long quiet evenings when the marsh is given over to the frogs and the herons. Lake communities ask for the preservation of the reed beds, for the return of the waterfowl, and for wisdom to manage the marsh that sustains them. The figure is honoured by every act of marsh husbandry, and the enduring fertility of the wetlands is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Quagmire is conceived as the living presence of the wetland. Her domain extends from the saturated margins of the lake to the deeper reed beds bordering the back-channels and slow-moving streams. She is the dense stand of common reed growing in the brackish water, the shallow pond in which the larvae of the marsh creatures develop, the mud in which the waterfowl find their food, and the long evening in which the heron stands motionless above the shallows. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by observation of the marsh and management of the reed beds as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Quagmire is the wetland itself, the broad belt of shallow water and saturated ground between the lake and the higher ground. She is invoked at customary shrines of the marsh, marked places at the edge of the reed beds. The shrines are not formal buildings but marked places, usually a stake of weathered wood or a cairn of stones raised at the margin of the standing water. Their tending is a responsibility shared among the households of the marsh communities. Her presence is renewed each year by the first cutting of the reeds and the first catch of the waterfowl.

Quagmire is also invoked in the songs of the reed-cutters, who work the marsh in late summer and preserve the names of the channels and pools in verses sung to keep the rhythm of the cutting. The songs are passed from parent to child, preserving the names of the spirits of the channels and pools.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Quagmire falls at the season of the migration, when the waterfowl arrive at the lake and marsh in large numbers and the hunters set out upon the still water. The rites are conducted at the customary shrine of the marsh by the elder, who speaks the customary words asking Quagmire to grant the return of the waterfowl and to bless the hunters. The festival is observed with great solemnity in every lake community.

The rite of the first cutting is conducted at the opening of the reed-cutting season, when the reed-cutters take their hooks and cutting knives to the first stand of mature reeds. The elder asks Quagmire to grant a fair cutting and preserve the roots for next year's growth. The rite of the first catch is performed at the first catching of waterfowl of the season. The first bird taken is offered at the customary shrine, a formal acknowledgement that the waterfowl are not the property of the hunter but the gift of Quagmire.

The rite of the closed marsh is observed during the nesting season, when the cutting of reeds and hunting of waterfowl are suspended to protect breeding stock. The closure is enforced by customary law as a religious obligation. Lake communities that have adhered to the closure have maintained richer stocks of waterfowl and more abundant reed growth over generations.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), her position being the sustaining of the lake cycle. [Limna](/content/belief/deity/limna) is the first, governing the still water, the reflection, and the depth. [Ice Breaker](/content/belief/deity/ice-breaker) is the third, governing the ice that forms and breaks in the spring. Quagmire is the second, governing the wetland that borders the lake and the creatures inhabiting the reed and mud. The three are acknowledged collectively at seasonal gatherings, but each has her own day of particular observance. The order is fixed by the lake cycle, and Quagmire is honoured second because the marsh is the second gift the gods bestow upon the lake communities.

Beneath the three greater powers are the gods of particular lakes, islands, inlets, and species of fish and waterfowl. Each lake community maintains the names of its local powers in small books kept by the elder.

## The Anurans and the Marsh

The [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes are the principal non-human adherents of Quagmire, their observance adapted to the wetland environment bordering the lake. Anuran communities dwell in the marsh and still water, and the marsh is held to be their inheritance. They honour Quagmire at the customary seasons and offer the first fish of the catch at the customary shrine. The marsh is not the exclusive domain of any single people but a shared space in which each people occupies its own niche.

The anuran observance takes a distinctive form. Anuran elders conduct the customary rites at the edge of the still water in the dusk, when the marsh is given over to the chorus of frogs and the movement of herons. The anuran understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and human observance has borrowed many customs from anuran practice. Anuran communities have particular knowledge of the channels and pools that human reed-cutters do not share, and they have warned humans of approaching storms and changes in water level.

The relationship between anurans and human lake communities is generally peaceful, regarded as one of the most successful examples of cross-species governance in the known world. Anurans and humans maintain the customary shrines of the marsh in common.

## The Greywash and the Reed Marshes

The [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) are among the most celebrated of the wetlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the marsh communities of the Greywash have maintained an observance of Quagmire described as the most refined of the inland wetlands. The Greywash descends from the [Vintner's Vale](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/vintners-vale) to the lower reaches of the river, covering several days of travel from east to west.

The reed-cutters of the Greywash have developed a body of customary knowledge governing the cutting of reeds, catching of waterfowl, and maintenance of channels. The regulations are administered by the magistrates of [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara) in consultation with the elders of the villages. The [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) is the principal formal agreement governing use of the marsh, renewed at seasonal gatherings at the customary shrines.

The figure is also honoured in the cutting of the reeds, performed at the customary season in late summer after the nesting season has ended. The cutting is a communal labour, and the bundles of cut reeds are taken by boat to towns of the coast and inland valleys for thatching, bedding, and baskets.

## Significance

Quagmire is the second of the greater powers of the [Lake Watchers' Path](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/lake-watchers-path), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the wetland life of the interior lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash). The reverence for the breeding cycles of the waterfowl, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) and other marsh peoples into the everyday practice of the lake year have contributed to the prosperity of the inland wetlands. Quagmire is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the reed-cutters and fishers who depend upon the marsh for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the marsh remaining healthy.
