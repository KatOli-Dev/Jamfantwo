## Overview

Cana is the principal power of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way), the goddess in whom the cold western waters and the long swell of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are embodied. Fishers, whalers, and cliff communities of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) invoke her at the calm of the fog and at the breaking of the storm. She is conceived as the grey sea itself, the fog that rises from it, and the long swell that moves through the deep water. The figure is older than the cliff towns that honour her, and the merfolk of the cold water have invoked her since before the first human boat was launched from the western headlands.

Cana is austere, and her festival is the most austere of the year's observances. She is invoked in silence rather than ceremony. Fishers do not ask her for favours in the manner of more elaborate faiths; they ask for clear water, steady hands, and wisdom to read the signs of the sea. The faith that surrounds her is less a matter of doctrine than of practice, and she is honoured by every act of seamanship the western communities perform.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Cana is conceived as the encompassing presence of the grey sea. Her domain extends from the surf of the cliffs to the deep water of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) and the colder waters beyond. She is the fog that shrouds the coast, the long swell that moves through the deep water, and the cold current that brings fish and whales to the western shores. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by reading the fog and the swell as much as by formal rite.

The fog is the veil of Cana. Fog-readers of the cliff towns observe the conditions of the fog and swell and relay assessments to the boats and harbours. They are the most respected of the coastal practitioners, and their work is considered a form of devotion. Fishers who enter the fog without the customary acknowledgement are believed to be at greater risk of becoming lost, both literally and spiritually. Fishers who observe the custom have lower rates of reported accidents in fog-bound conditions.

The long swell is the breath of Cana, moving the water with a force that the individual fisher cannot measure but can feel in the hull of the boat. Fishers have developed a refined understanding of the western waters through observation of the swell, and their predictions are relied upon by every fisher and whaler in the region. Reading the swell is held to be a form of communion with Cana, and those who have mastered it are regarded with unmatched respect along the coast.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Cana falls at midwinter, when storms of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are most frequent and fisherfolk are most dependent upon her protection. The rites are conducted in silence at the headland shrine of each cliff town. The elder stands at the highest point of the headland and speaks the customary words, a formal recognition that the sea has entered its most dangerous season and the community places itself under Cana's protection for the winter. The words have changed little over generations, and the form of the festival is the same from the northern headlands to the southern estuaries.

The midwinter festival is the occasion of the storm-cantors, who perform the old songs at the height of the winter gales. The songs are memorials of past storms and fishermen lost to them, and their performance is held to keep the dead present in the life of the community. The storm-cantors are usually women, widows of fishermen lost at sea, and they sing at the principal headland shrine during the worst storms, when the wind drowns their voices except to those standing close. The songs are believed to carry across the water to the spirits of the lost, who then guide surviving vessels safely back to harbour.

Fishers speak the customary words before putting to sea in any season, a brief acknowledgement of Cana and a request for clear water and steady hands. Whalers speak the customary words before the hunt, and the first blubber of the kill is offered at the headland shrine. Lighthouses and signal towers are maintained by watchkeepers, and the neglect of a lighthouse is considered a betrayal of Cana and the fishing community alike.

## The Merfolk of the Cold Water

The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters are the principal non-human adherents of Cana, and their observance is adapted to the life of the deep sea rather than the cliff town. Merfolk communities are dwellers of the cold water itself, with deep understanding of the movements of whales and currents of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean). They observe Cana at the customary seasons and maintain the traditional quotas governing the whaling hunt. In recent centuries, merfolk have acted as primary intermediaries between whaling communities and whale pods, warning whalers of approaching pods and advising on hunt conditions.

The relationship between merfolk and human cliff communities is one of practical reciprocity. Humans provide boats, nets, and technology of the hunt, while merfolk provide knowledge of whales and deep water. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements governing fishing grounds and the conduct of the hunt, with occasional friction but no sustained conflict.

Merfolk observance of Cana takes a form the cliff communities do not always witness. Merfolk gather at offshore rocks during the midwinter festival, and their singing can sometimes be heard from the cliffs when the sea is calm and the wind is in the right quarter. Merfolk do not build shrines in the manner of cliff towns, for the sea itself is their temple. The merfolk understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and human faith has borrowed many observances from merfolk practice.

## The Whales and the Hunt

The great whales of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) are honoured as the eldest of the sea's creatures. The [Greyring whale](/content/species/beasts/greyring-whale) is understood to be a creature placed under the protection of the whalers by Cana. The figure of [Cetus](/content/belief/deity/cetus), the second of the greater powers of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way), is the elder of the sea. Whalers honour Cetus before the hunt by speaking the customary words and setting aside the first blubber as an offering. Cetus is not worshipped as a deity to be placated but regarded as the elder of the sea, honoured and respected before the hunt, a being whose bounty is earned rather than given.

The quotas governing the whaling hunt are among the strictest in the known world. Whaling communities that have adhered to the quotas have maintained stable whale populations over generations, while those that expanded catches during years of abundance often found populations depleted in subsequent seasons. The faith teaches that the sea is a common inheritance of present and future generations, and that taking the last breeding stock denies grandchildren the bounty of the sea. This principle of restraint represents one of the earliest known formal management systems for marine megafauna.

Cana is understood to be the source of the whales' abundance. The first blubber is offered at the headland shrine, a formal acknowledgement that the whale is not the property of the hunter but the gift of Cana, lent for the keeping of the community. The practice has given the whaling communities a sense of stewardship over the fishery, and the whales' continued presence in the waters of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) is the clearest evidence of its success.

## The Coast and the Communities

Cana is honoured by cliff communities, whalers, fishers, and [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold water. The figure unites the disparate communities of the western coast in a single observance. Cliff towns maintain their own headland shrines, not connected by any central authority, but the rites conducted at them are recognised by every community as the same observance. The absence of hierarchy has prevented the kind of religious disputes that have afflicted more elaborate faiths.

The watch is the central duty of the cliff communities. Watchkeepers of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) maintain lighthouses, signal towers, and storm warnings that protect the fishing fleet and merchant traffic. Watchkeepers are drawn from the fisherfolk, and their role is an extension of their daily labour. They maintain a level of vigilance that is exceptional, and the lighthouses and signal towers of the western coast are among the most densely maintained in the known world.

Cana is present in every part of the western sea, from the surf of the cliffs to the deepest trenches of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean). Reading the fog, reading the swell, maintaining lighthouses, conducting the hunt, and singing the storm-cantors are all understood as observances of Cana, and she is present in the daily life of the coast in a manner no other figure of the western faiths quite matches.

## Significance

Cana is the principal power of the cold western sea, and her observance is the foundation of the [Grey Sea Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/grey-sea-way). The reverence for the breeding cycles of the whales, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of merfolk knowledge into the everyday practice of whalers have contributed to the prosperity of the western coast communities. Cana is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the fishers and whalers who depend upon the grey sea for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the sea remaining healthy.
