## Overview

The Sun Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the god of the sunlight essential to every crop and the warmth of the growing season. He is the light that quickens the seed, the warmth that ripens the grain, the long day of the summer solstice, and the golden hour of the harvest afternoon. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke him at the first ploughing, at the sowing, and at the harvest, and his particular day of observance is the summer solstice, when the day is longest and the sun's favour is most evident. The figure is older than the first field and the first harvest, and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the lowland communities.

The Sun Walker is the elder of the Wheel, the power invoked first at the customary shrine before the other powers. The order of invocation is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the Sun Walker is honoured first because the sunlight is the first gift the gods bestow upon the cultivated land. Farming communities ask him for a bright harvest, for the ripening of the grain, and for the long days of summer the crops require. He is honoured by every act of cultivation, and the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of his favour.

## The Figure and His Domain

The Sun Walker is conceived as the living presence of the sunlight in its passage across the cultivated land. His domain extends from the kitchen garden to the broad grainlands of the river valleys and the rolling grasslands of the central lowlands. He is the first light of morning striking the eastern fields, the high sun of summer noon ripening the grain, the long afternoon maturing the fruit, and the low gold of the harvest evening closing the day. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by observation of the light and timing of labour as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Sun Walker is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community gathers for seasonal rites. The shrine is not a formal building but a marked place, usually a standing stone, an ancient tree, or a spring. The Sun Walker is invoked first at the shrine, preceding the invocations of the [Rain Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) and the [Earth Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother). The order is fixed by the agricultural cycle.

The [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) to the east of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) is celebrated in the lore of the Sun Walker, for the golden sunsets visible along the western shores of the ocean are regarded as the figure's daily farewell to the continent. Dwellers of the eastern Aethelaran coast speak of the setting sun casting a shield of gold across the water from [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) to the southern horizon, tracing the earliest use of the name to pilots who first charted the great circle that the present-day [Auric Passage](/content/location/route/trade/auric-passage) follows.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Sun Walker falls at the summer solstice, when the day is longest and the sun's favour is most evident. The midsummer festival is the most widely observed of the agricultural year. The elder speaks the customary words asking the Sun Walker to grant the warmth the crops require and to lengthen the day until the harvest is ready. The festival is observed with great solemnity in every farming community.

The rite of the first ploughing marks the opening of the soil in spring. The first furrow is turned as the elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Earth Mother to receive the seed and the Sun Walker to ripen it. The rite of the sowing follows shortly after, when the seed is cast into the furrow. The green tide of late spring is marked by the rite of renewal, a quiet observance attended by women and children.

The [First-Grain Feast](/content/culture/first-grain-feast) is the late-summer observance celebrated in central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) upon the day the first sheaves are brought in from the fields. The feast is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone. The first sheaf is cut by the elder, and the cutting signals the commencement of the general harvest.

## The Order of the Wheel

The Sun Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), his position being the opening of the agricultural cycle. The Rain Bringer is the second, governing the water that nourishes the seed. The Earth Mother is the third, governing the soil and the harvest closing the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each has her or his own day of particular observance. The order is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the Sun Walker is honoured first because the sunlight is the first gift the gods bestow.

Beneath the three greater powers are the gods of individual crops, soil types, and weather patterns. The catalogue is long and localised. Elders maintain lists in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations.

The Sun Walker is the sunlight that ripens the crop. The Rain Bringer and the Earth Mother nourish the seed and receive it. The three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, understood as a single order of divine care for the cultivated land.

## The Long Day and the Sun's Favour

The long day of the summer solstice is the most visible sign of the Sun Walker's favour. The day is the longest of the year in every farming region of the temperate and warm lowlands, and farmers mark it with a festival described as the most universal of the agricultural year. The observation is not confined to the village shrine; the elder of each household marks the moment of the solstice with a brief prayer and act of thanksgiving.

The Sun Walker is also honoured in the daily observation of the light. Farmers have developed customary knowledge governing the timing of labour according to the position of the sun. Morning is the time of first ploughing, when the dew has dried and the soil is ready. Midday is the time of rest. Afternoon is the time of harvest, when the sun is low and the grain ready to be cut.

The long evening of the harvest season is a particular occasion of honour. Farmers mark the end of the day's labour with a customary prayer at the edge of the field, a brief acknowledgement of the figure's gift of daylight, observed with great regularity in every farming community.

## The Drought and the Sun's Withdrawal

The Sun Walker is honoured not only in the gift of sunlight but also in the recognition of his withdrawal. The dry season and drought are understood as expressions of the figure's will. Farmers of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) mark the season with a quiet observance described as among the most austere of the agricultural year, recognising that the figure has turned away. The community places itself under the protection of the [Rain Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) until the rains return.

The Sun Walker's withdrawal is also honoured in the observation of autumn, when the day grows shorter and the light fades from the fields. The harvest festival marks the closing of the agricultural cycle. The autumn is the season of the figure's rest, and the community acknowledges the rest by setting aside the tools of the harvest and giving thanks for the gift of the long summer.

## Significance

The Sun Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The reverence for the long day of the summer solstice, the formalisation of daily labour according to the position of the sun, and the integration of household observance into the everyday practice of the farming year have contributed to the prosperity of the lowland communities. The Sun Walker is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the sunlight for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the sun's favour returning each year.
