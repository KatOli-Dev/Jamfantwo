## Overview

The Sun-Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the god of the sunlight that is essential to every crop and the warmth of the growing season. He is the light that quickens the seed, the warmth that ripens the grain, the long day of the summer solstice, and the golden hour of the harvest afternoon. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke him at the first ploughing, at the sowing, and at the harvest, and his particular day of observance is the summer solstice, when the day is longest and the sun's favour is most evident. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the figure is older than the first field and the first harvest, and that his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the lowland communities.

The figure of the Sun-Walker is the elder of the Wheel, the power that is invoked first at the customary shrine before the other powers. The chronicler of the rural customs has recorded that the order of invocation is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the Sun-Walker is honoured first because the sunlight is the first of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the cultivated land. The farming communities do not ask him for blessings in the abstract; they ask him for a bright harvest, for the ripening of the grain, and for the long days of the summer that the crops require. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of cultivation, and that the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and His Domain

The Sun-Walker is conceived as the living presence of the sunlight in its passage across the cultivated land, and his domain extends from the kitchen garden of the village house to the broad grainlands of the river valleys and the rolling grasslands of the central lowlands. He is the first light of the morning that strikes the eastern fields, the high sun of the summer noon that ripens the grain, the long afternoon of the late summer that matures the fruit, and the low gold of the harvest evening that closes the day. The chronicler of the field records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the observation of the light and the timing of the labour as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Sun-Walker is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community has long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrine is not a building in the formal sense; it is a marked place, usually a standing stone or an ancient tree or a spring, and the tending of the shrine is a responsibility shared among the households of the village. The Sun-Walker is invoked first at the shrine, and his invocation precedes the invocations of the [Rain-Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) and the [Earth-Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother). The chronicler records that the order is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the figure of the Sun-Walker is honoured first because the sunlight is the first of the gifts that the gods bestow.

The [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) to the east of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) is celebrated in the lore of the Sun-Walker, for the golden sunsets that are visible along the western shores of the ocean are regarded as the figure's daily farewell to the continent. The dwellers of the eastern Aethelaran coast speak of the setting sun casting a shield of gold across the water from the harbour of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) to the southern horizon, and they trace the earliest use of the name to the pilots who first charted the great circle that the present-day Auric Passage follows. The chronicler of the coastal records has set down that the golden hour of the Auric sunset is the most visible sign of the Sun-Walker's presence in the daily life of the eastern communities, and the figure is honoured in the observation of the sunset as much as in any formal rite.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Sun-Walker falls at the summer solstice, when the day is longest and the sun's favour is most evident. The midsummer festival is the most widely observed of the agricultural year, and the rites are conducted at the village shrine by the elder of the community. The elder speaks the customary words of invocation, asking the Sun-Walker to grant the warmth that the crops require and to lengthen the day until the harvest is ready. The chronicler records that the midsummer festival is observed with great solemnity in every farming community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the agricultural year. The Sun-Walker's day at midsummer is the longest and most widely observed festival of the agricultural year.

The rite of the first ploughing marks the opening of the soil in spring, and it is conducted by the village elder at the principal field of the community. The first furrow is turned as the elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Earth-Mother to receive the seed and the Sun-Walker to ripen it. The rite of the sowing follows shortly after, when the seed is cast into the furrow, and the community gathers to offer the blessing of the harvest before the first green shoot appears. The green tide of late spring, when the first crops push above the soil, is marked by the rite of renewal, a quiet observance attended by the women and the children of the village and focused upon the blessing of the growing crop.

The [Feast of the First Grain](/content/culture/feast-of-the-first-grain) is the late-summer observance that is celebrated by the agricultural communities of the central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) upon the day that the first sheaves of the new harvest are brought in from the fields. The feast is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone, and the Sun-Walker is honoured in the feast as the power that has ripened the grain. The first sheaf is cut by the elder of the community in a ceremony that the chronicler has recorded at length, and the cutting of the sheaf is the signal for the commencement of the general harvest that follows in the days and weeks after the feast. The Sun-Walker is honoured in the cutting as the giver of the ripening, and the elder's words at the cutting are the formal acknowledgement of his gift.

## The Order of the Wheel

The Sun-Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his position in the Wheel is the opening of the agricultural cycle. The Rain-Bringer is the second, governing the water that nourishes the seed, and the Earth-Mother is the third, governing the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each also has her or his own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the figure of the Sun-Walker is honoured first because the sunlight is the first of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the cultivated land.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the gods of the individual crops, the soil types, and the weather patterns that govern each particular region. The god of wheat, the goddess of the barley, the spirit of the lentil, the guardian of the dry soil, the warden of the wet - the catalogue is long and localised. The elders of the more particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or a soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The chronicler notes that the practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that to name a power without having lived with its effects is to invite neither favour nor misfortune but only the confusion of the young.

The Sun-Walker is honoured in the relation of the three greater powers, for the figure is the sunlight that ripens the crop, and the Rain-Bringer and the Earth-Mother are the powers that nourish the seed and the soil that receives it. The chronicler records that the three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, and the three are understood to be a single order of divine care for the cultivated land. The Sun-Walker is the elder of the order, and his presence in the sunlight is the first of the gifts upon which the work of the Rain-Bringer and the Earth-Mother is built.

## The Long Day and the Sun's Favour

The long day of the summer solstice is the most visible sign of the Sun-Walker's favour, and the midsummer festival is the principal occasion upon which the figure is honoured. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the day is the longest of the year in every farming region of the temperate and warm lowlands, and the farmers of every community mark the day with a festival that the chronicler has described as the most universal of the agricultural year. The festival is not confined to the village shrine, for the observation of the solstice is a household observance as well, and the elder of each household marks the moment of the solstice with a brief prayer and a customary act of thanksgiving.

The Sun-Walker is also honoured in the daily observation of the light, and the farmers of every community have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the timing of the labour according to the position of the sun. The morning is the time of the first ploughing, when the dew has dried and the soil is ready to receive the share. The midday is the time of the rest, when the sun is high and the labour of the fields is suspended. The afternoon is the time of the harvest, when the sun is low and the grain is ready to be cut. The chronicler notes that the observation of the light is a form of devotion that is practised by every farmer, and the Sun-Walker is honoured in the timing of the labour as much as in any formal rite.

The long evening of the harvest season is a particular occasion of the Sun-Walker's honour, and the farmers of the lowland communities mark the end of the day's labour with a customary prayer at the edge of the field. The prayer is a brief acknowledgement of the figure's gift of the day's light, and the words have changed little over the generations. The chronicler records that the evening prayer is observed with great regularity in every farming community, and the figure is present in the daily labour of the fields in a manner that no other figure of the agricultural year quite matches. The golden hour of the harvest evening is the most visible sign of the Sun-Walker's presence, and the figure is honoured in the observation of the light as the spirit of the ripening that the day's labour has brought.

## The Drought and the Sun's Withdrawal

The Sun-Walker is honoured not only in the gift of the sunlight but also in the recognition of his withdrawal, for the dry season and the drought are understood to be expressions of the figure's will. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the dry season is the time when the figure's gift is withheld, and the farmers of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) mark the season with a quiet observance that the chronicler has described as among the most austere of the agricultural year. The observance is a recognition that the figure has turned away, and the community places itself under the protection of the [Rain-Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) until the rains return.

The drought is the most serious expression of the Sun-Walker's withdrawal, and the chronicler has recorded that the droughts of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) are the most severe in the known world. The farming communities of the dry regions mark the drought with a fast and a prayer, and the elder of the community speaks the customary words of supplication, asking the Sun-Walker to return and to grant the rain that the crops require. The chronicler notes that the prayers for rain at the height of the dry season are the most fervent of the agricultural calendar, and the village elder's request for gentle rain is the customary opening of the planting ritual.

The Sun-Walker's withdrawal is also honoured in the observation of the autumn, when the day grows shorter and the light fades from the fields. The harvest festival marks the closing of the agricultural cycle, and the Sun-Walker is honoured in the festival as the figure whose light has ripened the grain. The chronicler records that the autumn is the season of the figure's rest, and the community acknowledges the rest by setting aside the customary tools of the harvest and by giving thanks for the figure's gift of the long summer. The Sun-Walker is present in the autumn as the spirit of the light that has withdrawn, and his withdrawal is the foundation of the winter rest that the soil requires.

## Significance

The Sun-Walker is the first of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of his adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the long day of the summer solstice, the formalisation of the daily labour according to the position of the sun, and the integration of the household observance into the everyday practice of the farming year have all contributed to the prosperity of the lowland communities. The chronicler finds in the Sun-Walker one of the most generous and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the sunlight for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the sun's favour returning each year. The figure endures, in the field and at the shrine, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the cultivated light.
