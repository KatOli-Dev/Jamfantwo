## Overview

Long Night is the third of the greater powers in the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country), the divine of the polar darkness that descends upon the cold interior of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) for the weeks of deep winter when the sun does not rise above the horizon. Long Night is the patience of the dark, the long wait that the year imposes upon the people of the interior, and the figure is honoured at the heart of the winter rather than at its edges. The chronicler of the mountain records has set down that the figure is older than the first watchtower and the first snow-bound valley, and that the dwarven high clans and the human communities of the snow valleys have honoured the figure since before the first formal gathering of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) at the principal hall of the high country.

The figure of Long Night is austere, and the rites that attend her are austere in proportion. The figure is invoked in the long evenings of the high country, in the deep watches of the night, and in the brief hours of midday twilight that the winter allows, and the invocations are spoken quietly rather than proclaimed. The communities of the cold interior do not ask the figure for favours in the manner of the more elaborate faiths; they ask the figure for endurance, for the patience to bear the weeks of the polar dark, and for the wisdom to husband the stores of the household through to the return of the light. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of watchkeeping and by every long evening passed in the company of the household, and that the prosperity of the snow-bound communities is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Long Night is conceived as the living presence of the polar darkness, and her domain extends from the brief midday twilight of the deep winter to the long evenings of the autumn and the spring, when the sun sets early and the cold interior is given over to the dark. She is the slow accumulation of the night across the weeks of the high winter, the stillness of the unbroken dark, the cold that settles in the deep of the night, and the long waiting that the year imposes upon the peoples of the interior. The chronicler of the mountain records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the observation of the dark and the husbanding of the stores as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Long Night is the high country itself, the snow-bound valleys, the glaciers, and the high meadows that lie above the tree line of the cold interior. The figure is invoked at the long-night gathering of the clans, which is held each year at the principal hall of the high country at the midwinter, when the sun is at its lowest and the days are at their fewest. The chronicler records that the gathering is a multi-day event during which the stories of the past year are told, the deeds of the watch are recounted, and the young are taught the old songs that name the spirits of the peaks in order. Long Night is present in the gathering as the spirit of the dark that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the long evening.

The figure is also invoked at the customary shrines of the high country, the marked places upon the high passes and the principal summits where the communities have long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrines are not buildings in the formal sense; they are marked places, usually a cairn of stones or a standing stone or a sheltered hollow in the lee of a rock, and the tending of the shrines is a responsibility shared among the households of the clans. Long Night is present in the shrines as the spirit of the dark that the shrines acknowledge, and her presence is renewed each year by the lighting of the watch fires and the speaking of the customary words at the changing of the watch.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Long Night falls at the midwinter, the darkest time of the year, when the people of the high interior most need the reassurance that the polar dark will not last forever. The festival is observed in every clan and every snow-bound valley of the cold interior, and the rites are conducted at the principal hall of the high clan by the elder of the gathering. The [Long Night Festival](/content/culture/long-night-festival) that the temperate lowlands observe at the same season is a separate observance, descended from the older hunting rituals of the pre-historic peoples, and the chronicler notes that the two festivals share the timing but not the form. The festival of the high country is conducted in the customary quiet of the long evening, and the words spoken are the words of the high country rather than the words of the lowland towns.

The rite of the long-night gathering is the principal observance of Long Night, and the rite is conducted at the principal hall of the high clan over a period of several days. The elder of the gathering speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, asking Long Night to receive the community into her care for the remainder of the winter and to grant the endurance that the long dark requires. The chronicler records that the words have changed little over the generations, and that the form of the rite is the same in every clan from the high passes of the eastern mountains to the western reaches of the snow-bound valleys. The rite of the long-night gathering is the most universal of the observances of the cold interior, and the figure is honoured in the rite as the spirit of the dark that the gathering acknowledges.

A distinctive observance of Long Night is the rite of the watch fire, conducted at the changing of the watch upon the high points of the escarpment. The watchkeepers of the high country are charged with the maintenance of the signal fires that the clans maintain as the chief means of communication across the broken country, and the [Frostwall Fire Signs](/content/language/pseudo/frostwall-fire-signs) are the code by which the watchkeepers convey information. The lighting of the watch fire at the changing of the watch is accompanied by a brief invocation of Long Night, and the invocation is a formal acknowledgement that the watch is the service of the community to the gods of the peaks. The chronicler records that the watchkeepers who have maintained the fires through the long winter are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of Long Night's care for the high country.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Long Night is the third of the greater powers of the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country), and her position in the order of the greater powers is the closing of the winter cycle. The [Nivea](/content/belief/deity/nivea) is the first of the greater powers, governing the snow that blankets the peaks and the ice that bridges the deep chasms, and the [Aero](/content/belief/deity/aero) is the second, governing the high winds and the great storms that descend from the peaks. Long Night is the third, governing the polar darkness that holds the interior in unbroken night for the weeks of deep winter. The three are acknowledged collectively at the long-night gathering, but each also has her own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the annual cycle, and the figure of Long Night is honoured last because the dark is the last of the three gifts that the gods bestow upon the high country before the spring.

The three greater powers are understood to be a single order of divine care for the cold interior, and the figures are invoked together at the long-night gathering and at the principal shrines of the high country. The Nivea is the snow that covers the peaks and the ice that bridges the chasms, the Aero is the gales that scour the upper slopes, and Long Night is the polar darkness that holds the interior in unbroken night. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in a fixed sequence at the long-night gathering, and the sequence is observed with great strictness in every clan. The figure of Long Night is present in the sequence as the spirit of the dark that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is the foundation of the unity of the observance.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the gods of the individual peaks, glaciers, and valleys, and the catalogue of these local powers is long. The dwarven clans maintain lists of the gods of the principal summits of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) and the surrounding ranges, recording them alongside the names of the passes and the water sources that lie beneath each peak. A new name is added only only when a summit has been known across several generations and the community can attest to its consistency, and the practice ensures that the local gods are those that the people have actually lived with rather than abstract inventions. Long Night is present in the local gods as the spirit of the dark that the summits inhabit, and her presence is renewed each year by the lighting of the watch fires upon the high points.

## The Dark and the Endurance of the People

Long Night is honoured not only in the gift of the darkness but also in the recognition of the endurance that the dark requires. The chronicler of the mountain records has set down that the dark of the high winter is the most severe test of the year, and the communities of the cold interior mark the passage of the weeks of the polar dark with a series of observances that the chronicler has described as among the most austere of the high country. The observance of the dark is a recognition that the figure has the power to grant or withhold the endurance of the people, and the community places itself under the figure's protection until the light returns.

The weeks of the polar dark are the time when the watchkeepers of the high country are most active, and the chronicler records that the watch is the central duty of the clans during the long winter. The watchkeepers are responsible for monitoring the conditions of the snow and the weather, for maintaining the signal fires that the clans maintain as the chief means of communication, and for warning the communities of the approach of storms or the movement of enemy parties. Long Night is present in the watch as the spirit of the dark that the watchkeepers serve, and the neglect of the watch is regarded as a breach of duty to both the figure and the community. The chronicler records that the watchkeepers who have served through the long winter are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of Long Night's care for the high country.

The figure is also honoured in the observation of the stars, which are visible in the long evenings of the high country and which the watchkeepers and the elders use to read the passing of the weeks. The stars are the only light of the long winter beyond the watch fires and the household lamps, and the elders of the clans have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the reading of the stars and the timing of the watch. The chronicler notes that the observation of the stars is a form of devotion that is practised by every elder of the high country, and Long Night is honoured in the observation as the spirit of the dark that the stars illuminate. The figure is present in the long evenings in a manner that no other figure of the high country quite matches.

## The Long Night and the Return of the Light

The return of the light is the most visible sign of Long Night's withdrawal, and the communities of the cold interior mark the moment with a quiet observance that the chronicler has described as among the most hopeful of the high country. The observance is a recognition that the figure has turned away, and the community places itself under the protection of the Nivea until the snow begins to melt. The first appearance of the sun above the horizon at the end of the polar dark is marked by a brief ceremony at the principal shrine of the high country, and the ceremony is the formal acknowledgement that the dark has ended and the year has begun anew.

The chronicler records that the return of the light is the most universal of the observances of the high country, and the figure of Long Night is honoured in the return as the spirit of the dark that has withdrawn. The figure is present in the return of the light as the spirit whose gift has been the endurance of the people, and the community's thanks for the return is the formal acknowledgement of the figure's care. The chronicler notes that the observance of the return is conducted with great solemnity in every clan, and the figure is honoured in the observance as the foundation of the prosperity of the high country.

The long evening of the autumn is also an occasion of Long Night's honour, and the communities of the cold interior mark the closing of the grazing season with a customary rite at the principal shrine. The rite is conducted by the elder of the gathering, and the words spoken are the words of acknowledgement that the long dark is approaching and that the community must prepare for the weeks ahead. The chronicler records that the rite of the long evening is observed with great regularity in every clan, and the figure is present in the rite as the spirit of the dark that the community is preparing to enter. The autumn rite is the foundation of the unity of the observance, and Long Night is honoured in the rite as the spirit of the patience that the year requires.

## Relation to the Stone Reading

The [Stone Reading](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/stone-reading) is the dwarven reverence for the stone, and the two faiths are not contradictory but complementary. The gods of the ice are the powers of the surface of the cold interior, and the gods of the stone are the powers of the rock beneath the ice. Long Night is honoured in both traditions, and the chronicler records that the dual observance is considered the mark of the complete dwarf. The gods of the ice and the gods of the stone are held to be kin, for both are powers of the mountain, one above ground and one below, and the dwarven clans maintain that a community that honours only one cannot fully understand the nature of the mountain.

The dwarven contribution to the high country extends beyond religious practice, and the dwarven high clans are the principal builders of the passes and the bridges of the cold interior. The road networks that connect the remote valleys to the principal halls of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) are largely the product of dwarven engineering, and the maintenance of these roads is funded jointly by the clans and by the tolls levied upon the caravans that use them. The chronicler notes that the tolls are considered a form of payment to the gods of the peaks for the safe passage they provide, and Long Night is honoured in the maintenance of the roads as the spirit of the dark that the roads must withstand. The figure is present in the roads as the spirit of the patience that the long winter requires, and the dwarven engineers are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of the figure's care.

## The Lupines and the Long Night

The [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines) of the cold steppes are the principal non-dwarven adherents of Long Night, and their observance is adapted to the life of the hunt rather than the life of the high pasture. The lupine communities are hunters of the high land, and the hunt is central to their way of life. The lupine elders observe the rite of the first kill each season, offering the first portion of the prey to the shrine of the peak that governs their territory. The faith holds that the land provides the game, and that a hunter who takes more than is needed or who wastes the flesh of the quarries offends the Nivea.

The lupine observance of Long Night takes a distinctive form, for the lupine hunters do not gather at the principal hall of the high country but at the customary camps of their own territories. The lupine elders speak the customary words of acknowledgement at the changing of the watch in the cold camps, and the words are the words of the hunter rather than the herder. The chronicler records that the lupine observance is conducted with the same quietness that marks the dwarven rite, and the figure is honoured in the lupine camps as the spirit of the dark that the hunters must endure. The relationship between the lupine hunters and the dwarven high clans has been generally peaceful, and the chronicler records several instances in which the lupines have warned the clans of the approaches of large herds of game or of the movement of enemy parties.

## Significance

Long Night is the third of the greater powers of the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country), and her observance is the foundation of the winter life of the peoples of the cold interior of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the patience that the long dark requires, the formalisation of the watch through religious obligation, and the integration of the household observance into the everyday practice of the winter have all contributed to the prosperity of the high country. The chronicler finds in Long Night one of the most austere and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the watchkeepers and the householders who depend upon the endurance of the dark for their survival and whose survival's continuation depends upon the dark remaining patient. The figure endures, in the long evening and at the changing of the watch, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the polar dark of the high country.
