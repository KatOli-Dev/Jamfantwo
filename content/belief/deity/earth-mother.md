## Overview

The Earth Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the goddess of the soil, the furrow, and the harvest that closes the agricultural cycle. She is the dark loam that receives the seed, the moist earth that quickens the germ, the patient soil that yields the harvest, and the field that returns to rest after the last sheaf has been cut. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke her at the first ploughing, at the sowing, and at the harvest, and her particular day of observance is the harvest festival itself. The figure is older than the first furrow, and her observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the lowland communities.

The Earth Mother is patient and generous. She is invoked at every turning of the soil, every sowing, every harvest, and every storage of the grain in the granary. Farming communities ask her for a fair harvest, for the preservation of the seed, and for wisdom to leave enough in the granary for the next year's sowing. She is honoured by every act of cultivation, and the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of her favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Earth Mother is conceived as the living presence of the cultivated soil. Her domain extends from the kitchen garden to the broad grainlands of the river valleys and the rolling grasslands of the central lowlands. She is the dark loam of the well-tended field, the moist earth of the early spring, the sun-warmed soil of the late summer, and the cold ground of the winter fallow. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by the turning of the soil and the storing of the seed as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Earth Mother is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community gathers for seasonal rites. The shrine is not a formal building but a marked place, usually a standing stone, an ancient tree, or a spring. The rites conducted there are always attended by the whole community. Her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the agricultural cycle.

The granary is the practical dwelling of the Earth Mother in the household. The storing of seed grain in the granary is one of the most sacred agricultural observances. Customary law generally prohibits the sale of seed grain to ensure each household retains enough to sow the following year. The teaching holds that the gods lend their bounty for the keeping of the people, and to take the last seed or to sell the grain of the poor for profit is to offend the Earth Mother. Communities that have observed this prohibition have survived famines that destroyed those that relied solely upon the goodwill of their neighbours.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Earth Mother falls at the harvest, when the last sheaf has been cut and the grain stored for the winter. The harvest festival is the principal observance of the farming year. The first sheaf is cut with special care and carried in procession to the village shrine, where the elder speaks the customary words of thanks, asking the Earth Mother to receive the harvest and renew the fertility of the soil. The last sheaf is set aside for the poor of the village, a custom held to represent the gods' own share of the harvest.

The rite of the first ploughing marks the opening of the soil in spring. The first furrow is turned as the elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Earth Mother to receive the seed and the [Sun Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) to ripen it. The rite of the sowing follows shortly after, when the seed is cast into the furrow. The green tide of late spring, when the first crops push above the soil, is marked by the rite of renewal, a quiet observance attended by women and children focused upon the blessing of the growing crop.

The rite of the first grain is the late-summer observance celebrated in central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) upon the day the first sheaves are brought in from the fields. The [First-Grain Feast](/content/culture/first-grain-feast) is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone, though the accompanying customs vary. The first sheaf is cut by the elder in a ceremony that signals the commencement of the general harvest.

## The Storehouse and the Duty of Restraint

The storehouse is the practical heart of the observance of the Earth Mother. The custom of laying up grain against famine is considered a religious obligation. Each community is expected to set aside a portion of each harvest in a common store, to be drawn upon in bad years or distributed to those who have lost their grain to drought or flood. The store is administered by the village elder. The teaching holds that the storehouse is the Earth Mother's provision for the lean season, and the neglect of the store is a breach of duty to both the divine and the community.

Restraint in the taking of the harvest is a related teaching. The faith holds that the gods lend the land their bounty for the keeping of the people, not as an unqualified gift. To take the last seed or to sell the grain of the poor for profit offends the Earth Mother. Customary law incorporates this teaching, and several villages in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) specifically forbid the sale of seed grain. The prohibition is observed with great strictness, and breaking it is regarded as a serious offence against the community and the Earth Mother.

The prohibition on selling seed grain has done much to protect farming communities from the worst effects of bad years. Communities that have observed it have maintained a continuity of cultivation that those that neglected it have lost.

## The Halflings and the Small Farms

The [halflings](/content/species/sapient/halflings) of the lowland valleys are among the most devoted adherents of the Earth Mother, and their observance is the most refined of any farming people. Halflings cultivate the soil with care that borders on devotion, and their gardens and fields produce a great variety of grains, vegetables, fruits, and herbs. They were the first to set aside the last sheaf for the poor and the first to draw upon the common store in bad years.

The halfling observance of the Earth Mother is centred on the household garden, and the tending of the garden is regarded as a form of devotion as sacred as any formal rite. Halfling gardens are tended by the entire household, with labour divided according to customary order. Halfling gardens are a model of patient cultivation, and the variety and abundance of the halfling harvest is the envy of the larger farms of the lowland plains.

The relationship between halflings and larger farming communities is one of quiet cooperation. Halflings have shared their seed grain with human communities in bad years, and human communities have in turn shared produce with halfling households in lean months. This quiet reciprocity has sustained the farming communities of the lowland valleys through difficult seasons.

## The Wheel of the Powers

The Earth Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and her position is the closing of the agricultural cycle. The Sun Walker is the first, governing the light that ripens the crop. The [Rain Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) is the second, governing the water that nourishes the seed. The Earth Mother is the third, governing the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each has her or his own day of particular observance. The order is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the Earth Mother is honoured last because she is the closing of the cycle the Sun Walker and Rain Bringer have opened.

Beneath the three greater powers are the gods of individual crops, soil types, and weather patterns that govern each particular region. The catalogues are long and localised. Elders of particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The practice is conservative by design, for to name a power without having lived with its effects invites confusion rather than favour or misfortune.

The Earth Mother is the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The Sun Walker and the Rain Bringer are the powers that open the cycle and sustain it through the growing season. The three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, understood as a single order of divine care for the cultivated land.

## Significance

The Earth Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and her observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The reverence for the breeding cycles of the crops, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of halflings and other farming peoples into the everyday practice of the agricultural year have contributed to the prosperity of the lowland communities. The Earth Mother is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the soil for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the soil remaining fertile.
