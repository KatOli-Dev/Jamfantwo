## Overview

The Earth-Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the goddess of the soil, the furrow, and the harvest that closes the agricultural cycle. She is the dark loam that receives the seed, the moist earth that quickens the germ, the patient soil that yields the harvest in its season, and the field that returns to rest after the last sheaf has been cut. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke her at the first ploughing, at the sowing, and at the harvest, and her particular day of observance is the harvest festival itself. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the figure is older than the first furrow, and that her observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the lowland communities.

The figure of the Earth-Mother is patient and generous, and her observance is among the most enduring of the agricultural year. She is invoked at every turning of the soil, at every sowing, at every harvest, and at every storage of the grain in the granary, and the rites that are conducted in her honour are marked by a directness that the chronicler of the field customs has described as the most practical piety he has witnessed. The farming communities do not ask her for blessings in the abstract; they ask her for a fair harvest, for the preservation of the seed, and for the wisdom to leave enough in the granary for the next year's sowing. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of cultivation, and that the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Earth-Mother is conceived as the living presence of the cultivated soil, and her domain extends from the kitchen garden of the village house to the broad grainlands of the river valleys and the rolling grasslands of the central lowlands. She is the dark loam of the well-tended field, the moist earth of the early spring, the sun-warmed soil of the late summer, and the cold ground of the winter fallow. The chronicler of the field records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the turning of the soil and the storing of the seed as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Earth-Mother is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community has long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrine is not a building in the formal sense; it is a marked place, usually a standing stone or an ancient tree or a spring, and the tending of the shrine is a responsibility shared among the households of the village. The chronicler records that the shrine is not a place of private devotion but of communal observance, and the rites conducted there are always attended by the whole community. The Earth-Mother is present in the shrine as the spirit of the soil that the village has cultivated, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the agricultural cycle.

The granary is the practical dwelling of the Earth-Mother in the household, and the storing of the seed grain in the granary is one of the most sacred of the agricultural observances. The chronicler records that the customary law of the farming communities generally incorporates a prohibition on the sale of seed grain for the purpose of ensuring that each household retains enough to sow the following year. The teaching holds that the gods lend the land their bounty for the keeping of the people, not as an unqualified gift, and that to take the last seed or to sell the grain of the poor for profit is to offend the Earth-Mother. The chronicler notes that the communities that have observed this prohibition have survived famines that destroyed those that relied solely upon the goodwill of their neighbours, and that the practice is one of the oldest and most honoured institutions of the farming year.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Earth-Mother falls at the harvest, when the last sheaf has been cut and the grain has been stored in the granary for the winter. The harvest festival is the principal observance of the farming year, and the rites are conducted at the village shrine by the elder of the community. The first sheaf is cut with special care and carried in procession to the shrine, and the elder speaks the customary words of thanks, asking the Earth-Mother to receive the harvest and to renew the fertility of the soil for the year to come. The last sheaf is set aside for the poor of the village, a custom held to represent the gods' own share of the harvest. The chronicler records that the harvest festival is observed with great solemnity in every farming community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the agricultural year.

The rite of the first ploughing marks the opening of the soil in spring, and it is conducted by the village elder at the principal field of the community. The first furrow is turned as the elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Earth-Mother to receive the seed and the [Sun-Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) to ripen it. The rite of the sowing follows shortly after, when the seed is cast into the furrow, and the community gathers to offer the blessing of the harvest before the first green shoot appears. The green tide of late spring, when the first crops push above the soil, is marked by the rite of renewal, a quiet observance attended by the women and the children of the village and focused upon the blessing of the growing crop.

The rite of the first grain is the late-summer observance that is celebrated by the agricultural communities of the central and southern regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) upon the day that the first sheaves of the new harvest are brought in from the fields. The [Feast of the First Grain](/content/culture/feast-of-the-first-grain) is observed in every agricultural community of the temperate zone, though the customs that accompany it vary from one people to another. The first sheaf is cut by the elder of the community in a ceremony that the chronicler has recorded at length, and the cutting of the sheaf is the signal for the commencement of the general harvest that follows in the days and weeks after the feast. The Earth-Mother is honoured in the cutting as the giver of the harvest, and the elder's words at the cutting are the formal acknowledgement of her gift.

## The Storehouse and the Duty of Restraint

The storehouse is the practical heart of the observance of the Earth-Mother, and the custom of laying up grain against the famine is considered a religious obligation. The community is expected to set aside a portion of each harvest in a common store, to be drawn upon in the bad years or distributed to those who have lost their own grain to drought or flood. The store is administered by the village elder, and the chronicler records that communities that have observed this custom faithfully have survived famines that destroyed those that relied solely upon the goodwill of their neighbours. The teaching holds that the storehouse is the Earth-Mother's provision for the lean season, and the neglect of the store is regarded as a breach of duty to both the divine and the community.

A related teaching is the restraint in the taking of the harvest. The faith holds that the gods lend the land their bounty for the keeping of the people, not as an unqualified gift, and that to take the last seed or to sell the grain of the poor for profit is to offend the Earth-Mother. The customary law of the farming communities generally incorporates this teaching, and the laws of several villages in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) specifically forbid the sale of seed grain for the purpose of ensuring that each household retains enough to sow the following year. The chronicler records that the prohibition is observed with great strictness in the farming communities, and that the breaking of the prohibition is regarded as a serious offence against the community and against the Earth-Mother.

The teaching of restraint is observed most strictly in the matter of the seed grain, for the seed is the foundation of the next year's harvest, and a community that sells its seed grain for short-term gain has mortgaged its future for the present. The chronicler notes that the prohibition has done much to protect the farming communities from the worst effects of the bad years, and that the communities that have observed it have maintained a continuity of cultivation that the communities that have neglected it have lost. The Earth-Mother is honoured in the preservation of the seed as much as in any formal rite, and the figure is present in the granary as the spirit of the soil that the seed will renew.

## The Halflings and the Small Farms

The [halflings](/content/species/sapient/halflings) of the lowland valleys are among the most devoted adherents of the Earth-Mother, and their observance of the figure is the most refined of any of the farming peoples. The halflings cultivate the soil with a care that borders on devotion, and their gardens and fields produce a great variety of grains, vegetables, fruits, and herbs, each tended with the patience that the figure demands. The chronicler records that the halflings are the first of the farming peoples to set aside the last sheaf for the poor, and the first to draw upon the common store in the bad years, and that the practice has done much to maintain the unity of the small farming communities of the lowland valleys.

The halfling observance of the Earth-Mother is centred on the household garden, and the tending of the garden is regarded as a form of devotion that is as sacred as any of the formal rites of the village shrine. The halfling gardens are tended by the entire household, and the labour is divided according to the customary order that the community has established. The chronicler notes that the halfling gardens are a model of patient cultivation, and that the variety and the abundance of the halfling harvest is the envy of the larger farms of the lowland plains. The Earth-Mother is honoured in the halfling garden as the spirit of the soil that the household has tended, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the agricultural cycle.

The relationship between the halflings and the larger farming communities of the lowland valleys is one of quiet cooperation, and the chronicler records that the halflings have been known to share their seed grain with the human communities in the bad years, and the human communities have in turn shared the produce of the larger fields with the halfling households in the lean months. The exchange is the foundation of a quiet reciprocity that has sustained the farming communities of the lowland valleys through difficult seasons, and the Earth-Mother is honoured in the exchange as the spirit of the soil that the communities have cultivated together. The chronicler notes that the halfling observance is the most intimate of any of the farming peoples, and the figure is present in the small farms of the lowland valleys in a manner that no other figure of the agricultural year quite matches.

## The Wheel of the Powers

The Earth-Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and her position in the Wheel is the closing of the agricultural cycle. The Sun-Walker is the first of the greater powers, governing the light that ripens the crop, and the [Rain-Bringer](/content/belief/deity/rain-bringer) is the second, governing the water that nourishes the seed. The Earth-Mother is the third, governing the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each also has her or his own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the figure of the Earth-Mother is honoured last because the figure is the closing of the cycle that the Sun-Walker and the Rain-Bringer have opened.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the gods of the individual crops, the soil types, and the weather patterns that govern each particular region. The god of wheat, the goddess of the barley, the spirit of the lentil, the guardian of the dry soil, the warden of the wet - the catalogue is long and localised. The elders of the more particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or a soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The chronicler notes that the practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that to name a power without having lived with its effects is to invite neither favour nor misfortune but only the confusion of the young.

The Earth-Mother is honoured in the relation of the three greater powers, for the figure is the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle, and the Sun-Walker and the Rain-Bringer are the powers that open the cycle and sustain it through the growing season. The chronicler records that the three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, and the three are understood to be a single order of divine care for the cultivated land. The Earth-Mother is the foundation of the order, and her presence in the soil is the ground upon which the work of the Sun-Walker and the Rain-Bringer is built.

## Significance

The Earth-Mother is the third of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and her observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the crops, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of the [halflings](/content/species/sapient/halflings) and the other farming peoples into the everyday practice of the agricultural year have all contributed to the prosperity of the lowland communities. The chronicler finds in the Earth-Mother one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the soil for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the soil remaining fertile. The figure endures, in the field and at the granary, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the cultivated earth.
