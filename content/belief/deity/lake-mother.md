## Overview

The Lake-Mother is the principal deity of the [Old Gods of the Inland Lakes](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-inland-lakes), the goddess in whom the still water, the reflection, and the depth of the interior lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) are embodied. She is the calm surface that mirrors the sky, the dark depth that holds the secret of the lake, the still water that reflects the evening light, and the cold water that the winter ice seals and the spring thaw releases. The lake-shore communities of the [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) interior invoke her at the calm of the evening when the lake is at its stillest, and her festival falls at the breaking of the ice in the spring, when the lake becomes accessible to the fisherfolk and the communities can resume their full seasonal activities. The chronicler of the lake records has set down that the figure is older than the first net and the first shore-dwelling, and that the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes and the [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the still waters have honoured her since before the first human settlement was founded upon the lake-shore.

The figure of the Lake-Mother is patient and reflective, and her observance is among the most intimate of the inland year. She is invoked at every cast of the net, at every launch of the boat, at every quiet evening upon the shore, and at every breaking of the ice, and the rites that are conducted in her honour are marked by a stillness that the chronicler of the lake customs has described as the most attentive piety he has witnessed. The lake communities do not ask her for blessings in the abstract; they ask her for clear water, for the preservation of the fish stocks, and for the wisdom to manage the lakes that sustain them. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of lake husbandry, and that the prosperity of the inland lake communities is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Lake-Mother is conceived as the living presence of the still water, and her domain extends from the smallest pond of the marshes to the greatest of the inland lakes of the interior. She is the calm surface that mirrors the sky, the dark depth that holds the secret of the lake, the still water that reflects the evening light, and the cold water that the winter ice seals and the spring thaw releases. The chronicler of the lake records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the observation of the water and the management of the fishery as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Lake-Mother is the lake itself, and the islands of the great inland lakes are the sacred centres of her observance. The islands are not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for they are understood as natural dwellings of the divine, and the rites are conducted upon the shore and the interior of the islands with the customary observances. The chronicler records that the lake islands have long been centres of learning and cultural exchange among the lake communities, and that the gathering of the communities on the islands has done much to preserve the unity of the observance across the wide extent of the interior waters. The Lake-Mother is present in the islands as the spirit of the still water that the islands inhabit, and her presence is renewed each year by the rites of the lake year.

The figure is also honoured in the sacred coves that lie along the lake-shore, the sheltered places where the rites that require the quiet of the water are conducted. The coves are held in common by the lake communities, and the maintenance of the coves is the responsibility of all the lake peoples, regardless of their species or their place of origin. The chronicler notes that the coves are among the most peaceful places in the interior of the continent, and the Lake-Mother is honoured in the quiet of the coves as the spirit of the still water that the coves protect. The figure is present in the coves as the spirit of the water that is undisturbed, and her presence is the foundation of the peace that the coves provide.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Lake-Mother falls at the breaking of the ice in the spring, when the lake becomes accessible to the fisherfolk and the communities can resume their full seasonal activities. The festival is the principal observance of the figure, and the rites are conducted at the principal shore of each lake community by the elder of the community. The elder stands at the shore and speaks the traditional words of acknowledgement, asking the Lake-Mother to receive the community back into her care after the long winter. The chronicler records that the festival is observed with great solemnity in every lake community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the lake year. The Lake-Mother's day at the breaking of the ice is the most universal of the observances of the inland lakes.

The rite of the first catch is a distinctive observance of the Lake-Mother, performed at the first fishing of each season. A young fisher of the community is presented to the lake by the elder, who speaks the traditional words of welcome, asking the Lake-Mother to receive the candidate as a child of the water and to grant safe passage. The young fisher touches the surface and receives a small token - a piece of polished lake stone, the scale of the first fish caught, or a waterfowl feather - that is kept for life. The chronicler notes that this practice, while simple, has done much to strengthen the emotional bond between the young and the lake, and that lake communities that observe it faithfully report lower rates of accidental drownings among the young than those that neglect it.

The rite of the spawning follows the return of the fish to the inlet streams, and it is conducted by the fisherfolk of the community before the nets are set for the season. The elder speaks the traditional words of acknowledgement, asking the Lake-Mother to grant a fair catch and to preserve the breeding stock for the year to come. The harvest of the lake is the second principal observance, celebrated in the late summer when the fish stocks are at their peak and the waterfowl begin their southward migration. The first fish of the catch is offered at the customary shrine by the elder, and the offering is a formal acknowledgement that the fish are not the property of the fisher but the gift of the Lake-Mother, lent for the keeping of the community.

## The Peoples of the Lake

The [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes are the principal non-human adherents of the Lake-Mother, and their observance is adapted to the wetland environment that borders the lake. The anuran communities are dwellers of the marsh and the still water, and the marsh is held to be the inheritance of the anuran communities. The chronicler records that the anuran communities honour the figure at the customary seasons, and the anuran communities are required to offer the first fish of the catch at the customary shrine of the community. The marsh is not the exclusive domain of any single people; rather, it is understood as a shared space in which each people occupies its own niche, and the observance teaches that the marsh, like the lake, is a trust that is shared among the peoples who depend upon it.

The [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the still waters are also adherents of the Lake-Mother, although their observance is adapted to the life of the deep water rather than the shore. The kappa communities are dwellers of the lake and the pond, and their understanding of the lake ecosystem is deep, particularly their knowledge of the fish species and the winter conditions that are often hidden from the shore-dwellers. The kappa observe the breaking of the ice at the first thaw, and they maintain the customary quotas that govern the fishing of the lake during the spawning season. The chronicler notes that the kappa knowledge of the lake is often inaccessible to the human communities, and that the kappa are sometimes reluctant to share it, given the history of exploitation by shore-dwelling peoples in other parts of the known world.

The human lake-shore communities of the [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) interior are the third of the principal adherents of the Lake-Mother, and their observance is adapted to the life of the shore. The human communities maintain the customary shrines at the principal shores and islands of the lakes, and the shrines are tended by the elders of the communities under the oversight of the customary councils. The human observance of the figure is more formal than the anuran or the kappa observance, and the human communities have developed a body of customary law that governs the management of the fishery, the maintenance of the shrines, and the conduct of the seasonal rites. The chronicler notes that the human observance is the most public of any of the lake peoples, and the figure is present in the human shore communities in a manner that no other figure of the inland year quite matches.

## The Watch and the Ice-Breaker

The watch is the central duty of the inland lake communities, and the watchkeepers are responsible for monitoring the conditions of the water and relaying warnings about storms, the approach of dangerous ice, and the movement of the waterfowl. The watch is conducted from the principal shore and the island shrines, and the watchkeepers are often the same elders who conduct the seasonal rites. The Lake-Mother is honoured in the watch as the spirit of the water that the watchkeepers observe, and the neglect of the watch is regarded as a breach of duty to both the figure and the community. The chronicler records that the communities that have maintained the watch faithfully over the generations have been more resilient through bad seasons, and the figure is present in the watch as the spirit of the water that the watchkeepers serve.

The ice-breaker is the duty of keeping the channels open through the ice during the winter, allowing the fish to survive the cold months and the villagers to draw water from the lake. The ice-breaker is performed by a dedicated team of workers from the community, and the work is considered a form of service to the Lake-Mother, for the ice-breaker ensures that the lake remains alive and accessible even in the depths of winter. The chronicler records that the ice-breaker is among the most demanding of the communal duties of the lake year, and the workers who perform it are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of the figure's care for the lake. The Lake-Mother is present in the ice-breaker as the spirit of the water that the workers serve, and the work is the foundation of the prosperity of the lake communities.

The watch and the ice-breaker are among the most practical applications of the observance, and the chronicler notes that the communities that have maintained them faithfully have been more resilient through bad seasons. The watch is also a source of valuable information for the surrounding farming communities, as the lake communities are often the first to observe the weather patterns that precede the spring rains and the autumn storms. The Lake-Mother is honoured in the exchange of information as the spirit of the water that the lake communities observe, and the exchange is the foundation of the cooperation between the lake and the farming communities.

## The Sacred Coves and the Lake Islands

The lake islands of the great inland lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) are the principal sacred centres of the observance, and they serve as the gathering points for the lake communities at the turning of the year. The islands are not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for they are understood as natural dwellings of the divine, and the rites are conducted upon the shore and the interior of the islands with the customary observances. The chronicler records that the lake islands have long been centres of learning and cultural exchange among the lake communities, and that the gathering of the communities on the islands has done much to preserve the unity of the observance across the wide extent of the interior waters.

The sacred coves are the sheltered places along the lake-shore where the rites that require the quiet of the water are conducted, and the coves are held in common by the lake communities. The coves are not built upon, for they are understood as natural dwellings of the Lake-Mother, and the rites that are conducted in them are marked by a stillness that the chronicler has described as the most attentive piety of the inland year. The coves are the places where the young are presented to the lake in the rite of the first catch, and the coves are the places where the elders speak the customary words of blessing for the new season. The chronicler notes that the coves are the most peaceful places in the interior of the continent, and the figure is present in the coves as the spirit of the still water that the coves protect.

The island shrines are maintained by the communities in common, and the maintenance is considered the responsibility of all the lake peoples, regardless of their species or their place of origin. The observance teaches that the island is the dwelling of the divine in the still water, and the lake communities are required to honour the island and the cove with the customary observances. The chronicler records that the islands and the coves are the foundation of the unity of the observance, and the figure is present in the islands and the coves as the spirit of the still water that they protect. The Lake-Mother endures in the islands and the coves as the living inheritance of the lake communities, and the figure is honoured in the maintenance of the islands and the coves as the foundation of the prosperity of the inland lakes.

## Significance

The Lake-Mother is the principal power of the still water, and her observance is the foundation of the [Old Gods of the Inland Lakes](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-inland-lakes). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the fish, the formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and the integration of the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) and the [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) knowledge into the everyday practice of the lake communities have all contributed to the prosperity of the inland lake regions of the known world. The chronicler finds in the Lake-Mother one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the lake-fishers and the farmer who depend upon the lake for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the lake remaining healthy. The figure endures, in the still water and at the breaking of the ice, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the interior waters of the continent.
