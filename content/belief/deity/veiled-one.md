## Overview

The Veiled One is the divine of the [Path of the Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/path-of-the-sunless-veils), an ascetic and contemplative monotheist tradition held chiefly by the cloistered communities of the inland valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The figure is unnamed in the religion's own rite, for the path teaches that the name of the divine is a thing to be set aside rather than forgotten, and the faithful refer to the figure as the Veiled One in recognition of the successive coverings that the divine has wrapped about the world. The chronicler of the cloister records has set down that the figure is older than the first cloister of veils and the first long retreat, and that the hermits of the inland valleys have honoured the figure since before the first formal community withdrew from the market towns of the central highlands to take up residence in the quiet valleys where the sun's rays reach only briefly in the winter months.

The figure of the Veiled One is hidden from view by the world's brightness and by the noise of its commerce, and the path teaches that the soul must learn to veil itself from the world's distractions before it can attend to the dimmer light that the veils conceal. The path does not ask its adherents for elaborate rites or for public display; the path asks for the long retreat, the silence of the curtained chamber, and the patience to lift the coverings in their order without grasping at coverings yet unseen. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of withdrawal that the cloistered communities perform, and that the quietness of the inland valleys is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and the Doctrine of the Veils

The Veiled One is conceived as the living presence that the world conceals, and the figure's domain extends from the noise of the market town to the dim light of the curtained chamber. She is the stillness that lies behind the brightness of the day, the patience that lies behind the haste of the world's commerce, and the quiet that lies behind the noise of opinion and of news. The chronicler of the cloister records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the lifting of the veils and the veiling of the self as much as by any formal rite.

The doctrine of the path holds that the world is wrapped in a succession of coverings, each more subtle than the last, and that the practitioner's task is to learn to lift them in order. The first veil is the noise of the world's commerce, the busy traffic of news and of opinion that reaches the soul before it can attend to anything else. The second veil is the lure of the world's pleasure, the brightness of its appearance and the warmth of its regard, which holds the eye and prevents the soul from withdrawing. The third veil is the fear of the world's empty spaces, the dread of the quiet places and of the long still hours that the practice requires. The elders teach that these three are the veils commonly spoken of, and that beyond them lie further veils that the practitioner will discover in the course of the long retreat.

The Veiled One is present in the doctrine of the veils as the presence that the coverings conceal, and the figure is honoured in the lifting of each veil as the practitioner comes to perceive what the covering has hidden. The chronicler records that the doctrine is not a doctrine of breaking but a doctrine of lifting, and that the soul which tries to break the coverings rather than gently remove them will find itself in a worse state than if it had not begun. The teaching is one of patience and of attention, and the elders counsel the novice to lift each veil as it presents itself, without grasping at veils yet unseen. The figure is present in the patience of the practice as the spirit of the quiet that the practitioner is learning to attend.

## The Name and the Practice of Unnaming

The path teaches that the name of the divine is a thing to be set aside rather than forgotten, and the rite of the path makes no use of the name in the customary sense. The faithful refer to the figure as the Veiled One, and the title is a recognition of the coverings that the figure has wrapped about the world rather than a name in the ordinary sense. The chronicler of the cloister records has set down that the practice of unnaming is one of the most distinctive features of the path, and that the practice is observed with great strictness in every cloister of the inland valleys.

The rite of unnaming is conducted at the entrance of the novice to the cloister, when the senior veiler receives the candidate and speaks the customary words of welcome. The words make no use of the name of the divine, and the words are a formal acknowledgement that the novice is entering the practice of the veils and that the figure is to be sought in the quiet of the curtained chamber rather than in the noise of the public rite. The chronicler records that the rite of unnaming is observed with great solemnity in every cloister, and the figure is honoured in the rite as the presence that the practice of unnaming acknowledges. The chronicler notes that the practice of unnaming is not a denial of the figure but a setting aside of the customary forms of address, and the faithful hold that the figure is more truly honoured by the practice of unnaming than by any rite that makes use of the name.

The chronicler observes that the practice of unnaming has been the source of much misunderstanding among the visitors to the cloisters, and that the path has been described as atheistical by those who have not understood the distinction between the setting aside of the name and the denial of the figure. The elders of the path are patient with the misunderstanding, and they counsel the visitor that the figure is present in the practice of the veils as the presence that the coverings conceal, and that the practice of unnaming is the most attentive of the honours that the figure receives. The figure is honoured in the patience of the correction as the spirit of the quiet that the elders serve.

## The Cloister of Veils and the Long Retreat

The principal dwelling of the Veiled One is the cloister of veils, the curtained chamber where the long retreat is conducted. The chamber is a small room in the cloister, with a single window that is curtained against the day's brightness, and the practitioner is permitted the company only of a senior veiler who attends to the discipline without conversation. The retreat is not a punishment, and the path is careful to mark the difference. The retreat is held to be the chief instrument by which the veils are lifted in their order, and the elders regard it as the most demanding and the most rewarding of the path's disciplines.

The Veiled One is present in the cloister as the presence that the curtained chamber conceals, and the figure is honoured in the long retreat as the spirit of the quiet that the practitioner is learning to attend. The chronicler records that the figure is invoked by the simple observance of the silence and the dim light, and that the practitioner's task is to learn to perceive the figure behind the coverings of the world's noise and brightness. The chronicler notes that the long retreat is observed with great strictness in every cloister, and the senior veilers are given the title of keeper of the veils, and they are charged with the oversight of the retreats and the instruction of the novices.

The chronicler observes that the cloister of veils is supported by the labour of the community and by the small offerings of the faithful of the hill towns, who regard the retreat as a service to the wider faith and who contribute accordingly. The keepers of the veils hold no worldly authority, and they continue to maintain themselves by their hands, and the chronicler notes that the absence of worldly authority is held to be a mark of the path's fidelity to the teaching of the veils. The figure is honoured in the absence of worldly authority as the presence that the figure provides, and the chronicler notes that the cloisters have been the foundation of the quiet strength of the inland valleys for as many generations as the chronicles record.

## The Veiling of the Self

The path teaches that the lifting of the veils is impossible without a corresponding veiling of the self, and the elders speak of this as the veiling of the self. The practitioner must learn to wrap the small self in a comparable obscurity, so that the noise and the brightness of the small self do not reach outward to replace the veils that have been lifted. The elders counsel the novice that the discipline is to be taken slowly and without display, and a practitioner who returns from the retreat and speaks at length of his experience is regarded by the elders as having missed the chief point.

The Veiled One is honoured in the veiling of the self as the presence that the veiling acknowledges, and the figure is present in the simplicity of the practitioner's dress and habit. The path counsels simplicity in the clothing and a quietness in the speech, and the faithful do not wear a special habit at all times, but they are accustomed to keep a simple cloak for the days of retreat and a quieter form of address when the discipline is uppermost. The chronicler records that the elders caution against the temptation to make a display of the simplicity, for the path regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very veil the discipline counsels against.

The veiling of the self extends to the practitioner's daily life, for the path teaches that the lifting of the veils continues outward into the household and the workshop, and the practitioner who is unaware of this continuation has not yet completed the discipline. The figure is honoured in the household and the workshop as the presence that the practitioner serves, and the chronicler notes that the faithful of the hill towns are known for their quiet manner and their patience in the daily round. The chronicler observes that the veiling of the self is among the most distinctive of the path's teachings, and the figure is present in the self's veiling as the spirit of the quiet that the practitioner is learning to attend.

## The Wanderers and the Quiet Houses

The path is served by a class of wandering veilers who travel between the cloisters and the hill towns, maintaining the discipline in the wider community and recruiting novices for the long retreat. The wanderers are recognised by the small white staff they carry and by their custom of speaking softly and at length only when the company requires it. The wandering office arose from the need to maintain contact between the cloisters and the wider community of the hill towns, whose distance from one another would otherwise have permitted the discipline to drift apart over the years.

The Veiled One is honoured in the wanderers' labour as the presence that the wandering acknowledges, and the figure is present in the wanderers' visits to the hill towns as the spirit of the quiet that the wanderers bring. The chronicler records that the wanderers are received with great respect in the hill towns, and the figure is honoured in the reception as the presence that the visit acknowledges. The chronicler notes that the wandering office is one of the most distinctive of the path's institutions, and the figure is present in the wandering as the spirit of the quiet that the wanderers carry between the cloisters and the hill towns.

The path maintains also a number of quiet houses in the hill towns, where the faithful of the surrounding countryside may make a brief retreat without undertaking the long withdrawal of the cloister. The quiet houses are humble establishments that lack the chamber and the silence of the cloister but provide a few days of quiet and counsel to those who cannot leave their households for long. The keepers of the quiet houses are appointed by the senior veilers of the nearest cloister, and the office is regarded by the path as a worthy complement to the long retreat. The figure is honoured in the quiet houses as the presence that the brief retreat acknowledges, and the chronicler notes that the quiet houses have been the foundation of the path's quiet growth in the hill country.

## Relation to Other Contemplative Traditions

The [Path of the Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/path-of-the-sunless-veils) has long coexisted with the other contemplative traditions of the continent, and it has been particularly close to the [Doctrine of the Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/doctrine-of-the-unwritten-name), whose scholars maintain their own discipline of restraint. The two paths are not the same, for the Doctrine is a doctrine of careful reading in the cloister, while the Path of the Veils is a discipline of bodily retreat, but the teachers of each have been careful to mark the kinship between the disciplines. A senior reader of the Doctrine will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the cloister of the Veils, and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of readers, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions. The Veiled One is honoured in the courtesy as the presence that the kindred traditions share, and the figure is present in the exchange between the disciplines as the spirit of the quiet that both traditions serve.

The path has found a sympathetic hearing among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the southern woods, who maintain their own disciplines of withdrawal in the deep forest. The elven contemplatives and the keepers of the veils have exchanged visits more than once in recent generations, and each has found in the other a kindred disposition. The path regards this kinship as a privilege rather than a claim, and it asks nothing of the elven people beyond the courtesy of the meeting. The figure is honoured in the kinship as the presence that the kindred traditions share, and the chronicler notes that the kinship has been the foundation of the path's quiet growth in the inland valleys.

The path has found less common ground with the more public faiths of the trading cities, whose observances are conducted in the open air and in the hearing of the market. The chronicler records that the path has been regarded with a mixture of curiosity and respect by the urban peoples, and the path has not sought to extend its observance into the cities. The elders of the path are content with the slower growth, and they counsel the novice that the brightness of the cities is the very veil the path counsels against, and that the figure is more truly honoured by the long retreat than by any public display. The figure is honoured in the patience of the slow growth as the spirit of the quiet that the path serves.

## Significance

The Veiled One is the divine of the [Path of the Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/path-of-the-sunless-veils), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the ascetic and contemplative life of the cloistered communities of the inland valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts the discipline of the veils, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the quiet that the coverings conceal, the formalisation of the long retreat through religious obligation, and the integration of the veiling of the self into the everyday practice of the hill country have all contributed to the quietness that the path has given to the inland communities. The chronicler finds in the Veiled One one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the keepers of the veils and the wanderers who depend upon the quiet for their practice and whose practice's continuation depends upon the quiet remaining patient. The figure endures, in the curtained chamber and at the lifting of each veil, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the dim light that the coverings conceal.
