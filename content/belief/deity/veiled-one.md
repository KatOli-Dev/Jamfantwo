## Overview

The Veiled One is the divine of the [Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/sunless-veils), an ascetic and contemplative monotheist tradition held chiefly by the cloistered communities of the inland valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The figure is unnamed in the religion's own rite, for the path teaches that the name of the divine is a thing to be set aside rather than forgotten, and the faithful refer to the figure as the Veiled One in recognition of the successive coverings that the divine has wrapped about the world. The figure is older than the first cloister of veils and the first long retreat, and the hermits of the inland valleys have honoured the figure since before the first formal community withdrew from the market towns of the central highlands to take up residence in the quiet valleys where the sun's rays reach only briefly in the winter months.

The Veiled One is hidden from view by the world's brightness and the noise of its commerce. The path teaches that the soul must learn to veil itself from the world's distractions before it can attend to the dimmer light that the veils conceal. The path does not ask for elaborate rites or public display; it asks for the long retreat, the silence of the curtained chamber, and the patience to lift the coverings in their order without grasping at coverings yet unseen. The figure is honoured by every act of withdrawal the cloistered communities perform, and the quietness of the inland valleys is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and the Doctrine of the Veils

The Veiled One is conceived as the living presence that the world conceals. Her domain extends from the noise of the market town to the dim light of the curtained chamber. She is the stillness behind the brightness of the day, the patience behind the haste of the world's commerce, and the quiet behind the noise of opinion and news. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by the lifting of the veils and the veiling of the self as much as by formal rite.

The doctrine holds that the world is wrapped in a succession of coverings, each more subtle than the last, and the practitioner's task is to learn to lift them in order. The first veil is the noise of the world's commerce, the busy traffic of news and opinion that reaches the soul before it can attend to anything else. The second veil is the lure of the world's pleasure, the brightness of its appearance and warmth of its regard. The third veil is the fear of the world's empty spaces, the dread of quiet places and long still hours. The elders teach that these three are the veils commonly spoken of, and beyond them lie further veils the practitioner will discover in the course of the long retreat.

The Veiled One is present in the doctrine of the veils as the presence the coverings conceal, honoured in the lifting of each veil. The doctrine is not one of breaking but of lifting. The soul that tries to break the coverings rather than gently remove them will find itself in a worse state than if it had not begun.

## The Name and the Practice of Unnaming

The path teaches that the name of the divine is a thing to be set aside rather than forgotten. The rite of the path makes no use of the name in the customary sense. The faithful refer to the figure as the Veiled One, a recognition of the coverings rather than a name in the ordinary sense. The practice of unnaming is one of the most distinctive features of the path, observed with great strictness in every cloister.

The rite of unnaming is conducted at the entrance of the novice to the cloister, when the senior veiler receives the candidate and speaks the customary words of welcome. The words make no use of the name of the divine, formally acknowledging that the novice is entering the practice of the veils and that the figure is to be sought in the quiet of the curtained chamber rather than in the noise of public rite.

The practice of unnaming has been a source of misunderstanding among visitors to the cloisters, leading some to describe the path as atheistical. The elders counsel the visitor that the figure is present in the practice of the veils as the presence the coverings conceal, and the practice of unnaming is the most attentive of the honours the figure receives.

## The Veil Cloister and the Long Retreat

The principal dwelling of the Veiled One is the cloister of veils, the curtained chamber where the long retreat is conducted. The chamber is a small room with a single window curtained against the day's brightness. The practitioner is permitted the company only of a senior veiler who attends to the discipline without conversation. The retreat is not a punishment, and the path carefully marks the difference. It is held to be the chief instrument by which the veils are lifted in order, regarded as the most demanding and rewarding of the path's disciplines.

The Veiled One is present in the cloister as the presence the curtained chamber conceals, honoured in the long retreat as the spirit of the quiet the practitioner is learning to attend. The figure is invoked by the simple observance of silence and dim light. Senior veilers are given the title of keeper of the veils, charged with oversight of the retreats and instruction of the novices.

The cloister of veils is supported by the labour of the community and the small offerings of the faithful of the hill towns, who regard the retreat as a service to the wider faith. Keepers of the veils hold no worldly authority and maintain themselves by their hands. The absence of worldly authority is held to be a mark of the path's fidelity to the teaching of the veils.

## The Veiling of the Self

The path teaches that the lifting of the veils is impossible without a corresponding veiling of the self. The practitioner must learn to wrap the small self in comparable obscurity, so that the noise and brightness of the small self do not reach outward to replace the veils that have been lifted. The discipline is taken slowly and without display. A practitioner who returns from the retreat and speaks at length of his experience is regarded as having missed the chief point.

The Veiled One is honoured in the veiling of the self as the presence the veiling acknowledges, present in the simplicity of the practitioner's dress and habit. The path counsels simplicity in clothing and quietness in speech. The faithful do not wear a special habit at all times but keep a simple cloak for days of retreat and a quieter form of address when the discipline is uppermost. The elders caution against making a display of simplicity, for such a display is itself a kind of brightness.

The veiling of the self extends to the practitioner's daily life. The lifting of the veils continues outward into the household and the workshop, and the practitioner unaware of this continuation has not yet completed the discipline. The faithful of the hill towns are known for their quiet manner and patience in the daily round.

## The Wanderers and the Quiet Houses

The path is served by a class of wandering veilers who travel between the cloisters and the hill towns, maintaining the discipline in the wider community and recruiting novices for the long retreat. They are recognised by the small white staff they carry and by their custom of speaking softly and at length only when the company requires it. The wandering office arose from the need to maintain contact between the cloisters and the wider community of the hill towns.

The path maintains a number of quiet houses in the hill towns where the faithful of the surrounding countryside may make a brief retreat without undertaking the long withdrawal of the cloister. The quiet houses are humble establishments lacking the chamber and silence of the cloister but providing a few days of quiet and counsel. They are appointed by the senior veilers of the nearest cloister, regarded as a worthy complement to the long retreat.

## Relation to Other Contemplative Traditions

The [Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/sunless-veils) has long coexisted with other contemplative traditions, particularly the [Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/unwritten-name), whose scholars maintain their own discipline of restraint. The two paths are not the same; the Doctrine is one of careful reading in the cloister, while the Path of the Veils is a discipline of bodily retreat. However, the teachers of each recognise kinship between the disciplines. A senior reader of the Doctrine sometimes undertakes the long retreat at the cloister of the Veils, and a senior veiler sometimes attends the conference of readers, welcomed with the courtesy of kindred traditions.

The path has found a sympathetic hearing among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the southern woods, who maintain their own disciplines of withdrawal in the deep forest. Elven contemplatives and keepers of the veils have exchanged visits more than once in recent generations, each finding in the other a kindred disposition.

The path has found less common ground with the more public faiths of the trading cities. It has been regarded with curiosity and respect by urban peoples but has not sought to extend its observance into the cities. The elders are content with slower growth, counselling the novice that the brightness of the cities is the very veil the path counsels against.

## Significance

The Veiled One is the divine of the [Sunless Veils](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/sunless-veils), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the ascetic and contemplative life of the cloistered communities of the inland valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). The reverence for the quiet the coverings conceal, the formalisation of the long retreat through religious obligation, and the integration of the veiling of the self into the everyday practice of the hill country have contributed to the quietness the path has given to the inland communities. The Veiled One is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the keepers of the veils and the wanderers who depend upon the quiet for their practice, whose continuation depends upon the quiet remaining patient.
