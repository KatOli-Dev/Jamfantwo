## The God of the High Winds

The Aero is the second of the greater powers in the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country), the god of the high winds and the great storms that descend from the peaks of the cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and sweep across the valleys of the high country. He is conceived as the spirit of the gale that rises from the snows of the highest crests, the long storm that scourges the upper slopes for days at a time, and the sudden squall that catches the unwary traveller upon the high passes. The dwarven high clans and the human communities of the snow-bound valleys invoke him at the height of the storm season, and his festival falls at the time of the longest gales, when the watchkeepers of the high country sound the alarms and the communities secure their shelter. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the figure is older than the first refuge hall and the first watch fire, and that the highland communities have honoured him since before the first great road was cut through the passes of the cold interior.

The figure of the Aero is honoured with a mixture of reverence and caution, for the storms are both necessary to the fertility of the high country and dangerous to the communities that dwell within their reach. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the winds that descend from the peaks carry the snow that protects the winter stores and the melt that fills the rivers in the spring, but the same winds can flatten a flock of sheep upon the open meadow and can seal a high pass for weeks at a time. The Aero is honoured in the winds as the spirit of the gales that the highland communities must endure and that they must learn to read.

## The Figure and His Domain

The Aero is conceived as the living presence of the wind in its passage across the cold interior, and his domain extends from the highest crests of the cold interior ranges to the sheltered valleys where the highland communities maintain their settlements. He is the gale that rises in the afternoon and dies with the night, the long storm that rages for days and is followed by a stillness that the highland communities regard with caution, and the sudden squall that descends from a clear sky when the conditions of the upper crests change. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the wind and the management of the high country as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Aero is the high pass, the opening in the range where the wind can gather force and where the communities of the high country are most exposed to the gales. The Aero is honoured in the high passes as the spirit of the wind that the passes channel, and the watchkeepers of the high country maintain the signal fires and the refuge halls that protect the travellers who must cross the passes in the storm season. The figure is present in the high passes as the spirit of the wind that the highland communities must observe, and his presence is renewed each year by the storms of the autumn and the spring.

## The Greater Powers of the Cold Interior

The Aero is the second of the greater powers of the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country), and his position in the faith is the honouring of the winds of the high country. The [Nivea](/content/belief/deity/nivea) is the first, the goddess of the snow that blankets the peaks and the ice that bridges the deep chasms. The [Long Night](/content/belief/deity/long-night) is the third, the power of the polar darkness that holds the interior in unbroken night for the weeks of deep winter. Beneath these three are set the gods of the individual peaks, glaciers, and valleys, and the catalogue of these local powers is long. The dwarven clans maintain lists of the gods of the principal summits of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) and the surrounding ranges, recording them alongside the names of the passes and the water sources that lie beneath each peak.

The Aero is honoured in the order of the greater powers as the spirit of the gales that the highland communities must endure, and the watchkeepers speak his name before the name of any of the other powers at the height of the storm season. The chronicler of the cold interior records has noted that the order of the greater powers is fixed by the conditions of the high country, and the figure of the Aero is honoured second because the wind is the second of the forces that the highland communities must master after the snow. The Nivea provides the snow that the wind moves, the Aero provides the wind that the snow obeys, and the Long Night provides the darkness that the storm inhabits.

## The Rites of the Storm Season

The storm season of the cold interior occupies the autumn and the spring, and the rites of the Aero are bound up with the practical labour of the watch. The highland communities maintain the watch fires that signal the state of the passes, and the watchkeepers of the high country are required to sound the alarm when the gales of the upper crests descend upon the valleys. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the stormwatchers of the high country have developed a highly refined understanding of meteorological phenomenon, and that their predictions are often relied upon by the lowland communities of the coasts, who have no comparable means of foreseeing the gales that may descend from the interior.

The principal observance of the Aero falls at the height of the storm season, when the watchkeepers of the high country gather at the principal hall of the high clan to renew the customary words of acknowledgement. The elder of the community speaks the traditional words, asking the Aero to spare the season and the flocks and to grant the highland communities the wisdom to read the signs of the approaching gales. The chronicler records that the words have changed little over the generations, and that the form of the observance is the same in every high clan from the western marches to the eastern ranges.

## The Watch and the Storm Signs

The watch is the central duty of the high country, and the watchkeepers are charged with the observation of signs in the sky and the wind that warn of approaching storms. The watch is conducted from the high points of the escarpment, and the signal fires that the clans maintain are the chief means of communication across the broken country. The [Frostwall Fire Signs](/content/language/pseudo/frostwall-fire-signs) are the code by which the watchkeepers convey information, and the code is observed with great strictness; the failure to maintain the signals at the customary hours is regarded not merely as a neglect of duty but as a breach of religious obligation.

The watchkeepers are drawn from the warrior class of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans), but their role is more than martial. They are also tasked with the reading of the weather, and their knowledge of the sky and the patterns of the storm is considered sacred. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the stormwatchers of the high country are trained from youth in the reading of the wind, and the most experienced of the watchkeepers are held in particular esteem by the highland communities for the accuracy of their predictions. The Aero is honoured in the watch as the spirit of the wind that the watchkeepers observe.

## The Long Storms and the Refuge Halls

The long storms that descend from the peaks can last for days at a time, and the highland communities maintain refuge halls at the principal points of the high country where travellers and herders can shelter from the gales. The refuge halls are stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber, and the halls are maintained by the highland communities in accordance with the customary law that the founding of the watch established. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the refuge halls are among the most useful of the works of the high country, and the halls have saved the lives of travellers and herders who would otherwise have been lost to the gales of the upper slopes.

The Aero is honoured in the refuge halls as the spirit of the wind that the halls protect against, and the figure is present in the maintenance of the halls as the spirit of the elder whose gales the maintenance endures. The chronicler notes that the halls are not built in the manner of the temples of the lowland faiths, for they are practical structures whose purpose is the saving of life rather than the formal acknowledgement of the divine, but the halls are regarded with the same reverence that the temples of the lowland communities inspire.

## The North and the Wind of the Tundra

The cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) is not the only place where the figure of the Aero is honoured, for the cold countries of the far north acknowledge the wind as a power of comparable force and austerity. The continent of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) lies beyond the principal seas of the known world, and the winds that sweep across the Nordheim tundra are as exacting as the winds of the Morvind interior. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the human communities of Nordheim have long observed customs that resemble the observances of the high country, though the figure of the Aero is not invoked by the same name in every community of the far north. The practice of the watch, the reading of the wind before the spring thaw, and the maintenance of the shelters that protect against the gales are customs that the people of the far north and the people of the cold interior have refined in parallel rather than in common.

The chronicler has noted that the cold interior and the far north share a respect for the long storms and a recognition of the necessity of the wind to the renewal of the land. The Aero is honoured in both regions as the spirit of the wind that scours the snow from the slopes and that carries the warmth of the spring to the valleys. The figure is present in the customs of both regions in a manner that the chronicler has recorded as one of the more interesting examples of parallel development in the religions of the known world.

## The Lupines and the Hunt

The [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines) of the cold steppes are the principal non-dwarven adherents of the faith, and their observance is adapted to the life of the hunt rather than the life of the high pasture. The lupine communities are hunters of the high land, and the hunt is central to their way of life. The lupine elders observe the rite of the first kill each season, offering the first portion of the prey to the shrine of the peak that governs their territory. The faith holds that the land provides the game, and that a hunter who takes more than is needed or who wastes the flesh of the quarries offends the Aero.

The relationship between the lupine hunters and the dwarven high clans has been generally peaceful, and the chronicler records several instances in which the lupines have warned the clans of the approaches of large herds of game or of the movement of enemy parties. This mutual assistance has been formalised through customary agreements that govern the sharing of hunting grounds and the protection of the passes during the hunt season. The lupines do not observe the full rites of the northern peaks; they take only those portions of the faith that are relevant to the life of the hunter, and the dwarven clans accept this without objection. The Aero is honoured in the cooperation of the two peoples as the spirit of the wind that carries the news across the broken country.

## The Dwarven High Roads

The dwarven high clans are the principal builders of the passes and the bridges of the cold interior, and the road networks that connect the remote valleys to the principal halls of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) are largely the product of dwarven engineering. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the high roads are designed to withstand the gales of the upper slopes, and the engineers of the high clans have developed construction techniques that are adapted to the conditions of the wind. The retaining walls that support the roads are set deep into the rock, and the cuttings that carry the roads through the ridges are angled to deflect the prevailing winds away from the surface of the road.

The high roads are maintained by the clans and by the tolls levied upon the caravans that use them, and the tolls are considered a form of payment to the gods of the peaks for the safe passage they provide. The Aero is honoured in the maintenance of the high roads as the spirit of the wind that the roads must withstand, and the figure is present in the engineering of the roads as the spirit of the force that the engineers have learned to accommodate.

## The Greeting of the Gale

A distinctive observance of the Aero is the greeting of the gale, conducted by the highland communities when the first great storm of the autumn descends upon the high country. The elder of the community stands at the customary point of the valley and speaks the traditional words of acknowledgement, and the words are a formal recognition that the Aero has returned for the season and that the community places itself under his protection for the duration of the storm. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the greeting of the gale is observed with great solemnity in every highland community, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the storm season.

The greeting is not confined to the highland communities, for the lowland towns at the foot of the ranges maintain their own observances of the Aero, and the magistrates of the lowland towns mark the arrival of the first great storm with a brief acknowledgement at the customary shrine. The chronicler notes that the lowland observance is less elaborate than the highland observance, but the lowland communities acknowledge that the gales that descend from the peaks are the same gales that the highland communities endure, and the figure is honoured in both the highland and the lowland observances as the spirit of the wind that the communities must learn to live with.

## Significance

The Aero is the second of the greater powers of the cold interior, and his observance is the foundation of the stormwatching tradition of the [Snow Country](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/snow-country). The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of his adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the long storms, the formalisation of the watch and the high roads through religious obligation, and the integration of the dwarven engineering and the lupine hunting customs have all contributed to the continuity of the cold interior through the long centuries of the wind. The chronicler finds in the Aero one of the most necessary and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the watchkeepers and the herders who depend upon the high country for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the wind returning each year in its season. The figure endures, in the gales and at the watch fire, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the long storms of the cold interior.
