## The Living Presence of the Wood

The Forest Mother is the principal power of the [Forest Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/forest-way), the goddess in whom the ancient temperate woodlands of the known world are embodied. She is the living presence of the wood itself, the canopy that shades the forest floor, the streams that gather beneath the trees, and the small creatures whose daily labours shape the soil. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the temperate woodlands invoke her at the turning of the seasons and at the presentation of the young to the sapling, and her festival falls at the unfolding of the first leaves in spring. The figure is older than the first elven settlement in the forest, and the elven people have honoured her since before the first clearing was made beneath the canopy.

The Forest Mother is patient and enduring, and her observance is among the most intimate of the woodland year. Elven communities ask her for the preservation of the trees, for the protection of the breeding creatures of the forest, and for wisdom to manage the woodland without diminishing it. She is honoured by every act of forest husbandry that the elven people perform, and the enduring fertility of the ancient temperate forests is the clearest evidence of her favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Forest Mother is conceived as the encompassing presence of the wood. Her domain extends from the smallest grove of the forest margin to the greatest of the old growths of the central heartwood. She is the canopy that intercepts rain and returns it to the streams, the leaf litter that feeds the soil, the fallen log that shelters small creatures, and the seedling that rises in the gap an old tree has left. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by the tending of the trees and the management of the forest as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Forest Mother is the great tree, the ancient oak, elm, or ironwood that has stood for centuries and that elven communities have tended with care. The ancient trees are held to be the pillars of the forest and sometimes the dwelling of the Forest Mother herself, honoured at the turning of the seasons by the elven communities of the surrounding woodland.

## The Spirits of the Living Wood

Beneath the Forest Mother are the specific powers of individual trees and plants. The god of the oak, the goddess of the silver elm, the spirit of the ironwood -- each is recognised in the groves where that tree predominates. The spirits of the streams, mosses, and ground cover are localised to the particular hollows and ridges where they dwell, and their names are maintained by the elders in lists updated only reluctantly. A new name is added only after the spirit has been known across several generations.

The practice is conservative by design, for to name a power without having lived with its effects invites confusion of the young. Elders are charged with remembering these names and teaching them to the young, maintaining small books in which the names are recorded.

## The Rites of the Forest Year

The elven calendar follows the forest cycle rather than the agricultural year. The rite of the leafing marks the spring renewal and is the principal festival of the faith. Elders gather in the principal clearing of the woodland and sing the old songs that name the spirits of the forest in order, from the canopy trees down to the mosses. The rite of the flowering celebrates the spring ephemerals that carpet the forest floor before the canopy closes, a quieter observance attended mainly by women and children. The rite of the fruiting honours the mast -- acorns, beechnuts, and other tree seeds that fall in autumn and sustain the winter. The rite of the leaf-fall marks the return of the year's growth to the soil.

A distinctive practice is the presentation of the young to the sapling, performed when a child reaches the age of understanding. The eldest member of the community leads the child to a young tree and asks the Forest Mother to receive the child as a new growth of the wood. The child touches the bark and receives a small token -- a leaf pressed between flat stones or a fragment of bark from a fallen branch -- kept for life. This practice strengthens the emotional tie between the individual and the forest and is cited as one of the reasons elven communities show remarkable continuity within a single woodland across many generations.

## The Elven Communities and the Stewardship

The management of the forest is itself a form of devotion. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) do not clear land in the manner of human farmers, and they do not fell living trees for timber except in the most limited circumstances and with formal rites of thanks. They gather fallen wood for fuel and fallen branches for crafting, and they shape the growth of the forest through selective planting and careful pruning. The result is a woodland richer and more diverse than any purely natural forest. An elven-managed grove reveals a density of life difficult to match in untouched areas, for the elves understand that a healthy forest requires both continuity and change.

The elven approach to forestry has no precise equivalent among other peoples. Human foresters of the surrounding lowlands take timber by cutting and replanting; dwarven quarries remove stone in quantity. The elves take only what falls and shape what grows, a method demanding patience and a long view of the landscape. The faith teaches that the forest is a trust, not a possession, and the elves who hold to this teaching maintain their settlements in the wood for centuries without exhausting their surroundings.

## The Sylvan Court and the Forest Governance

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) is the principal governance body of the elven forest, regulating the woodland and mediating disputes between elves and surrounding communities. The court is not a kingdom but a council of elders governing through consensus and tradition, its authority based not on military power or territorial control but on the deep connection between the elves and the woodland they inhabit. The Forest Mother is honoured in the court as the spirit of the wood that the court governs.

The court does not maintain a standing army or fortifications; the forest itself is the defence of the elven people. The Sylvan Court is the oldest continuous political entity in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and its stability is largely attributable to the shared interest of elven and human communities in preserving the boundary.

## The Heartwood Fellowship

The [Heartwood Fellowship](/content/government/local/heartwood-fellowship) is the standing body of wardens, beaters, and trappers who tend the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) and the surrounding forests of southern [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). The Fellowship regulates the chase, keeps the bounds of the forest, and adjudicates disputes among villagers, charcoal-burners, and small lords holding land within or adjacent to the wood. The Foundation of the Fellowship is ascribed to an accord between the villages of the forest margin and the lords of the Sylvan Court more than three centuries ago.

The Forest Mother is honoured in the work of the Fellowship. The chief warden and sworn fellows maintain the bounds of the forest, regulate the chase, and adjudicate disputes among foresters and small lords. The Fellowship has been one of the quiet foundations of the prosperity of the forest margin.

## The Forest Edge and the Human Settlements

The boundary between elven forest and human lowland settlements is a zone of considerable interaction. Human woodcutters and farmers trade with elves for rare woods, medicinal herbs, and crafted goods, and elves permit humans to harvest fallen timber and gather surplus mast within agreed boundaries. The relationship is governed by a body of customary law balancing elven protection of the deep wood with human need for resources. Breaches -- cutting protected trees, overharvesting medicinal herbs -- have led to occasional conflict, but most disagreements are settled through formal negotiation between elders of the forest and local human authorities.

The elven approach is one of guarded coexistence, in which the faith's teachings are kept within the community but its practical benefits are shared with neighbouring settlements. Monotheistic missionaries who have visited elven communities have been received politely but have found little ground for argument; the elves do not deny the singular light the missionaries speak of but find it irrelevant to the life of the forest.

## The Ancient Trees and the Old Groves

The greatest expressions of the faith are the ancient trees and the old groves of the central heartwood. The ancient trees are the eldest of the canopy species, oaks, elms, and ironwoods that have stood for centuries and perhaps millennia, held to be the pillars of the forest and the dwelling of the Forest Mother herself. The old groves are untouched remnants of the original woodland, where the canopy has never been broken and the understory has developed according to its own law. These are the sacred centres of the faith, and elders of every elven community make pilgrimages to the principal old groves at the turning of the year.

No tree within the principal groves may be felled and no old growth disturbed, an agreement considered as fundamental as any law. The preservation of these areas has made the Aethelar Heartwood the most extensive and oldest continuous temperate forest in the known world. The Forest Mother is honoured in the preservation of the ancient trees and the old groves as the spirit of the wood that the preservation protects.

## Significance

The Forest Mother is the principal power of the deep wood, and her observance is the foundation of the [Forest Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/forest-way) and a defining element of the [elven](/content/species/sapient/elves) civilisation. The reverence for the breeding cycles of the forest creatures, the formalisation of sustainable stewardship through religious obligation, and the integration of the elven foresters' knowledge into the everyday practice of the woodland communities have contributed to the preservation of the ancient temperate forests. The Forest Mother is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the foresters who depend upon the wood for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the forest remaining healthy.
