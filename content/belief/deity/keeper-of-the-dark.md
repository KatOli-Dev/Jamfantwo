## Overview

The Dark Keeper is the second of the three greater powers in the [Stone Reading](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/stone-reading), the power who is honoured in the unlit workings where no lamp will burn and no torch can find the air to feed it. He is the deep stone, the place beneath the mountain, the gallery that no surface light has ever reached, and the long memory of the rock that endures when the lamps have failed and the sound of the picks has died away. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the holds of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and of the mountain spine of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) hold him to dwell within the deep places themselves, and the older songs of the stoneworkers say that the sound of a step in a gallery that no one has entered in living memory is the sound of the figure's slow turning toward the visitor. The chronicler of the mountain rites records that the Dark Keeper has been honoured since the first workings were driven below the level at which the air will feed a flame, and that his cult is the most particular of the three greater powers, for the deep places are not entered by every workman and the rites of the figure are observed only by those who have been taught to read the dark.

## The Figure and the Deep

The Dark Keeper is conceived as the living presence of the deep stone and the gallery no light has reached, and his domain extends from the lowest workings of the deepest holds to the sealed chambers that are not entered at all. He is the cool air that rises from a newly opened gallery, the absolute stillness of a chamber that has not been disturbed for generations, the dripping of water from a rock that the heat of the workings will never warm, and the long echo that returns when a hammer is struck in a passage of which the end is not in sight. The chronicler of the stoneworkers has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is honoured by the careful reading of the dark as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Dark Keeper is the unlit working itself, the gallery in which the lamps burn poorly or not at all and the workman must move solely by memory and by sound. The unlit working is not set apart from the rest of the mine, and the unlit working is not marked by any outward sign. The workman knows the unlit working by the failure of the lamp, and the workman enters the unlit working only when the work requires it. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by the workman who enters the dark with the customary words, and the words are spoken in a low voice so that the other workmen in the lit gallery may not hear. The words are the workman's private acknowledgement of the figure, and the words are spoken as a sign that the workman knows the figure to be present.

The sealed chamber is the second dwelling of the figure, and the sealed chamber is honoured with a particular respect that the chronicler has described as the deepest expression of the cult. A sealed chamber is a gallery that has been closed at one end by a fall of stone or by the deliberate walling-up of the entrance, and the sealed chamber is not entered. The workman does not break the seal, and the workman does not speak aloud in the presence of the seal. The workman passes the seal in silence, and the workman leaves a small token at the seal as an acknowledgement of the figure's presence within. The chronicler notes that the practice is uniform across the holds, and the practice is one of the most distinctive of the customs that mark the mountain workers as a people apart.

## The Unlit Workings

The unlit workings are the galleries in which the air will not feed a lamp, and the unlit workings are entered only by the workman who has been taught to read the dark. The teaching of the dark is one of the most demanding of the mountain disciplines, and the apprentice who is entrusted with the work in the unlit galleries is one who has spent years in the lit workings and has been observed to possess the steadiness and the memory that the work requires. The master does not teach the reading of the dark as a set of rules but as a way of attending to the place, and the apprentice learns by long practice in the company of the master. The chronicler records that the apprentice who enters the dark before he is ready is held to be a danger to himself and to the work, and the apprentice is returned to the lit workings until the master is satisfied that the apprentice is ready.

The workman who enters the unlit working carries no lamp, for the lamp will not burn, and the workman carries only the tools of the trade and the customary token of acknowledgement. The workman moves by the feel of the wall against the hand, by the sound of his own breath, by the memory of the gallery that he has walked before, and by the small sounds that the figure is held to make when the workman has wandered too far from the safe path. The chronicler records that the small sounds are not the sounds of a living voice but the sounds that the gallery itself makes: the dripping of a distant spring, the settling of a stone that has been loosened by the working, the rustle of air through a crack that the workman has not yet discovered. The sounds are read by the workman as the figure's guidance, and the workman returns to the lit working by the path that the sounds have indicated.

The chronicler notes that the workman who is found to have read the dark aright is held in particular respect by the master and by the other workmen of the hold, and the workman's name is recorded in the hall book of the gallery as a sign of the figure's favour. The workman who is found to have read the dark amiss is returned to the lit workings and is not entrusted with the unlit work again until the master is satisfied that the figure has forgiven the failure. The chronicler records that the practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that the figure does not easily forgive the workman who has presumed upon the dark.

## The Lost and the Returned

The chronicles of every hold record the occasions upon which a workman has been lost in the deep galleries and has been returned to the light by the figure's intervention. The chronicler of the stoneworkers has collected these accounts with care, and the accounts are preserved in the hall books of the principal holds as a testimony to the figure's continued presence in the deep places. The accounts agree in their general form, though they differ in their particulars, and the agreement is held by the chronicler to be the strongest evidence that the figure is real and that the figure acts within the deep stone.

The typical account describes a workman who has wandered beyond the customary bounds of a familiar gallery and has found himself in a passage that he does not recognise. The workman calls out, and the call is not answered, for the lit workings are too far away for the voice to carry. The workman waits, and the figure makes itself known by a small sound that the workman can follow. The workman follows the sound, and the sound leads the workman to a passage that he does recognise, and the workman returns to the lit workings by the path that the figure has indicated. The chronicler notes that the workman who is returned in this manner speaks of the experience with a sobriety that the chronicler has described as one of the most striking features of the accounts, and the workman does not claim to have seen the figure or to have heard a voice. The workman claims only to have been guided, and the guidance is held to be the figure's work.

The chronicler observes that the figure is not invoked in the manner of a god who can be called upon to act at the request of the petitioner, and the figure is honoured rather as a presence that may be acknowledged and that may, in its own time, make itself known. The teaching holds that the figure's guidance is not to be commanded, and the figure's guidance is given only to those who have entered the dark with the proper preparation and the proper acknowledgement. The workman who is returned to the light does not thank the figure in the manner of a prayer, and the workman leaves a token at the place where the figure made itself known. The token is a small piece of worked metal, and the token is the workman's acknowledgement of the figure's favour.

## The Long Memory

The Dark Keeper is also the power of the long memory, and the long memory is the foundation of the mountain tradition of careful record. The chronicler records that the figure is held to remember every gallery that has been opened in the rock and every chamber that has been sealed, and the figure's memory is the model for the memory of the workman. The hall books of the principal holds are kept with a care that the chronicler of the mountain rites has described as the most meticulous of any archives in the known world, and the care is the foundation of the tradition of the unlit workings. A workman who enters a gallery that has not been entered for generations is held to be entering the figure's memory, and the workman leaves a record of the entry in the hall book as an addition to the figure's memory.

The practice of the long memory is observed most strictly in the deepest holds, where the galleries have been driven through stone that the surface communities have not seen in living memory. The hall books of the deepest holds contain entries that the chronicler has dated to the founding of the holds, and the entries are the foundation of the tradition that the figure's memory is unbroken. The chronicler notes that the practice of the long memory is one of the foundations of the mountain discipline, and the practice distinguishes the mountain stoneworkers from the miners of the lowlands, whose galleries are shorter and whose records are less carefully kept.

## Relation to the Other Powers

The Dark Keeper is the second of the three greater powers, and his position is fixed by the order of the work, for the dark is the second of the mountain places after the hearth. The [Smith God](/content/belief/deity/smith-god) is honoured at the forge and the anvil, and the miner-god is honoured at the first strike into virgin rock. The Dark Keeper is honoured in the galleries that the light does not reach, and the galleries are the places where the work of the miner is finished and the silence of the deep stone begins. The three figures are not in competition, and the three are not ranked by their importance. The three are honoured in the order of the place, and the order is fixed by the labour of the hold.

The chronicler records that the Dark Keeper is sometimes invoked together with the miner-god, and the invocation is the customary form at the first opening of a gallery that the workman suspects will be unlit. The miner-god is thanked for the ore that the gallery will yield, and the Dark Keeper is asked to receive the workman who will labour in the dark. The [Smith God](/content/belief/deity/smith-god) is invoked in a different context, and the invocation belongs to the forge and the smelting of the ore that the dark has yielded. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in their proper places, and the figure of the Dark Keeper is the dwelling of the unlit gallery and the sealed chamber.

## Observance Among the Holds

The observance of the Dark Keeper is most particular in the deepest holds of the mountain ranges, and the observance is least particular in the shallower workings of the surface communities. The principal holds of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and of the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) maintain the full discipline of the cult, and the discipline is observed with the same care in both realms. The chronicler records that the two realms have exchanged traditions of the figure's honour, and the exchange has contributed to the unity of the cult across the wide range of the mountain settlements.

The hall books of the principal holds are the principal archives of the cult, and the hall books contain the records of the unlit workings, the accounts of the lost and the returned, and the tokens that have been left at the seals. The hall books are kept by the elder masons of the holds, and the elder masons are the custodians of the figure's honour. The chronicler notes that the elder masons are the foundation of the continuity of the cult, and the elder masons teach the apprentices the reading of the dark in the customary manner. The teaching is conducted in the unlit workings themselves, and the apprentice learns by long practice in the company of the elder mason. The chronicler records that the practice is conservative by design, and the practice is one of the foundations of the unity of the cult across the holds.

The human shepherds of the upper slopes have adopted a fragment of the observance, particularly the custom of leaving a token at the entrance of a cave that they do not enter, and the custom is conducted with a short word of acknowledgement that the chronicler of the mountain rites has recorded as a borrowing from the dwarven custom. The borrowing is acknowledged by both peoples, and the borrowing is not regarded as a conversion. The elven foresters of the upper forest line have adopted a similar fragment, particularly the custom of leaving a token at the mouth of a hollow tree, and the custom is held by the elves to be a kindred observance. The chronicler notes that the figure is honoured in the borrowing as the power of the dark, and the figure is not honoured as a god of the dwarven halls. The distinction is observed with care, and the distinction is one of the foundations of the peaceful coexistence of the three peoples upon the mountain.

## Significance

The Dark Keeper is the second of the three greater powers of the [Stone Reading](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/stone-reading), and his observance is the foundation of the deep workings that have distinguished the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the mountain spine for centuries beyond reckoning. The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of his adherents that contradicts their daily labour in the deep places, and the figure offers nothing that undermines the work of the unlit galleries. The reverence for the sealed chamber, the formalisation of the reading of the dark as a religious discipline, and the integration of the figure's long memory into the records of the hall books have all contributed to the unity of the deep workers across the wide range of their settlements. The chronicler finds in the Dark Keeper one of the most patient and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady attention of the workmen who labour in the deep stone and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the figure's continued presence in the galleries where no surface light has ever reached. The figure endures, in the dark and at the seal, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the deep stone.
