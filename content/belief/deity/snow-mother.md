## The Goddess of the Snow and the Long Winter

The Snow-Mother is the principal power of the [Old Gods of the Northern Peaks](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-northern-peaks), the goddess in whom the snow that blankets the cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and the high country of the great mountain ranges is embodied. She is the first snowfall of the autumn that closes the high passes, the deep snow that buries the meadows of the high pasture, the ice that bridges the chasms between the peaks, and the slow melt of the spring that releases the rivers down the valleys. The dwarven high clans and the human communities of the snow-bound valleys invoke her at the first snowfall and at the last melting of the winter, and her festival falls at the midwinter, the darkest time of the year, when the people of the high interior most need the reassurance that the snow will not last forever. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the figure is older than the first high clan and the first refuge hall, and that the peoples of the cold interior have honoured her since before the great halls of the mountains were carved from the living rock.

The figure of the Snow-Mother is austere and exacting, and her observance is among the most earnest of the cold interior year. The highland communities do not ask her for favours in the manner of the more elaborate faiths; they ask her for a snow that lies deep enough to protect the winter stores and shallow enough to release the rivers in the spring, for the survival of the flocks through the long cold, and for the wisdom to read the signs of the approaching storms. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of highland husbandry that the cold interior communities perform, and that the continuity of the high country through the long winter is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and Her Domain

The Snow-Mother is conceived as the encompassing presence of the snow and the ice, and her domain extends from the highest crests of the cold interior ranges to the snow-bound valleys where the human communities of the high country maintain their settlements. She is the snow that falls in the early autumn and lies upon the meadows until the late spring, the ice that seals the lakes and the streams through the long months of the cold, the frost that cracks the rock of the highest peaks, and the slow melt that sends the rivers down to the valleys when the sun returns. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the snow and the management of the high pasture as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Snow-Mother is the high peak, the summit that rises above the line of perpetual snow and that the highland communities regard as the most direct dwelling of the divine. The sacred peaks are the most prominent of the summits of the cold interior, and the pilgrimages to the lesser of the sacred peaks are the most arduous of the observances of the high country. The Snow-Mother is present in the high peaks as the spirit of the snow that they hold, and her presence is renewed each year by the first snowfall of the autumn.

## The Greater Powers of the Ice

Beneath the Snow-Mother are set the other greater powers of the cold interior. The [Wind-Walker](/content/belief/deity/wind-walker) is the second, the god of the high winds and the great storms that descend from the peaks and sweep across the valleys. He is honoured at the height of the storm season, when the watchkeepers of the high country sound the alarms and the communities secure their shelter. The [Long-Night](/content/belief/deity/long-night) is the third, the power of the polar darkness that holds the interior in unbroken night for the weeks of deep winter. The Long-Night is not feared but respected, for the faith teaches that the darkness is necessary to the renewal of the land and that the people who endure it with patience will be rewarded when the light returns.

Beneath these three are set the gods of the individual peaks, glaciers, and valleys, and the catalogue of these local powers is long. The dwarven clans maintain lists of the gods of the principal summits of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) and the surrounding ranges, recording them alongside the names of the passes and the water sources that lie beneath each peak. A new name is added only when a summit has been known across several generations and the community can attest to its consistency, and the practice ensures that the local gods are those that the people have actually lived with rather than abstract inventions. The Snow-Mother is honoured in the catalogue as the spirit of the snow that the catalogue preserves.

## The Rites of the Long Winter

The religious year of the northern peaks is dominated by the winter, which occupies the greater part of the year in these latitudes. The observance of the winter is bound up with the custom of the long-night gathering, held at the principal hall of the high clan at the midwinter. The gathering is a multi-day event during which the stories of the past year are told, the deeds of the watch are recounted, and the young are taught the old songs that name the spirits of the peaks in order. The gathering is also the occasion for the settlement of disputes between the clans and for the formalisation of the seasonal movements of the flocks. The Snow-Mother is honoured in the gathering as the spirit of the long winter that the gathering endures.

The brief summer occupies only a fraction of the year, and the rites of the summer are correspondingly simple. The rite of the first lambing marks the beginning of the high pastures, and it is conducted at the customary shrine of the valley where the flocks are brought to graze. The rite of the last snow falls at the end of the summer, when the herds are brought down from the high meadows and the watchkeepers prepare the passes for the return of the winter. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the two rites of snow - the first and the last - form the pivot of the religious year, and their observance with strict regularity is held to be the foundation of the community's continuity through the cold months.

## The Frostwall Clans and the Watch

The [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) are the principal political and military entity of the cold interior, and the clans are inseparable from the faith. The watch is the central duty of the clans, and the watchkeepers of the high country are charged with the observation of signs in the sky and the snow that warn of approaching storms, of the movement of enemy parties, and of the conditions of the passes. The watch is conducted from the high points of the escarpment, and the signal fires that the clans maintain are the chief means of communication across the broken country. The [Frostwall Fire Signs](/content/language/pseudo/frostwall-fire-signs) are the code by which the watchkeepers convey information, and the code is observed with great strictness; the failure to maintain the signals at the customary hours is regarded not merely as a neglect of duty but as a breach of religious obligation, for the watch is the service of the community to the gods of the peaks.

The watchkeepers are drawn from the warrior class of the clans, but their role is more than martial. They are also tasked with the reading of the weather, and their knowledge of the sky and the patterns of the storm is considered sacred. The chronicler records that the stormwatchers of the high country have developed a highly refined understanding of meteorological phenomenon, and that their predictions are often relied upon by the lowland communities of the coasts, who have no comparable means of foreseeing the gales that may descend from the interior. The Snow-Mother is honoured in the watch as the spirit of the snow that the watchkeepers observe.

## The High Pasture and the Flocks

The flocks of the high interior are the economic foundation of the clans and of the human communities of the snow valleys, and the care of the flocks is a central religious duty. The flocks are moved to the high pastures at the beginning of the summer and brought down again before the first snowfall of the autumn. The movement is governed by a body of customary law that determines the order of the clans, the timing of the crossing of the passes, and the distribution of the grazing lands. The customary law is administered by the elders of the clans, and disputes over the grazing rights are settled at the gathering of the clans, which is held each year at the principal shrine of the high country.

The faith teaches that the high pasture is the inheritance of the clans and the snow-valley communities in common, and that no single clan or household has the right to monopolise it. The chronicler notes that this principle has prevented the kind of territorial conflicts that have destroyed other pastoral communities, and that the steady succession of herds across the high meadows has maintained the fertility of the pastures for centuries. The rite of the first lambing is the chief observance connected with the flocks, and the lambing is blessed by the elder who leads the procession to the high pasture. The first wool of the season and the first milk are offered at the shrine, and the offering is held to ensure the fertility of the herd for the coming year.

## The Dwarven Dimension

The dwarven high clans are the principal adherents of the Old Gods of the Northern Peaks, and their observance of the faith is closely tied to the [Old Gods of the Mountains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-mountains), the dwarven reverence for the stone. The two faiths are not contradictory; the gods of the ice are the powers of the surface of the cold interior, and the gods of the stone are the powers of the rock beneath the ice. The dwarven communities honour both sets of powers, and the chronicler records that the dual observance is considered the mark of the complete dwarf. The gods of the ice and the gods of the stone are held to be kin, for both are powers of the mountain, one above ground and one below, and the dwarven clans maintain that a community that honours only one cannot fully understand the nature of the mountain.

The dwarven contribution to the high country extends beyond religious practice. The dwarven high clans are the principal builders of the passes and the bridges of the cold interior, and the road networks that connect the remote valleys to the principal halls of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) are largely the product of dwarven engineering. The chronicler notes that the maintenance of these roads is funded jointly by the clans and by the tolls levied upon the caravans that use them, and that the tolls are considered a form of payment to the gods of the peaks for the safe passage they provide.

## The North and the Far Cold

The cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) is not the only place where the figure of the Snow-Mother is honoured, for the cold countries of the far north acknowledge the snow as a power of comparable force and austerity. The continent of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) lies beyond the principal seas of the known world, and the long winters of the Nordheim tundra are as exacting as the winters of the Morvind interior. The chronicler of the cold interior records has set down that the human communities of Nordheim have long observed customs that resemble the observances of the high country, though the figure of the Snow-Mother is not invoked by the same name in every community of the far north. The practice of the long-night gathering, the reading of the snow before the spring thaw, and the watch upon the conditions of the high country are customs that the people of the far north and the people of the cold interior have refined in parallel rather than in common.

The chronicler has noted that the cold interior and the far north share a respect for the long winter and a recognition of the necessity of the snow to the renewal of the land. The Snow-Mother is honoured in both regions as the spirit of the snow that sustains the winter, and the figure is present in the customs of both regions in a manner that the chronicler has recorded as one of the more interesting examples of parallel development in the religions of the known world.

## The Sacred Peaks and the Forbidden Places

The highest summits of the cold interior are considered the dwelling of the gods in their most immediate form, and the principal of these peaks are sacred centres of pilgrimage. The pilgrimage to the summit of a sacred peak is arduous and dangerous even in good weather, and the chronicler records that several pilgrims have lost their lives attempting the ascent. The pilgrimage is held to be a test of devotion, and those who complete it are regarded by the community as having received the blessing of the gods. The forbidden places are the highest peaks, those where the gods are held to dwell in their most terrible form, and these are not visited at all. The chronicler notes that the distinction between the sacred and the forbidden peaks is maintained through longstanding oral tradition, and that the boundary is respected even by those clans that do not make pilgrimages to the lesser summits.

The Snow-Mother is honoured in the pilgrimage as the spirit of the snow that the pilgrim crosses, and the figure is present in the summit as the dwelling of the divine. The chronicler records that the pilgrimage is one of the most demanding of the observances of the high country, and the pilgrimage is reserved for those whose devotion has been tested in the long winter.

## Significance

The Snow-Mother is the principal power of the snow and the long winter, and her observance is the foundation of the [Old Gods of the Northern Peaks](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-northern-peaks) and the spiritual inheritance of the cold interior communities. The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the long winter, the formalisation of the watch and the high pasture through religious obligation, and the integration of the dwarven stone-reverence and the human pastoral customs have all contributed to the continuity of the cold interior through the long centuries. The chronicler finds in the Snow-Mother one of the most austere and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the watchkeepers and the herders who depend upon the snow for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the snow returning each year in its season. The figure endures, in the snow and at the watch fire, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the long winter of the high country.
