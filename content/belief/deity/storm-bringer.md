## Overview

Storm-Bringer is the third of the greater powers in the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), the divine of the long gales that sweep in from the open [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) and break against the cliff towns of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) of western [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Storm-Bringer is the heavy weather that builds in the west, the dark line of the cloud upon the horizon, the long roll of the thunder across the water, and the bright sheets of the rain that fall upon the cliffs in the hours before the gale. The fishers, the whalers, and the cliff communities of the western coast invoke Storm-Bringer with a mixture of reverence and caution, for the storms are necessary to the fertility of the sea but dangerous to the boats and the nets of the fishers who depend upon the cold water. The chronicler of the western records has set down that the figure is older than the first cliff town and the first whaling camp, and that the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters have honoured the figure since before the first human boat was launched from the western headlands.

The figure of Storm-Bringer is austere and unpredictable, and the rites that attend the figure are marked by the same watchfulness that marks the fisher's reading of the weather. The figure is invoked at the first sign of the building of the storm, at the height of the gale when the boats are safe in harbour, and at the passing of the weather when the boats are put to sea again. The cliff communities do not ask Storm-Bringer for favours in the manner of the more elaborate faiths; they ask the figure for warning of the approaching storm, for the strength to ride out the gale in harbour, and for the wisdom to read the signs of the weather. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of seamanship that the western communities perform, and that the figure's blessing upon the sea is held to be the source of the fertility that the fishers draw from the cold water.

## The Figure and Her Domain

Storm-Bringer is conceived as the living presence of the long gales that move across the cold water of the west, and the figure's domain extends from the first line of the building cloud upon the western horizon to the last squall that breaks against the cliffs in the hours after the gale has passed. She is the darkening of the sky in the west, the rising of the long swell before the wind arrives, the hammering of the rain upon the cliffs and the boats, and the long clearing that follows the passing of the weather. The chronicler of the western records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the reading of the weather and the management of the boats as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of Storm-Bringer is the open water of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), the cold expanse to the west of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) across which the gales build before they reach the coast. The figure is invoked at the customary headland shrines of the cliff towns, the marked places at the highest points of the headlands where the western communities have long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrines are not buildings in the formal sense; they are marked spaces upon the natural rock, and the tending of the shrines is a responsibility shared among the households of the cliff communities. Storm-Bringer is present in the shrines as the spirit of the storm that the headlands acknowledge, and her presence is renewed each year by the first gale of the autumn and the last gale of the spring.

The figure is also invoked in the watch upon the headlands, which the watchkeepers of the cliff communities maintain through the storm season and beyond. The watchkeepers are charged with observing the conditions of the weather and the sea, and the watch upon the headlands is a form of devotion that is practised by every household of the cliff towns. The chronicler records that the watchkeepers of the western coast have developed a refined understanding of the gales of the Greyring, and that their predictions are relied upon by every fisher and every whaler who operates in the region. Storm-Bringer is present in the watch as the spirit of the storm that the watchkeepers observe, and the watchkeepers who have served through the worst of the gales are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of the figure's care for the cold water.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of Storm-Bringer falls at the height of the storm season, in the late autumn when the gales are at their most frequent and the fisherfolk are most dependent upon the figure's protection. The festival is the principal observance of the figure among the cliff communities, and the rites are conducted at the principal headland shrine of each cliff town by the elder of the community. The elder stands at the highest point of the headland and speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, asking Storm-Bringer to grant warning of the approaching storms and to keep the boats safe in harbour through the worst of the weather. The chronicler records that the festival is observed with great solemnity in every cliff town from the northern headlands to the southern estuaries, and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the western year. Storm-Bringer's day at the height of the storm season is the most universal of the observances of the cold water.

The rite of the first gale is conducted at the first named gale of the autumn, when the elder of the community stands at the headland shrine and speaks the customary words of acknowledgement, asking Storm-Bringer to grant that the boats be safe in harbour and that the nets be stored against the coming weather. The rite of the clearing follows the last named gale of the spring, when the elder speaks the traditional words of thanksgiving for the passing of the storm season and the opening of the calmer waters. The chronicler notes that the two rites of the gale - the first and the last - form the pivot of the western year, and the observance of the rites with strict regularity is held to be the foundation of the continuity of the cliff communities through the storm season.

A distinctive observance of Storm-Bringer is the rite of the warning, conducted by the watchkeepers of the cliff towns at the first sign of the building of a storm. The watchkeepers light the warning fires upon the headlands, and the fires are the signal for the boats to make for the nearest harbour. The chronicler records that the lighting of the warning fires is accompanied by a brief invocation of Storm-Bringer, and the invocation is a formal acknowledgement that the storm is approaching and that the community places itself under the figure's protection for the duration of the gale. The chronicler notes that the watchkeepers who have lit the warning fires through the worst of the storms are honoured by their communities as the human instruments of the figure's care for the cold water.

## The Order of the Greater Powers

Storm-Bringer is the third of the greater powers of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), and the figure's position in the order of the greater powers is the testing of the western year. The [Grey Mother](/content/belief/deity/grey-mother) is the first of the greater powers, the goddess of the cold western waters, the fog, and the long swell. The Whale-Walker is the second, the elder of the sea and the spirit of the great whales that migrate through the cold water. Storm-Bringer is the third, the power of the long gales that sweep in from the open ocean. The three are acknowledged collectively at the headland shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each also has her own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the western year, and the figure of Storm-Bringer is honoured last because the storms are the last of the three gifts that the gods bestow upon the cliff communities before the spring.

The three greater powers are understood to be a single order of divine care for the cold water, and the figures are invoked together at the customary headland shrines of the cliff towns. The Grey Mother is the encompassing presence of the grey sea, the Whale-Walker is the elder of the great whales, and Storm-Bringer is the long gales that sweep the waters. The chronicler notes that the three figures are honoured in a fixed sequence at the headland shrines, and the sequence is observed with great strictness in every cliff town. The figure of Storm-Bringer is present in the sequence as the spirit of the storm that the gathering acknowledges, and her presence is the foundation of the unity of the observance.

Beneath the three greater powers are set the spirits of the particular rocks and headlands of the western coast, a catalogue that is long and localised. The communities of each cliff town maintain the names of the spirits of the nearby headlands and rocks in small books kept by the elder, and the names are updated only when a spirit has been known across several generations. Storm-Bringer is present in the local spirits as the spirit of the gales that strike the particular headlands, and her presence is renewed each year by the first gale of the autumn and the last gale of the spring. The chronicler records that the local catalogue is held in particular esteem by the cliff communities, and the local spirits are honoured at the headland shrines alongside the greater powers.

## The Storm and the Fertility of the Sea

Storm-Bringer is honoured not only in the danger of the gales but also in the recognition that the storms are necessary to the fertility of the cold water. The chronicler of the western records has set down that the gales of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) bring the upwelling of the cold nutrient-laden water from the deep, and the upwelling is the foundation of the abundance of the fish and the whales that the western communities depend upon. The figure is invoked at the first gale of the autumn with a word of welcome as well as a word of caution, and the word of welcome is the formal acknowledgement that the storm is the giver of the fertility of the sea. The chronicler records that the fishers of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) who have observed this dual observance have maintained richer fisheries over generations than those who have regarded the storms as a threat alone.

The figure is also honoured in the long calm that follows the passing of the gale, when the sea is at its most bountiful and the boats can put to sea in safety. The chronicler records that the fishers set out upon the clearing with a customary word of thanks to Storm-Bringer, and the word is the formal acknowledgement that the figure's passing has released the bounty of the sea. The figure is present in the clearing as the spirit of the storm that has withdrawn, and the abundance of the catch in the days that follow the gale is the clearest evidence of the figure's care for the cold water. The chronicler notes that the dual observance of the figure - as bringer of danger and as bringer of fertility - is the foundation of the quiet strength of the western communities.

The chronicler observes that this dual understanding of the storm is one of the most distinctive features of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), and that the faith's teaching that the storm is both necessary and dangerous has done much to maintain the balance of the western fishery. The fishers who fear the storm alone stay in harbour and miss the catch. The fishers who welcome the storm alone put to sea in weather that destroys their boats. The fishers who honour Storm-Bringer in both aspects read the weather with the attention that the sea requires, and the prosperity of the cliff communities is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Merfolk and the Deep Water

The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold waters are the principal non-human adherents of Storm-Bringer, and their observance is adapted to the life of the deep sea rather than the cliff town. The merfolk communities are dwellers of the cold water itself, and their understanding of the gales of the Greyring is deep. The merfolk observe Storm-Bringer at the customary seasons, and they maintain the traditional cautions that govern the conduct of the boats in the storm season. The chronicler records that the merfolk have acted as the primary intermediaries between the cliff communities and the weather of the deep water in recent centuries, warning the fishers of the approaching gales and advising them on the conditions of the sea.

The relationship between the merfolk and the human cliff communities is one of practical reciprocity, and the warning of the storms is one of the most valuable of the courtesies that the merfolk extend. The humans provide the boats, the nets, and the technology of the fishery, while the merfolk provide the knowledge of the weather and the deep water. The exchange is formalised through customary agreements that govern the fishing grounds and the conduct of the boats in the storm season, and the chronicler records that the system has generally worked well, with occasional friction but no sustained conflict. Storm-Bringer is honoured in the cooperation as the spirit of the storm whose passage the two peoples share, and the chronicler notes that the cooperation has done much to maintain the unity of the observance across the wide extent of the cold water.

The merfolk observance of Storm-Bringer takes a form that the cliff communities do not always witness. The merfolk gather at the offshore rocks during the height of the gales, and the chronicler records that the merfolk singing can sometimes be heard from the cliffs when the sea is calm and the wind is in the right quarter. The merfolk do not build shrines in the manner of the cliff towns, for the sea itself is their temple, and the figure of Storm-Bringer is invoked by the merfolk in the waters where the gales are most fully felt. The chronicler notes that the merfolk understanding of the figure is older than the human understanding, and that the human faith has borrowed many of its cautions from the merfolk practice of the cold water.

## Significance

Storm-Bringer is the third of the greater powers of the [Old Faith of the Western Deep](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-faith-of-the-western-deep), and the figure's observance is the foundation of the storm-season life of the cliff communities of the [Grey Coast](/content/location/settlement/region/grey-coast) and the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the cold water. The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of her adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the gales that bring the upwelling of the deep water, the formalisation of the warning of the storms through religious obligation, and the integration of the merfolk knowledge into the everyday practice of the fishers have all contributed to the prosperity of the western coast. The chronicler finds in Storm-Bringer one of the most austere and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the fishers and the watchkeepers who depend upon the cold water for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the gales remaining true to their season. The figure endures, in the headland shrine and at the lighting of the warning fires, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the long gales of the western deep.
