## Overview

The Rain-Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the power of the rain and the dew that sustains the seed through the early months of growth. He is the gentle rain that quickens the germ, the heavy shower that swells the rivers, the morning dew that lies upon the leaves, and the evening mist that descends upon the fields at the close of the warm day. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke him at the sowing and through the early growing season, and his particular day of observance is the onset of the wet season, when the first rains return to the dry land. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the figure is older than the first irrigation channel, and that his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of every community whose crops depend upon the seasonal rain.

The figure of the Rain-Bringer is gentle and necessary, and his observance is among the most earnest of the agricultural year. The figure is invoked at every sowing, at every blessing of the crop, and at every drought, and the rites that are conducted in his honour are marked by a directness that the chronicler of the field customs has described as the most necessary piety of the agricultural calendar. The farming communities do not ask him for blessings in the abstract; they ask him for gentle rain, for the watering of the seed, and for the wisdom to read the signs of the approaching weather. The chronicler records that the figure is honoured by every act of cultivation, and that the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of the figure's favour.

## The Figure and His Domain

The Rain-Bringer is conceived as the living presence of the water that falls from the sky, and his domain extends from the highest clouds of the summer storm to the morning dew that lies upon the kitchen garden. He is the gentle rain that quickens the germ, the heavy shower that swells the rivers and fills the irrigation channels, the morning dew that lies upon the leaves of the young grain, and the evening mist that descends upon the fields at the close of the warm day. The chronicler of the field records has set down that the figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, and that the figure is invoked by the observation of the clouds and the reading of the weather as much as by any formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Rain-Bringer is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community has long gathered to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrine is not a building in the formal sense; it is a marked place, usually a standing stone or an ancient tree or a spring, and the tending of the shrine is a responsibility shared among the households of the village. The Rain-Bringer is invoked second at the shrine, after the [Sun-Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) and before the [Earth-Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother), and his invocation is the formal acknowledgement that the seed requires water as well as light and soil. The chronicler records that the order is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the figure of the Rain-Bringer is honoured second because the rain is the second of the gifts that the gods bestow.

The wet season of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) is the most visible sign of the Rain-Bringer's favour, and the farming communities of the dry regions mark the season with a festival that the chronicler of the rural records has described as the most fervent of the agricultural year. The onset of the wet season is the time when the first rains return to the dry land, and the farmers of every community gather at the village shrine to mark the moment with a customary prayer and a brief act of thanksgiving. The chronicler records that the prayers for rain at the height of the dry season are the most fervent of the agricultural calendar, and the village elder's request for gentle rain is the customary opening of the planting ritual.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Rain-Bringer falls at the onset of the wet season, when the first rains return to the dry land and the seed can be sown. The festival is the principal observance of the figure in the dry regions, and the rites are conducted at the village shrine by the elder of the community. The elder speaks the customary words of invocation, asking the Rain-Bringer to grant the gentle rain that the seed requires and to withhold the heavy storms that would damage the young crops. The chronicler records that the festival is observed with great solemnity in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and the occasion is one of the most important of the ceremonial events of the agricultural year. The Rain-Bringer's day at the onset of the wet season is the most earnest of the observances of the agricultural calendar.

The rite of the first sowing is the principal observance of the Rain-Bringer in the temperate regions, and the rite is conducted by the village elder at the principal field of the community when the seed is cast into the furrow. The elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Rain-Bringer to water the seed and to grant the germination that the crop requires. The rite of the green tide follows the first rains, when the young crops push above the soil and the plants require the steady moisture that the figure provides. The chronicler notes that the rite of the green tide is a quiet observance, attended by the women and the children of the village, and the focus is upon the blessing of the growing crop.

The rite of the gentle rain is a distinctive observance of the Rain-Bringer, conducted at the height of the dry season in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) when the crops are most vulnerable to the failure of the rain. The elder of the community leads the rite, and the community gathers at the village shrine to offer the customary prayer for gentle rain. The prayer is a formal acknowledgement that the figure has the power to grant or withhold the rain, and the community places itself under the figure's protection until the wet season returns. The chronicler records that the rite of the gentle rain is observed with great earnestness in the dry regions, and the figure is honoured in the prayer as the spirit of the rain that the crops require.

## The Order of the Wheel

The Rain-Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his position in the Wheel is the sustaining of the agricultural cycle. The Sun-Walker is the first, governing the light that ripens the crop, and the Earth-Mother is the third, governing the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each also has her or his own day of particular observance. The chronicler records that the order of the three is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the figure of the Rain-Bringer is honoured second because the rain is the second of the gifts that the gods bestow upon the cultivated land.

Beneath the three greater powers are counted the gods of the individual crops, the soil types, and the weather patterns that govern each particular region. The god of wheat, the goddess of the barley, the spirit of the lentil, the guardian of the dry soil, the warden of the wet - the catalogue is long and localised. The elders of the more particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or a soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The chronicler notes that the practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that to name a power without having lived with its effects is to invite neither favour nor misfortune but only the confusion of the young.

The Rain-Bringer is honoured in the relation of the three greater powers, for the figure is the rain that nourishes the seed, and the Sun-Walker and the Earth-Mother are the powers that open the cycle and receive the harvest. The chronicler records that the three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, and the three are understood to be a single order of divine care for the cultivated land. The Rain-Bringer is the second of the order, and his presence in the rain is the second of the gifts upon which the work of the Sun-Walker and the Earth-Mother is built. The figure is invoked at the customary shrine alongside the other two powers, and the three are understood to be inseparable in the work of the cultivation.

## The Dry Regions and the Rain's Necessity

The Rain-Bringer is honoured with a particular intensity in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), where the rain is scarce and matters greatly. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the dry regions depend upon the seasonal rain in a manner that the wetter regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) do not, and the farmers of the dry regions mark the onset of the wet season with a fervour that the chronicler has described as the most earnest of any agricultural observance. The Rain-Bringer is present in the dry regions as the spirit of the rain that the crops require, and the figure is invoked at every sowing, at every blessing, and at every drought with a directness that the chronicler has recorded as the most necessary piety of the dry country.

The dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) are the most vulnerable to the failure of the rain, and the chronicler of the inland records has noted that the droughts of the dry regions are the most severe in the known world. The farming communities of the dry regions have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the management of the limited water, and the Rain-Bringer is honoured in the management of the water as much as in any formal rite. The irrigation channels that carry the water from the springs to the fields are blessed at their opening by the elder of the community, and the blessing is a formal acknowledgement that the water is the gift of the Rain-Bringer, lent for the keeping of the community.

The Rain-Bringer is also honoured in the observation of the dew, which the farmers of the dry regions regard as the figure's quiet gift to the crops in the absence of the rain. The morning dew is the most reliable source of moisture in the dry regions, and the farmers have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the timing of the labour according to the presence or the absence of the dew. The chronicler notes that the observation of the dew is a form of devotion that is practised by every farmer of the dry regions, and the Rain-Bringer is honoured in the observation of the dew as the spirit of the water that falls without the storm. The figure is present in the daily labour of the fields in a manner that no other figure of the agricultural year quite matches in the dry regions.

## The Drought and the Rain's Withdrawal

The Rain-Bringer is honoured not only in the gift of the rain but also in the recognition of his withdrawal, for the drought is understood to be an expression of the figure's will. The chronicler of the rural records has set down that the drought is the time when the figure's gift is withheld, and the farmers of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) mark the season with a fast and a prayer that the chronicler has described as among the most austere of the agricultural year. The observance is a recognition that the figure has turned away, and the community places itself under the protection of the [Sun-Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) and the [Earth-Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother) until the rains return.

The drought is the most serious expression of the Rain-Bringer's withdrawal, and the chronicler has recorded that the droughts of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) are the most severe in the known world. The farming communities of the dry regions mark the drought with a fast and a prayer, and the elder of the community speaks the customary words of supplication, asking the Rain-Bringer to return and to grant the rain that the crops require. The chronicler notes that the prayers for rain at the height of the dry season are the most fervent of the agricultural calendar, and the village elder's request for gentle rain is the customary opening of the planting ritual.

The Rain-Bringer's withdrawal is also honoured in the observation of the winter, when the rain is scanty and the soil is dry. The winter is the season of the figure's rest in the wet regions, and the community acknowledges the rest by setting aside the customary tools of the sowing and by giving thanks for the figure's gift of the autumn rains. The Rain-Bringer is present in the winter as the spirit of the water that has withdrawn, and his withdrawal is the foundation of the winter rest that the soil requires. The figure is honoured in the recognition of the withdrawal as much as in the celebration of the gift, and the chronicler records that the figure's presence in the winter is as necessary to the agricultural year as his presence in the wet season.

## Significance

The Rain-Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Wheel of the Sun and Field](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of every community whose crops depend upon the seasonal rain. The figure endures because the figure asks nothing of his adherents that contradicts their daily survival, and the figure offers nothing that undermines it. The reverence for the seasonal rain, the formalisation of the management of the water through religious obligation, and the integration of the household observance into the everyday practice of the farming year have all contributed to the prosperity of the dry and the temperate communities alike. The chronicler finds in the Rain-Bringer one of the most necessary and least displaced of the figures of the known world, honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the rain for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the rain returning each year in its season. The figure endures, in the field and at the shrine, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the water that falls from the sky.
