## Overview

The Rain Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), the power of the rain and the dew that sustains the seed through the early months of growth. He is the gentle rain that quickens the germ, the heavy shower that swells the rivers, the morning dew that lies upon the leaves, and the evening mist that descends upon the fields at the close of the warm day. The farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) invoke him at the sowing and through the early growing season, and his particular day of observance is the onset of the wet season, when the first rains return to the dry land. The figure is older than the first irrigation channel, and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of every community whose crops depend upon the seasonal rain.

The Rain Bringer is gentle and necessary, and his observance is among the most earnest of the agricultural year. He is invoked at every sowing, at every blessing of the crop, and at every drought. Farming communities ask him for gentle rain, for the watering of the seed, and for wisdom to read the signs of approaching weather. He is honoured by every act of cultivation, and the prosperity of the farming lowlands is the clearest evidence of his favour.

## The Figure and His Domain

The Rain Bringer is conceived as the living presence of the water that falls from the sky. His domain extends from the highest clouds of the summer storm to the morning dew that lies upon the kitchen garden. He is the gentle rain that quickens the germ, the heavy shower that swells the rivers and fills irrigation channels, the morning dew upon the leaves of the young grain, and the evening mist that descends upon the fields. The figure is not a person in the human sense but a presence, invoked by observation of the clouds and reading of the weather as much as by formal rite.

The principal dwelling of the Rain Bringer is the village shrine, a marked place at the edge of the cultivated land where the community gathers for seasonal rites. The shrine is not a formal building but a marked place, usually a standing stone, a tree, or a spring. The Rain Bringer is invoked second at the shrine, after the [Sun Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) and before the [Earth Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother). The order is fixed by the agricultural cycle: the rain is the second of the gifts the gods bestow.

The wet season of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) is the most visible sign of the Rain Bringer's favour. The onset of the wet season is the time when the first rains return to the dry land, and farmers gather at the village shrine to mark the moment with a customary prayer and act of thanksgiving.

## Festival and Observances

The festival of the Rain Bringer falls at the onset of the wet season, when the first rains return to the dry land and the seed can be sown. The elder speaks the customary words asking the Rain Bringer to grant the gentle rain the seed requires and to withhold the heavy storms that would damage young crops. The festival is observed with great solemnity in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana).

The rite of the first sowing is the principal observance in temperate regions, conducted when the seed is cast into the furrow. The elder asks the Rain Bringer to water the seed and grant the germination the crop requires. The rite of the green tide follows the first rains, when young crops push above the soil and require steady moisture. This quiet observance is attended by women and children, focused on blessing the growing crop.

The rite of the gentle rain is a distinctive observance conducted at the height of the dry season in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) when the crops are most vulnerable. The community gathers at the village shrine to offer the customary prayer for gentle rain, acknowledging that the figure has the power to grant or withhold the rain.

## The Order of the Wheel

The Rain Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), his position being the sustaining of the agricultural cycle. The Sun Walker is the first, governing the light that ripens the crop. The Earth Mother is the third, governing the soil that receives the seed and the harvest that closes the cycle. The three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each has her or his own day of particular observance. The order is fixed by the agricultural cycle, and the Rain Bringer is honoured second because the rain is the second of the gifts the gods bestow.

Beneath the three greater powers are the gods of individual crops, soil types, and weather patterns. The catalogue is long and localised. Elders of particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine. New names are added only when a crop or soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The practice is conservative by design, for to name a power without having lived with its effects invites confusion.

The Rain Bringer is honoured in the relation of the three greater powers as the rain that nourishes the seed. The Sun Walker and the Earth Mother are the powers that open the cycle and receive the harvest. The three powers are invoked together at the village shrine at the turning of the seasons, understood as a single order of divine care for the cultivated land.

## The Dry Regions and the Rain's Necessity

The Rain Bringer is honoured with particular intensity in the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), where the rain is scarce and matters greatly. The dry regions depend upon seasonal rain in a manner the wetter regions of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) do not. Farmers of the dry regions mark the onset of the wet season with a fervour described as the most earnest of any agricultural observance.

The dry regions are the most vulnerable to the failure of rain. Farming communities have developed a body of customary knowledge governing the management of limited water. Irrigation channels carrying water from springs to fields are blessed at their opening by the elder, a formal acknowledgement that the water is the gift of the Rain Bringer.

The Rain Bringer is also honoured in the observation of the dew, which farmers of the dry regions regard as the figure's quiet gift to the crops in the absence of rain. The morning dew is the most reliable source of moisture in the dry regions, and farmers have developed customary knowledge governing the timing of labour according to the presence or absence of dew.

## The Drought and the Rain's Withdrawal

The Rain Bringer is honoured not only in the gift of rain but also in the recognition of his withdrawal, for the drought is understood as an expression of the figure's will. The drought is the time when the figure's gift is withheld, and farmers of the dry regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) mark the season with a fast and a prayer described as among the most austere of the agricultural year. The observance recognises that the figure has turned away, and the community places itself under the protection of the [Sun Walker](/content/belief/deity/sun-walker) and the [Earth Mother](/content/belief/deity/earth-mother) until the rains return.

Prayers for rain at the height of the dry season are the most fervent of the agricultural calendar. The village elder's request for gentle rain opens the planting ritual.

## Significance

The Rain Bringer is the second of the greater powers in the [Sun-and-Field Wheel](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/wheel-of-the-sun-and-field), and his observance is the foundation of the agricultural life of every community whose crops depend upon the seasonal rain. The reverence for the seasonal rain, the formalisation of the management of water through religious obligation, and the integration of the household observance into the everyday practice of the farming year have contributed to the prosperity of dry and temperate communities alike. The Rain Bringer is honoured not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon the rain for their livelihood, whose continuation depends upon the rain returning each year in its season.
