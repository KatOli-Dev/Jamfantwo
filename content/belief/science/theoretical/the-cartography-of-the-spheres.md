## Overview

The Cartography of the Spheres is the theoretical science concerned with the shape of the world, the disposition of its lands and seas, and the geometry by which the navigator fixes his place upon the open water. It is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, for it proceeds by reasoning upon the observed positions of the sun, the moon, and the fixed stars, and constructs from these a coherent account of the world's form that the physical disciplines may then employ. The cartographers of the spheres hold that the world is a sphere enveloped by the heavens, and that the three known continents and the great waters between them occupy determinate positions upon its surface that may be set down in measured relation one to another.

## Foundations in Observation

The science rests upon a long tradition of observation of the heavens, for the cartographers hold that the fixed stars, which appear to revolve about a single point in the northern sky, are the surest evidence of the world's roundness. The elevation of the pole star above the horizon is found to increase as the traveller moves northward and to decline as he moves south, a fact recorded by mariners upon every coast, and the cartographers reason from this that the surface of the world must curve beneath the feet of the observer. The chronicler notes that the practitioners of the [Study of Waters and Currents](/content/belief/science/physical/the-study-of-waters-and-currents) reject this reasoning, holding that the same measurements are explained as well by the bending of light across a flat surface, and that the cartographers' claims to a spherical world outrun the evidence of the senses. The two schools are thus divided, and their dispute is a long and unresolved one.

The scholars of the science further observe that vessels departing upon the open ocean presently disappear from the sight of those upon the shore from the hull upward, the mast remaining visible longest, a fact they hold to be inexplicable upon a flat world and decisive upon a round one. The disappearance of the sun and the moon below the horizon, and their return after a measured interval, is likewise adduced as proof of the world's turning or, as some hold, of the turning of the heavens about it, a question upon which the cartographers are not wholly agreed.

## The Mapping of the Known World

From these foundations the cartographers have constructed maps of the known world that set down the three continents in their relative positions, together with the oceans, seas, and straits that lie between. The continents are named in the maps as [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), the largest and most central, [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) to the north across the Stormfall Sea, and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) to the south across the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean). The great waters are likewise recorded: the warm Auric to the east of Aethelar, the cold [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) to its west, the stormful [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) between Aethelar and Morvind, and the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), the desert interior of Sahrana, crossed by the ancient caravan routes of the south.

The cartographers reckon the positions of these lands by the elevation of the pole star and by the observed duration of the longest and shortest days, and they express the relations of the lands upon a grid of lines that they term latitudes, measured north and south from a central band, and longitudes, measured east and west from a line of their own choosing. The precision of these maps is limited by the difficulty of measuring the passage of time at sea and by the imperfect knowledge of the lands beyond the travelled routes, yet the broad disposition of the known world is set down with a confidence that the mariners who depend upon it do not dispute.

## The Question of the Heavens

A matter of enduring debate among the cartographers of the spheres is the nature of the heavens themselves, and whether the world turns within them or the heavens turn about the world. The older teaching, preserved in the schools of the inland cities of Aethelar, holds that the earth stands fixed at the centre and that the sun, the moon, and the host of stars revolve about it in successive spheres, the motion of each accounting for the measured regularity of the days and the seasons. A newer school, advanced by certain scholars of the coastal observatories, contends that the world itself turns upon an axis, and that the apparent revolution of the heavens is a consequence of this turning, pointing to the behaviour of falling bodies and to the varying elevation of the pole star as it is observed from distant latitudes.

The chronicler observes that this dispute, though vigorous among the learned, has little effect upon the practice of navigation, for both schools arrive at the same measurements by different reasoning, and the mariner is content to use the tables that answer to his experience upon the water. The cartographers themselves are divided, and the question is reckoned among those that the science has not yet resolved, a frank admission that the theoretical sciences, unlike the physical, are apt to outrun the certainty of their evidence.

## Instruments and Method

The science is advanced by the use of the observing staff and the timed glass, instruments shared with the physical study of the waters, and by the careful keeping of logs in which the elevations of sun and star are recorded against the hour and the place of observation. The [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes), whose workshops produce the fine mechanisms valued by scholars and mariners alike, are counted among the principal makers of these instruments, and a precisely divided staff is accounted a treasure of any house of learning. The cartographers further employ the drawn chart, upon which the observed positions are plotted and the relations of the lands made visible, and the great charts of the known world, kept in the libraries of the principal cities, are among the most valued possessions of the realms that hold them.

The method of the science is to reason from the observed to the general, and to amend the general account where new observation contradicts it, a discipline the cartographers claim for themselves against the physical sciences, whose practitioners maintain that reasoning without the test of the hand is no science at all. The subject of the cartographers, being removed from the immediate test of the hand, admits of greater uncertainty, and their opponents are quick to point to the gaps in their charts as evidence of the weakness of their method. The chronicler notes that the cartographers are careful to distinguish what is measured from what is inferred, and that the maps they produce are revised as the bounds of the known world are extended by the voyages of the age.

## Centres of the Science

The cartography of the spheres is pursued in the libraries and observatories of the great cities of the known world, chief among them the scholarly establishments of the river valleys of Aethelar and the coastal observatories that look out upon the Auric and the Stormfall. The strait towns, where the learning of three continents meets, are noted for the exchange of charts and methods between the mapmakers of differing peoples, and the pilot guilds of the narrows maintain their own corps of cartographers whose work serves the practical needs of the passage. The [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), though scattered, contribute to the science through the wide experience of their navigators, who returned to the island chartmakers accounts of waters and shores remote from the northern continents.

The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) predominate among the practitioners of the science, as among the mariners who supply its observations, though individual [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) and [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) are reckoned among its students where their peoples have dealings with the sea and the sky. The science asks no allegiance of creed, and its practitioners are found among the faithful of every religion of the world, for the shape of the earth is held to be a matter of observation rather than of doctrine.

## Significance

The Cartography of the Spheres is accounted among the foremost of the theoretical sciences, for it provides the framework within which the practical arts of navigation and the physical study of the waters are made coherent and useful. By establishing the roundness of the world and the relative positions of its lands, it permits the mariner to venture upon the open ocean with confidence that the shore he seeks lies at a determinate place upon a determinate sphere, and it gives the chronicler and the ruler alike a unified picture of the world that trade and travel are forever enlarging. If its conclusions outrun its certainties in the matter of the heavens, that is the nature of the theoretical discipline, and the science is none the less esteemed for it. In an age of widening contact between the peoples of the three continents, the cartography of the spheres is the quiet intellect that maps the world they share, and its charts are among the enduring monuments of the age.
