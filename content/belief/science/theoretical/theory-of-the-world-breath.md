## Overview

The Theory of the World Breath is the theoretical doctrine that the air which surrounds the world is no idle envelope but a single living substance in perpetual motion, drawing breath from the warming of the lands by day and from their cooling by night, and circulating from the hot regions of the south to the cold regions of the north and back again. The school takes its name from this circulation, which it likens to the breath of a great creature, and it holds that the winds of the known world are the visible signs of this circulation, and that they may be understood and predicted once their underlying cause is known. The theory is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, for it proceeds by reasoning upon the observed winds and their relation to the sun's course and the disposition of the lands.

## The Original Doctrine

The theory arose among the astronomers and natural philosophers of the [Kingdom of Aldreth](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-aldreth), whose scholars, in comparing the records of the wind kept by the mariners of the [Velden Strait Run](/content/location/route/trade/velden-strait-run), observed that the winds of the strait shifted in a regular manner through the seasons. The winter winds, which blow from the north, give place in the spring to variable breezes, which in turn give place to the steady westerlies of summer, and these to the gales of autumn, and at last to the north winds again. The scholars of Aldreth, working under the patronage of the crown, proposed that the shifting of the winds was not the caprice of the air but the consequence of a regular circulation set up by the warming and cooling of the lands.

The doctrine, as set out in the principal treatise of the school, holds that the air above the warmer regions of the world is lifted by heat, and that cooler air from the colder regions flows in to take its place, producing a steady circulation from south to north in the upper air and from north to south in the lower. The winds that the mariner feels are, in this account, the lower portion of the circulation, and their direction is determined by the position of the sun and the disposition of the lands beneath them.

## The Behaviour of the Winds

The theory offers an explanation for several patterns of the winds that the mariners of the known world have long observed. The steady trade winds of the warm [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), which blow from the east in the latitudes of the southern [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) coasts, are explained as the lower portion of a great circulation set up by the warming of the air above the [Sahrani Coastal Strip](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sahrani-coastal-strip) and the cooled air above the waters to the south. The seasonal winds of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), which the elves have long used to time their planting and their harvest, are explained in the same way, as the local signs of a circulation that extends across the breadth of the world.

The school further holds that the variable winds of the temperate latitudes, where the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast) lies, are to be understood as the meeting of two circulations, the great southern circulation and the lesser northern circulation of the polar regions. The mariner who sails the Stormfall knows that the winds are uncertain, and the school explains the uncertainty as the consequence of the contest between two systems of breath.

## The Question of Storms

The handling of storms is the principal stronghold of the theory, for the great storms of the known world, the hurricanes of the Auric and the long gales of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), are among the most striking phenomena of the air. The school holds that storms are disturbances of the regular circulation, arising when the warmth of the sun is so great, or the cooling of the night so sudden, that the ordinary circulation is overwhelmed and the air rushes in to restore the balance. The storms of the Auric, which build in the afternoon heat and dissipate by morning, are explained as local disturbances of the lower circulation, and the long gales of the Greyring, which sweep in from the open ocean and last for days, are explained as disturbances of a wider reach, set up by the great difference of temperature between the warm waters of the south and the cold waters of the north.

The practitioners of the [Study of Waters and Currents](/content/belief/science/physical/study-of-waters-and-currents) accept the explanation in part, but they observe that the storms often arise without any clear antecedent change of temperature, and they hold that the theory of the world breath is incomplete in its account of these sudden events. The scholars of the theory reply that the antecedent change is always present, though it may not be perceived by the instruments at hand, and that the making of better instruments is one of the tasks to which the theory gives rise.

## Difficulties and Objections

The theory is not without its serious difficulties, and the more cautious of its adherents acknowledge them. The upper portion of the supposed circulation has never been directly observed, for no instrument has yet been raised to the height at which the returning breath is supposed to flow. The natural philosophers of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), who are accustomed to demand evidence for every assertion, observe that a circulation that depends upon an unobserved upper return is hardly better established than the older doctrines that the theory was advanced to replace.

The scholars of the theory reply that the upper return is inferred from the regularity of the lower winds, and that the regularity is itself evidence for the existence of the circulation. The reply is not conclusive, and the more experienced scholars of the school are careful to distinguish between what is observed and what is inferred, in the manner of all honest inquiry.

## Centres of the Theory

The theory is pursued chiefly in the libraries of the great cities of Aethelar, where the records of wind and weather are longest kept and most carefully preserved. The scholars of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) remain the foremost authorities of the school, and the academy of the kingdom of [Aldreth](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-aldreth) continues to instruct apprentices in the theory. The natural philosophers of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) have given the theory a sympathetic hearing, and the [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes), whose fine instruments are valued by every school of learning, have contributed treatises upon the measurement of the wind.

## Significance

The Theory of the World Breath is, in the judgment of the chronicler, among the more elegant of the theoretical doctrines, for it brings under a single principle a great variety of phenomena that had previously been studied separately. Whether or not the great circulation is real in the precise form that the school supposes, the theory has directed the attention of scholars to the regular patterns of the winds and to the relation between wind and sun, and it has produced a manner of speaking about the air that is now in common use among the learned. The doctrine will be remembered as one of the bolder constructions of the age, and as an example of what the theoretical sciences may accomplish when observation is patient and reasoning is bold.
