## Overview

Deep Stones is the theoretical doctrine that the visible stones of the world's surface are only a light crust over a succession of deeper, denser, and older stones whose nature must be inferred from fragments that occasionally reach the surface. The hypothesis holds that the deep places contain a graduated sequence of materials, each laid down in its proper order, and each preserving in its grain and composition a record of the conditions under which it was formed. The doctrine is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, proceeding by reasoning upon the observed behaviour of stones in deep mines and mountain faces rather than by direct inspection of the deep places themselves.

## The Original Observation

The hypothesis arose among the delvers of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), whose mines pierce the flank of the mountains to a depth unmatched in the known world. The delvers had long observed that the stone of the upper shafts differs from the stone of the deeper shafts, the deeper stone being as a rule harder, darker, and heavier. The thanes of the holds preserved the testimony of the delvers in the ledgers of the mines, and [Thrain Ironbrow](/content/people/historical/thrain-ironbrow) is said to have first proposed that the difference of stone marked a succession of deeper layers, each older than the one above.

The principal treatise of the school holds that the stones of the world's surface are the lightest and most recent of a series, and that beneath them lie heavier and older stones formed under conditions of greater heat and pressure than any that now obtain at the surface. The deepest stones, which no mine has yet reached, are supposed to be of a density and composition altogether unlike the familiar rocks of the fields.

## The Succession of the Layers

The philosophers of the school describe the succession of the layers as a graduated series, each differing from the one above by some marked change in composition or grain. The uppermost layer, termed the soil, is the loose and fertile matter in which the roots of plants are fixed, renewed year by year by the falling of leaves and the action of water. Beneath the soil lie the sandstones and clays of the lowlands, held to be the deposits of ancient seas. Beneath the sandstones lie the limestones, held to be the compressed remains of the creatures of those seas. Beneath the limestones lie the granites and other hard stones of the mountains, which the school holds to be of a different origin, formed by the cooling of molten matter rather than by the laying down of sediments.

Each layer preserves in its grain and fossils a record of the conditions under which it was formed, and the careful reading of a layer may reveal what the surface was like when that layer was laid down. The natural philosophers of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) have taken up this doctrine with enthusiasm, and the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) supports a small academy devoted to the reading of the stones.

## The Reading of the Stones

The school teaches that the stones may be read by the patient comparison of one layer with another and by the observation of the marks and fossils that each layer contains. The fossils, the petrified remains of creatures embedded in the stone, are of particular interest, for they reveal what manner of creatures lived when the layer was formed. The limestones of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), for example, contain the shells of sea creatures, and the school holds that the presence of these shells proves that the heartwood was once covered by the sea.

The reading of the stones is a difficult art, and the practitioners are careful to distinguish what is observed from what is inferred. A fossil embedded in a layer is observed; the conclusion that the layer was once beneath the sea is inferred. The school maintains that its inferences are well founded, pointing to the agreement of independent observers and the consistency of the inferences with one another.

## Difficulties and Objections

The hypothesis faces several serious difficulties. The deepest layers have never been directly observed, and their description is a matter of inference from the behaviour of the shallower layers. Practitioners of the physical sciences observe that a hypothesis depending upon what cannot be seen is hardly better established than the older doctrines it was advanced to replace.

The philosophers of the school reply that the deeper layers are inferred from the regularities observed in the shallower layers, and that these regularities are themselves evidence for the existence of the deeper layers. The reply is not conclusive, but they maintain that no other hypothesis accounts so well for the observed succession of the stones.

The cartographers of the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) observe that the hypothesis, if true, would require that the surface was once very different from what it is now, and they ask what force could have lifted the mountains and drained the seas from the lands. The philosophers reply that the lifting of the mountains is the work of the [Underlying Fire](/content/belief/science/theoretical/underlying-fire), and that the two doctrines are complementary rather than inconsistent.

## Reception Among the Learned

The hypothesis has been received with interest by the natural philosophers of the known world, and the academies of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) and [Vashari](/content/location/settlement/city/vashari) have given it a sympathetic hearing. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the deep holds are unanimous in their support, for the succession of the stones is the daily experience of their mines, and the delvers of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) and [Karhold](/content/location/settlement/city/karhold) have contributed many of the observations upon which the hypothesis rests. The [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) maintains a school of the deep stones in the libraries of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim), and the [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) of the workshops have devised instruments by which the density and grain of a stone may be measured with precision.

Certain philosophers have criticised the hypothesis on the grounds that the reading of the stones is too uncertain to support the conclusions drawn from it, and that the inferences of the school are sometimes contradicted by new observations. The school replies that the inferences are revised as new observations are made, and that the doctrine is therefore not a fixed account but a growing one.

## Significance

Deep Stones is among the more fruitful of the theoretical sciences, having directed the attention of scholars to the deep places of the world and to the record that the stones preserve of the ages in which they were formed. Whether or not the succession of the layers is precisely as the school supposes, the doctrine has produced a manner of inquiry now in use among every careful observer of the rocks, and the chroniclers of the deep mines have begun to keep their logs with greater precision. The doctrine may in time be confirmed by discoveries yet to be made, or it may give place to a better account of the world's stony foundation. In either case it will be remembered as one of the great constructions of the age.
