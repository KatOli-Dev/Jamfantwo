## Overview

The Theory of the Moving Continents is the name given to a small and contested school of reasoning that holds the lands of the world to be no more fixed than the waters that surround them. The school argues that the three known continents, [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), together with the islands scattered through the warm waters of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), have not always occupied their present positions, and that they have shifted, and continue to shift, across the face of the world by measurable amounts over long ages. The theory is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, for it proceeds by reasoning upon observed forms of shore and mountain range rather than by direct experiment, and its conclusions, if accepted, would require the cartographers of the spheres to redraw the charts that have governed navigation for several generations.

## The Original Observation

The doctrine arose in the libraries of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), where scholars of the physical studies had long collected specimens of stone brought by mariners from distant shores. The Amurran scholars, working under the direction of [Magister Halen of Amurra](/content/people/notable/magister-halen-of-amurra), observed that certain strata of rock along the eastern coast of Aethelar bore a strong likeness in colour and grain to strata reported by miners of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) upon the far western shore. The matching was not perfect, but it was close enough to provoke inquiry, and a correspondence was opened between the Amurran scholars and the thanes of the stone-holds, who sent samples of their own.

When the samples were set side by side upon a single table, the likeness could no longer be set aside as the caprice of the eye. The red sandstone of the Aethelar coast matched that of the [The Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/the-stoneheim-highlands), and a grey-green granite reported from the southern flank of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) was found to resemble in turn a granite that quarriers of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) reported from the far northern cliffs. The Amurran scholars proposed, cautiously at first, that these likenesses were best explained by the lands having once lain closer one to another, and that they had since drifted apart.

## The Proposed Mechanism

The scholars of the school further proposed a mechanism for the supposed drift. They reasoned that the land rests upon a deeper stratum of the world, and that this deeper stratum is not perfectly rigid but capable of slow movement under pressures too great to be observed in a single human lifetime. The mechanism is set out in the original Amurran treatise as a series of reasoned conjectures rather than as demonstrated fact, and its authors are careful to distinguish between what has been observed and what has been inferred.

Opponents of the theory, and they are many, object that the proposed mechanism demands a force sufficient to move a continent, and that no such force has been identified. The cartographers of the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/the-cartography-of-the-spheres) hold that the disposition of the lands is fixed and ancient, and that the matching of stone is to be explained by the action of the seas, which they suppose to have once covered the lower portions of the present continents. The physical practitioners of the [Study of Waters and Currents](/content/belief/science/physical/the-study-of-waters-and-currents) likewise hold to a fixed disposition of the continents, though they allow that the level of the seas has varied in past ages.

## Supporting Evidence

The adherents of the theory have assembled a body of supporting observation over the years since the original Amurran inquiry, though none of it is held to be conclusive by the broader community of scholars. The shape of the coasts is one such piece of evidence, for the eastern shore of Aethelar, where it meets the warm Auric, is said to fit, in the manner of a broken rim, against the western shore of Sahrana as drawn upon the great charts, and the scholars of the school argue that the fit is too exact to be the work of chance. The fit is not exact, and the cartographers are quick to point out the gaps and overlaps, but the school maintains that erosion over uncounted ages would account for the imprecision.

A second piece of evidence is drawn from the distribution of certain living creatures, a study pursued by natural philosophers who are not themselves of the school. The [amber crawler](/content/species/beasts/amber-crawler), which is found in the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/the-amber-vale) of Aethelar and nowhere else in the known world, has a near cousin in the [plains thunder boar](/content/species/beasts/plains-thunder-boar) of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-great-grass-plains) of Sahrana, and the resemblance is such that the creatures have been held by some to share a common ancestry. The adherents of the theory argue that this is explained if the two lands were once joined, and the creatures have since been separated by the widening of the seas between.

## Objections and Difficulties

The theory is not without its difficulties, and the more sober of its adherents are the first to acknowledge them. The proposed mechanism remains unverified, and no instrument has yet been devised by which the supposed drift of the continents might be measured directly. The fit of the coasts, though suggestive, is not precise enough to compel assent, and the resemblance of certain beasts may be explained as well by the independent working of nature in two separate lands as by the former union of those lands.

A further objection is drawn from the testimony of the oldest cultures. The [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/the-old-pantheon-of-the-plains), whose hymns preserve the memory of the early wanderings of the Sahrani peoples, makes no mention of any land other than the present continent, and the ancient chronicles of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-aethelar-heartwood), which purport to record the earliest ages of the world, set down the three continents as they now are. The adherents reply that human memory is short and the drift of continents slow, and that what the ancient hymns do not record may nevertheless have occurred in ages before them.

## Reception Among the Learned

The theory has been received with a wary interest by the cartographers, with outright rejection by the physical practitioners of the study of waters, and with cautious sympathy by certain natural philosophers whose observations of beast and stone led them to the same conclusions by independent paths. The great [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast), which maintains academies of learning in several of its port cities, has given the doctrine a hearing but has not adopted it as a teaching of the league's own schools. The Amurran republic, where the theory first took shape, continues to support the inquiry, and the scholars of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) are accounted the foremost authorities of the school.

The dispute is thus a long and unresolved one, and the chronicler records it here without taking a side, for the evidence, on either showing, is not yet sufficient to settle the matter. The theory may in time be confirmed or refuted by further observation, or it may pass from the memory of the learned as the quaint conjecture of an age that lacked the instruments to test its own ideas.

## Significance

The Theory of the Moving Continents, whether or not it proves true, has already accomplished one enduring work, for it has directed the attention of scholars to the matching of stone across distant shores and to the distribution of living creatures upon lands separated by wide seas. The natural philosophers of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), whose long familiarity with the desert interior of Sahrana had made them alert to the patterns of the rock, contributed early to the inquiry and continue to provide specimens from the deep exposures of the southern mountains. Whether the continents move or stand, the inquiry has enlarged the bounds of the theoretical sciences, and the doctrine will be remembered as one of the bolder speculations of the age.
