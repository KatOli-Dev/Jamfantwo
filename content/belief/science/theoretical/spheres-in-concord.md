## Overview

Spheres in Concord is the theoretical doctrine that the celestial spheres, by which the heavens are divided into successive regions, are not the silent and independent shells described by the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography), but a single ordered system whose parts are bound together in a measured correspondence. The philosophy takes its name from this correspondence, which it likens to the harmony of a well-tuned instrument, and holds that the proper motion of each sphere is determined by its relation to the others, and that any disturbance in one sphere is communicated after a fashion to the rest. The doctrine is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, its conclusions drawn from reasoning upon long records of celestial observation rather than from direct demonstration of the harmony.

## The Doctrine of Correspondence

The philosophy arose in the academies of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), whose scholars in the generation after the great chronicles of [Vaelen Eldreth](/content/people/historical/vaelen-eldreth) began to compare the motions of the planets with the motions of the sun and the moons. The comparison revealed that the motions were not unrelated: the times at which one planet reversed its apparent course in the heavens corresponded after an interval with the times at which another planet did the same. The scholars proposed that the correspondence was not the caprice of the eye but a true relation of the spheres, by which each part of the heavens responded to the motions of the others.

The principal treatise of the school holds that the celestial spheres are arranged one above the other in a strict order, and that each sphere is tuned to the spheres above and below it. The lower spheres, being nearer to the earth, are slower in motion and heavier in influence, while the higher spheres are swifter and finer. The harmony of the whole is preserved by the correspondence between the spheres, and any disturbance in one is communicated in time to the rest.

## The Music of the Spheres

The philosophers employ the metaphor of music to describe the correspondence of the spheres, holding that the heavens produce by their motions a sound too fine for the ear to hear but apprehensible by the trained mind. The metaphor is an old one, and the philosophers have refined it with care, proposing that the proportions of the motions of the spheres are the same as the proportions of the notes of a tuned instrument, and that the harmony of the spheres is the same in kind as the harmony of music, though different in degree.

The metaphor is not held to be a literal truth, and the more cautious philosophers are careful to say so, for a literal interpretation would require the existence of an air in the heavens capable of transmitting sound, of which no philosopher has any evidence. The metaphor is offered as a help to the understanding, by which the correspondence of the spheres may be conceived in familiar terms. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), whose music is held to be the most subtle in the known world, have given the metaphor particular approval, and certain of their philosophers have composed treatises upon the music of the spheres preserved in the libraries of the sylvan court.

## The Question of Influence

The handling of celestial influence is the principal stronghold of the philosophy. The belief that the heavens exercise an influence upon the lives of men and women is of long standing, and the philosophers of the harmony offer a more coherent account of this influence than any other school. They hold that the correspondence of the spheres is the means by which this influence is transmitted, and that the configuration of the spheres at the moment of a birth determines in some measure the disposition and fortunes of the newborn. The doctrine does not assert that the heavens compel; it allows wide freedom to the will and circumstances of the person, but holds that the celestial configuration is one of the conditions of every life.

The practitioners of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), whose faith admits no influence save that of the one god, are divided. The more austere reject the philosophy altogether, holding that the heavens are the work of the god and not a second cause. The more moderate allow that the heavens may be read as signs, though not as causes, and that the philosophy in its more restrained form may be reconciled with the faith. The philosophers welcome the moderated reading and are careful to distinguish their doctrine from the older practices of divination, which they hold to be without foundation.

## Difficulties and Objections

The correspondence of the spheres has never been directly observed, and the philosophers admit that it is inferred from the regularities of celestial motion. The cartographers of the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) observe that the regularities may be explained as well by the independent motions of the spheres as by their correspondence.

The philosophers reply that the correspondences are too numerous and too precise to be the work of chance, and that the hypothesis of an ordered harmony is the simplest account of what is observed. The reply is not conclusive, but they maintain that a hypothesis which brings a great variety of phenomena under a single principle is to be preferred to one which leaves them separate.

## Centres of the Philosophy

The philosophy is pursued chiefly in the libraries and observatories of the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), where the doctrine first took shape and where the records of celestial observation are longest kept. The scholars of [Vaelmouth](/content/location/settlement/city/vaelmouth) and [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) are accounted among the foremost authorities of the school, and the academies of those cities instruct apprentices under the direction of elder masters. The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) maintains its own philosophers of the harmony, and the [Caelindis](/content/people/notable/caelindis-sylvancourt), whose learning is renowned among the elves, is said to have composed a commentary upon the principal treatise of the school.

## Significance

Spheres in Concord is among the more ambitious of the theoretical sciences, essaying to bring the whole of the heavens under a single principle and to link the heavens with the lives of the people beneath them. Whether or not the harmony is real in the precise form that the school supposes, the philosophy has enlarged the careful study of the heavens and has produced a manner of speaking about celestial influence now in use among every school of learning.
