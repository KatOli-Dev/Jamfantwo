## Overview

Drifting Continents is a small and contested school of reasoning that holds the lands of the world to be no more fixed than the waters that surround them. The school argues that the three known continents, [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), together with the islands scattered through the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), have not always occupied their present positions, and that they have shifted and continue to shift across the face of the world by measurable amounts over long ages. The theory is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, proceeding by reasoning upon observed forms of shore and mountain range rather than by direct experiment, and its conclusions, if accepted, would require the cartographers of the spheres to redraw charts that have governed navigation for several generations.

## The Original Observation

The doctrine arose in the libraries of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), where scholars of physical studies had long collected specimens of stone brought by mariners from distant shores. Working under the direction of [Halen Amurra](/content/people/notable/halen-amurra), they observed that certain strata of rock along the eastern coast of Aethelar bore a strong likeness in colour and grain to strata reported by miners of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) upon the far western shore. The matching was not perfect, but it was close enough to provoke inquiry, and a correspondence was opened between the Amurran scholars and the thanes of the stone-holds, who sent samples of their own.

When the samples were set side by side upon a single table, the likeness could no longer be set aside as the caprice of the eye. The red sandstone of the Aethelar coast matched that of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands), and a grey-green granite from the southern flank of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) was found to resemble a granite that quarriers of the [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) reported from the far northern cliffs. The Amurran scholars proposed, cautiously at first, that these likenesses were best explained by the lands having once lain closer together and since drifted apart.

## The Proposed Mechanism

The scholars further proposed a mechanism for the supposed drift. They reasoned that the land rests upon a deeper stratum of the world that is not perfectly rigid but capable of slow movement under pressures too great to be observed in a single human lifetime. The mechanism is set out in the original Amurran treatise as a series of reasoned conjectures rather than demonstrated fact.

Opponents of the theory object that the proposed mechanism demands a force sufficient to move a continent, and that no such force has been identified. The cartographers of the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) hold that the disposition of the lands is fixed and ancient, and that the matching of stone is to be explained by the action of seas that once covered the lower portions of the present continents. The practitioners of the [Tide-Reading](/content/belief/science/physical/tide-reading) likewise hold to a fixed disposition of the continents, though they allow that the level of the seas has varied in past ages.

## Supporting Evidence

The adherents of the theory have assembled a body of supporting observation, though none is held to be conclusive by the broader community of scholars. The shape of the coasts is one such piece of evidence: the eastern shore of Aethelar, where it meets the warm Auric, is said to fit against the western shore of Sahrana in the manner of a broken rim, and the school argues that the fit is too exact to be the work of chance. The fit is not exact, and the cartographers are quick to point out the gaps and overlaps, but the school maintains that erosion over uncounted ages would account for the imprecision.

A second piece of evidence is drawn from the distribution of certain living creatures. The [amber crawler](/content/species/beasts/amber-crawler), found in the [Amber Vale](/content/location/settlement/region/amber-vale) of Aethelar and nowhere else, has a near cousin in the [plains thunder boar](/content/species/beasts/plains-thunder-boar) of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) of Sahrana, and the resemblance is such that the creatures have been held by some to share a common ancestry. The adherents argue that this is explained if the two lands were once joined and the creatures have since been separated by the widening of the seas.

## Objections and Difficulties

The theory faces several difficulties. The proposed mechanism remains unverified, and no instrument has yet been devised by which the supposed drift might be measured directly. The fit of the coasts, though suggestive, is not precise enough to compel assent, and the resemblance of beasts may be explained as well by the independent working of nature in two separate lands.

A further objection is drawn from the testimony of the oldest cultures. The [Sky Walkers' Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sky-walkers-way), whose hymns preserve the memory of the early wanderings of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) peoples, makes no mention of any land other than the present continent, and the ancient chronicles of the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood) set down the three continents as they now are. The adherents reply that human memory is short and the drift of continents slow, and that what the ancient hymns do not record may nevertheless have occurred in ages before them.

## Reception Among the Learned

The theory has been received with wary interest by the cartographers, with outright rejection by the physical practitioners of the study of waters, and with cautious sympathy by certain natural philosophers whose observations of beast and stone led them to the same conclusions by independent paths. The [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league), which maintains academies in several port cities, has given the doctrine a hearing but has not adopted it as a teaching of the league's own schools. The Amurran republic continues to support the inquiry, and the scholars of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) are accounted the foremost authorities of the school.

The dispute remains unresolved. The theory may in time be confirmed or refuted by further observation, or it may pass from the memory of the learned as the quaint conjecture of an age that lacked the instruments to test its own ideas.

## Significance

Whether or not it proves true, Drifting Continents has already directed the attention of scholars to the matching of stone across distant shores and to the distribution of living creatures upon lands separated by wide seas. The natural philosophers of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), whose familiarity with the desert interior of Sahrana made them alert to the patterns of the rock, contributed early to the inquiry and continue to provide specimens from the deep exposures of the southern mountains. Whether the continents move or stand, the inquiry has enlarged the bounds of the theoretical sciences.
