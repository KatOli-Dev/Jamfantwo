## Overview

The Theory of the Ebb and Flow is the theoretical doctrine that accounts for the regular rising and falling of the seas, the swelling of certain waters at certain seasons, and the long-period variations of level that are observed upon the coasts of the known world. The theory takes its name from the phenomena it seeks to explain, and it holds that the waters of the world are in perpetual motion, drawn now toward one shore and now toward another by the action of celestial bodies, by the warming and cooling of the air, and by the slow movement of the world itself. The doctrine is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, for it proceeds by reasoning upon the observed behaviour of the waters rather than by any direct measurement of the forces that are supposed to move them.

## The Original Doctrine

The theory arose among the natural philosophers of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), whose scholars, in the generation after the principal treatises of the [Study of Waters and Currents](/content/belief/science/physical/study-of-waters-and-currents), began to compare the records of the tide kept by the port towns of several coasts. The comparison revealed that the tides of different coasts did not all rise and fall at the same hour, and that the times of high water shifted in a regular manner from one coast to another. The scholars of Amurra proposed that the tides were not local phenomena, set up by the particular configuration of each coast, but were the visible signs of a single great motion of the waters, propagated across the seas from one region to another.

The theory, as set out in the principal treatise of the school, holds that the waters of the world are drawn by the attraction of the two moons, and that the waters are also affected by the warming of the air above them and by the slow turning of the world beneath the heavens. The tides of the coasts are the local signs of these great motions, and the careful observation of the tides at any coast may reveal what is happening in the open seas beyond.

## The Behaviour of the Tides

The theory offers an explanation for several patterns of the tides that the mariners of the known world have long observed. The twice-daily rise and fall of the waters upon most coasts is explained as the consequence of the daily turning of the world beneath the two moons, and the greater height of the tides at the full of the Lady is explained as the consequence of the alignment of the two moons with the sun, by which their attraction is combined. The seasonal variation of the tides, which are higher in some months than in others, is explained as the consequence of the changing distance of the moons from the earth in the course of their circuits, and the long-period variation, which is observed by the most careful of the port keepers, is explained as the consequence of a slow precession of the heavens above.

The school further holds that the behaviour of the tides is different in different seas, and that the peculiarities of each coast are to be explained by the local configuration of the shore and the depth of the water beyond. The tides of the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), which rush through the narrows with great violence, are explained as the consequence of the narrowness of the passage and the great body of water that must force its way through. The tides of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), which are small and uncertain, are explained as the consequence of the open reach of the water and the absence of any land to contain it.

## The Question of the Long Swell

The handling of the long swell, which is observed upon the coasts of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) and the open [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), is the principal stronghold of the theory. The long swell is a slow rise and fall of the water, distinct from the ordinary tides, which occurs at intervals of several days and is held by the practitioners of the study of waters to be the consequence of distant storms in the open seas. The philosophers of the theory propose that the long swell is in truth a tide of a different period, set up by the slow motion of the waters beneath the heavens, and that the storms of the open ocean are themselves the consequence of this slow motion.

The two explanations are not necessarily exclusive, the philosophers observe, for a distant storm may set the water in motion, and the motion so set up may persist for some time after the storm has passed. The practitioners of the study of waters accept the explanation in part, but they hold that the long swell is more usefully explained as the direct consequence of the wind upon the open water, and that the slow motion of the waters beneath the heavens is a hypothesis not yet required by the evidence.

## Difficulties and Objections

The theory is not without its serious difficulties, and the more cautious of its adherents acknowledge them. The forces that are supposed to move the waters have never been directly measured, and the philosophers of the theory admit that they are inferred from the regularities of the observed tides. The cartographers of the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/cartography-of-the-spheres) observe that the regularities may be explained as well by the local configuration of each coast as by a single great motion of the waters, and they ask why the more elaborate explanation should be preferred.

The philosophers reply that the regularities are too numerous and too widely distributed to be the consequence of local configuration alone, and that the hypothesis of a single great motion is the simplest account of what is observed. The reply is not conclusive, and the philosophers know it, but they maintain that a theory which brings a great variety of phenomena under a single principle is to be preferred to one which leaves them separate.

The mariners of the port towns, who must trust their lives to the predictions of the tide tables, are divided in their allegiance, and many continue to use the older tables of the study of waters, which take account only of the observed times of high and low water at each port. The philosophers of the theory observe that the older tables do not explain why the times of high water shift from one port to another, and that the theory of the ebb and flow is the only doctrine that accounts for the shift.

## Centres of the Theory

The theory is pursued chiefly in the libraries of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), where the records of tide and current are longest kept, and in the academies of [Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) and [Nordhaven](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-nordhaven), where the motions of the moons are most carefully studied. The scholars of the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose wide experience of the warm ocean gives them a particular authority upon the behaviour of its waters, have contributed treatises upon the long swell and the seasonal variations of level. The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the southern coasts, who dwell upon the water itself, are said to keep their own records of the tides, and a [Captain Sael of the Pact Guard](/content/people/notable/captain-sael-of-the-pact-guard) of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) is said to have composed a treatise upon the tides of the strait.

## Significance

The Theory of the Ebb and Flow is, in the judgment of the chronicler, among the more elegant of the theoretical sciences, for it brings under a single principle a great variety of phenomena that had previously been studied separately. Whether or not the great motion of the waters is real in the precise form that the school supposes, the theory has directed the attention of scholars to the regularities of the tides and to the relation between the waters and the heavens above them. The doctrine will be remembered as one of the bolder constructions of the age, and as an example of what the theoretical sciences may accomplish when observation is patient and reasoning is bold. The theory may in time be confirmed by discoveries yet to be made, or it may give place to a better account of the motions of the waters. In either case it will be remembered as one of the great monuments of the inquiring spirit.
