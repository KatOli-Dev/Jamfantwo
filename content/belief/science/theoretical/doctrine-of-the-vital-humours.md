## Overview

The Doctrine of the Vital Humours is the theoretical science of health and disease that ascribes the functioning of the living body to the balance of four internal substances, the blood, the phlegm, the yellow bile, and the black bile, which the practitioners of the doctrine term the humours. The doctrine holds that health is the proper balance and free motion of these four substances within the body, and that disease is their imbalance, their corruption, or their stagnation. The doctrine is reckoned among the theoretical sciences, for it proceeds by reasoning upon observed signs of health and disease, and upon the effects of remedies, rather than by any direct inspection of the humours themselves, which no physician has yet seen.

## The Four Humours

The doctrine, as received by the physicians of the known world, distinguishes four humours, each with its own character and its own seat within the body. The blood is held to be warm and moist, and to be produced in the liver from the nourishment taken in by mouth, and to flow through the veins to every part of the body, giving heat and strength. The phlegm is held to be cold and moist, and to reside chiefly in the head and the lungs, where it cools the blood and lubricates the joints. The yellow bile is held to be warm and dry, and to be secreted in the spleen and the stomach, where it sharpens the appetite and kindles the digestion. The black bile is held to be cold and dry, and to be secreted in the kidneys and the bowels, where it thickens the blood and gives stability to the frame.

The four humours are further associated with the four qualities of matter, with the four seasons of the year, and with the four ages of human life. Blood is held to predominate in the spring and in the young, phlegm in the winter and in the old, yellow bile in the summer and in the mature, and black bile in the autumn and in those of middle years. The physician who knows the humour that predominates in his patient may, by this knowledge, the better understand the patient's disposition and the disorders to which he is most prone.

## The Causes of Disease

The doctrine teaches that disease arises when the balance of the humours is disturbed, either by an excess of one humour, by a deficiency of another, by the corruption of one of them, or by its stagnation in some part of the body where it ought to flow. The disturbance may be brought about by any of several causes. The seasons of the year are held to be powerful causes, for a long winter may engender an excess of phlegm, and a dry summer may burn up the blood. The quality of food and drink is held to be of great importance, and the physicians of the doctrine are particular in their prescriptions of diet. The emotions of the mind are held also to affect the humours, and a chronic anger is believed to inflame the yellow bile, while a settled grief is believed to thicken the black bile.

The doctrine further teaches that each disease has its seat in some particular humour, and that the physician must first determine the humour affected before he may hope to cure. A fever, with its heat and its thirst, is held to be a disease of the blood, and a heavy cough with copious expectoration is held to be a disease of the phlegm. A wasting sickness, with its loss of appetite and its sallow complexion, is held to be a disease of the yellow bile, and a lingering melancholy, with its heaviness and its lack of joy, is held to be a disease of the black bile. The diagnosis of the humour affected is, in the doctrine, the first and most important step in the cure.

## The Art of Treatment

The physician of the doctrine treats disease by the correction of the humour that is at fault. If the humour is in excess, it must be drawn off or reduced; if it is deficient, it must be replenished; if it is corrupted, it must be purified; if it is stagnant, it must be moved. The means of correction are of several kinds. Diet is the first and gentlest, and the physician prescribes foods of the quality opposite to the humour that is in excess. Thus an excess of the warm and moist blood is treated with cool and dry foods, and an excess of the cold and moist phlegm is treated with warm and dry foods. The second means is by purging and bleeding, by which the offending humour is drawn out of the body. The third means is by the use of simples and compounded remedies, drawn from the herbs of the field and the bark of the forest, and prepared according to the ancient formulae of the doctrine.

The physicians of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) and [Vashari](/content/location/settlement/city/vashari) are reputed among the most skilled of the doctrine, and the great hospitals of those cities are renowned for their careful keeping of case books, in which the humours diagnosed and the remedies prescribed are recorded against the course of the disease. The [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), whose people have a particular regard for the art of healing, supports a college of physicians at [Vashari](/content/location/settlement/city/vashari) where the doctrine is taught to apprentices under the direction of the elder masters.

## Difficulties and Objections

The doctrine is not without its difficulties, and the more cautious of its adherents acknowledge them. The four humours have never been directly observed, and no anatomist has yet succeeded in isolating them within the body. The natural philosophers of the theoretical sciences, who take an interest in the doctrine, observe that the assumed correspondence between the humours and the seasons of the year is not confirmed by the more accurate observations of the astronomers, who report that the seasons vary in their onset from year to year, while the humours, if they are constant, ought to vary with them.

The physicians of the doctrine reply that the correspondence with the seasons is approximate and not precise, and that the experienced physician takes account of the season as one sign among several. The natural philosophers further object that the remedies prescribed by the doctrine sometimes fail, and that the failures are not accounted for by the doctrine itself but explained by the unskillfulness of the physician or by the resistance of the patient. The physicians reply that no art is infallible, and that the failures of particular physicians are not arguments against the doctrine itself.

## Reception and Influence

The doctrine is the prevailing theory of health and disease among the physicians of every nation of the known world, and the physicians of the [Kingdom of Aldreth](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-aldreth) and the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) are united in their adherence to it, even where they differ upon the details of its application. The [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame), whose temples maintain houses of healing in many cities, has given the doctrine a cautious approval, and the physicians of the temples are trained in the doctrine as well as in the scriptures of the faith. The [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-pantheon-of-the-plains) is more reserved, holding that the humours are subject to the disposition of the gods, and that no remedy may avail without their favour.

The doctrine has been criticised by certain scholars who maintain that the living body is governed by principles not yet understood, and that the doctrine, though useful in practice, is in theory an oversimplification of a matter far more complex. The critics are few, and their voices are little heeded by the practising physicians, who find the doctrine sufficient for the daily work of healing and who have no better account to put in its place.

## Significance

The Doctrine of the Vital Humours is, in the judgment of the chronicler, among the most influential of the theoretical sciences, for it governs the practice of every physician of the known world and touches every household in which the sick are tended. Whether or not the humours are real in the sense that the doctrine supposes, the doctrine has produced an art of diagnosis and treatment that has relieved more suffering than any other single body of learning, and its long history is a record of careful observation and painstaking record. The doctrine may in time be confirmed by discoveries yet to be made, or it may give place to a better account of the body and its disorders. In either case it will be remembered as the principal medicine of its age.
