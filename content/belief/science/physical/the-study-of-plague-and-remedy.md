## Overview

The Study of Plague and Remedy is the practical discipline by which the healers, apothecaries, and watchers of public health of the known world observe the patterns of disease, the conditions under which it spreads, and the remedies that have proven useful against it. The discipline is younger than the other practical disciplines, for the first systematic observations of plague were made only in the last several centuries, and the discipline still has the character of an inquiry that is gathering its material rather than a body of knowledge that has been settled. The discipline is shared across the monasteries, the academies, and the public hospitals of the larger cities, and its practitioners are unanimous in their respect for the subject: plague is the most dangerous of the enemies of the people, and the proper management of disease is among the most important of the responsibilities of any authority.

The discipline is empirical, and its conclusions are tested by the gradual agreement of independent observers in different places. A healer who reports a remedy that cannot be confirmed by other healers loses standing within the discipline; a watcher who can stand upon his records of the spread of a disease when they are checked is consulted by magistrates for decades. The discipline therefore maintains a strict code of record, with the place, the time, the conditions of the patient, and the remedy that was used all noted alongside the outcome. The records of a careful watcher are the foundation of the discipline, and the records are consulted by every healer and every magistrate of every land.

## The Observation of the Patient

The basic work of the discipline is the observation of the patient, and the observation is conducted by the healer at the bedside. The healer notes the colour of the skin, the pulse, the breathing, the temperature, the state of the bowels, and the state of the urine. The healer notes the food that the patient has taken, the water that the patient has drunk, and the air that the patient has breathed. The healer notes the season, the weather, and the conditions of the dwelling, and the healer notes whether other members of the household have fallen sick. The observation is recorded carefully, and the record is the foundation of the diagnosis and the remedy.

The discipline holds that the observation must be made at fixed hours and that the observations must be compared from hour to hour. A patient whose pulse is steady and whose skin is cool is recovering; a patient whose pulse is rapid and whose skin is hot is worsening. The discipline maintains tables of the signs that indicate the progress of a disease, and the tables are consulted by every healer of every land. The tables are revised as new observations are made, and the revised tables are the most useful of the productions of the discipline. The chronicler notes that a careful observation can be the difference between a recovery and a death, and the discipline maintains the observation as one of its most important rules.

## The Catalogue of Remedies

The discipline maintains a catalogue of remedies, and the catalogue lists the medicines and the treatments that have been used against the diseases that the healers have encountered. The catalogue includes the name of the remedy, the disease for which it was used, the conditions of the patient, the dose that was given, and the outcome. The catalogue is compiled by the scholars of the monasteries and the academies, and the catalogue is revised as new remedies are introduced or as new observations are made. The catalogue of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) is the most comprehensive of the warm coast, and the catalogue of [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) is the most comprehensive of the cold north.

The catalogue is consulted by every healer of every land, and the catalogue is the foundation of the practice of healing. A healer who uses a remedy that is not in the catalogue is expected to test it carefully and to record the test in the catalogue. The discipline holds that no remedy is to be accepted without the test of repeated observation, and the discipline maintains the records of every test in its archives. The chronicler notes that the careful work of an earlier healer often outlasts the institution that employed him, and the records of the healers of the monasteries are the foundation of the modern practice of healing.

## The Spread of Disease

The discipline maintains a separate set of records for the spread of disease, and the records are the foundation of the public health of every region. The watchers of public health are appointed by the magistrates of the cities and the regions, and the watchers observe the appearance of disease in the populations under their care. The watcher notes the place, the time, and the conditions of the patient, and the watcher notes whether other members of the household or the neighbourhood have fallen sick. The watcher enters the observation in the public register, and the register is consulted by the magistrates and the healers of the region.

The discipline holds that the spread of disease can be limited by the separation of the sick from the well, by the purification of the air and the water, and by the careful disposal of the dead. The discipline maintains a set of rules for the management of a plague, and the rules are agreed upon by the magistrates and the healers of every land. A magistrate who neglects the rules is judged harshly by the discipline, for the neglect can be the difference between a contained outbreak and a devastation. The chronicler notes that the careful management of a plague has saved many lives, and the discipline maintains the rules as carefully as it maintains the catalogues of remedies.

## The Use of the Apothecary

The apothecary is the practitioner of the discipline who prepares the medicines, and the apothecary is consulted by every healer of every land. The apothecary maintains a shop in which the plants and the minerals of the catalogue are prepared according to the methods that the catalogue prescribes. The apothecary tests the materials that are brought in by the gatherers and the merchants, and the apothecary rejects the materials that do not meet the standard. The apothecary prepares the medicines in small batches, and the apothecary labels each batch with the date, the ingredients, and the method of preparation.

The discipline holds that the apothecary is the custodian of the catalogue of remedies, and the discipline maintains a strict code of practice. An apothecary who sells a medicine that has not been tested is judged harshly by the discipline, and an apothecary who sells a medicine that is past its date is judged even more harshly. The discipline maintains a register of every apothecary of every city, and the register is consulted by the magistrates and the healers. The chronicler notes that the careful work of an apothecary is the foundation of the practice of healing, and the discipline maintains the standards of the apothecary as one of its most important responsibilities.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the health of the people, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims of their own. The scholars of the [The Doctrine of the Vital Humours](/content/belief/science/theoretical/the-doctrine-of-the-vital-humours) consult the healers for the patterns of disease, and the scholars of the [Counting of Plants and their Virtues](/content/belief/science/physical/the-counting-of-plants-and-their-virtues) consult the apothecaries for the virtues of the plants that are used in healing. The healers in turn consult the scholars for the principles that may guide the search for new remedies, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical outcome of the patient. A remedy that does not produce the expected effect is rejected; a remedy that produces the expected effect is entered in the catalogue. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the remedy is the test of the patient, and the test cannot be argued with. The chronicler notes that the practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world, and the authority is one of the reasons that the disciplines are held in such high esteem.

## Significance

The Study of Plague and Remedy is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every healer, apothecary, magistrate, and commander of an army, and its records are the foundation upon which the health of the people of every land is maintained. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the recovery of the patient. The catalogues of the monasteries and the academies are the most widely consulted of the productions of the discipline, and the chronicler notes that no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a catalogue of the remedies of its own region.
