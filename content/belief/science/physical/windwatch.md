## Overview

Windwatch is the practical discipline by which pilots, harbour-masters, farmers, and coastal watchers observe and record the direction and strength of the wind, the gathering and breaking of storms, and the sequence of weather that marks the passing of a season. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first sailor and the first farmer both required a judgement of the weather. Written records are preserved by harbour-masters of the great ports, by pilot guilds of coastal cities, and by chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and conventions of record, and the records of a careful watcher are consulted by every commander of a fleet and every steward of a farm.

The discipline is empirical, its conclusions tested by the gradual agreement of independent observers in different places. A pilot who reports a wind that the harbour-master cannot confirm loses standing within the guild; a watcher whose records withstand checking is consulted by captains and magistrates for decades. The discipline maintains a strict code of observation, with the place, time, state of the sky, and state of the sea all noted alongside the wind itself.

## The Instruments of the Watcher

The basic instrument of the discipline is the wind-vane: a flat blade fixed to a vertical shaft, free to swing with the wind. The vane is set upon a tower or high roof so that it is clear of buildings that disturb the air, and its direction is read against a circle of marked bearings set about the shaft. The reading is taken at fixed hours of the watch and recorded in the watch-book against the time and weather. A simple wind-vane is a common object upon the towers of the larger ports, and the readings of the towers are the foundation of coastal weather records.

The wind-vane records direction, but strength is recorded by the behaviour of the sea, the bending of trees, or the simpler expedient of a cloth streamer fixed to a pole. The streamer is allowed to fly freely, and its behaviour is judged against a standard set of descriptions: a flag that hangs limp indicates still air, a flag that lifts from the pole indicates a light wind, a flag that streams out straight indicates a strong wind, and a flag that tears from its fastenings indicates a storm. These descriptions are agreed upon by harbour-masters and entered in the watch-book without further measurement. A disciplined watcher can distinguish five or six states of the wind by the behaviour of the cloth, sufficient for the practical needs of pilots and farmers.

The more careful pilots of the great ports carry a small instrument resembling a wind-vane but fitted with a graduated arc and a sliding weight. The instrument is held with the arc vertical and the vane presented to the wind; the wind presses the vane against the weight. The strength of the wind is read from the position of the weight on the arc and entered in the log alongside the direction and state of the sea. The instrument is small enough to be held in one hand, and a careful pilot can take a reading every half-hour through a watch. The readings of the pilots are the most precise the discipline produces.

## Watching the Gathering of a Storm

The discipline maintains a set of signs by which the gathering of a storm can be recognised in advance. The fall of the barometer, the rising of the swell upon a calm sea, the appearance of high clouds moving in a different direction from the wind below, the ring of distant thunder, and the behaviour of certain birds and fishes are all recorded as signs, entered in the watch-book against the time at which they were first observed. The signs are not infallible: a sign not followed by a storm is noted as a false alarm, and a storm that arrives without warning is noted as an exceptional event.

The discipline records the life of a storm from its first appearance to its last. The wind at the beginning of a storm is commonly from a different quarter than at the end, and the change is recorded as part of the storm. Rain, lightning, thunder, hail, and snow are all noted alongside the wind, and the sea is described by the height of the waves and the behaviour of the spray. The records of a complete storm are entered in the watch-book in the order in which events occurred, consulted by harbour-masters and pilots who must decide whether a particular day is safe for sailing.

## Records and the Almanacs

The discipline maintains an almanac for each of the principal ports, recording the weather of the port for the year past. The almanac contains the wind and weather for each day, the storms that occurred and their behaviour, the temperature of the air and water, and any unusual events the watchers considered worthy of note. The almanacs are compiled by the chroniclers of the harbour-masters and printed in small numbers for the use of pilots, magistrates, and farm stewards. The almanacs are compared from year to year, and the comparison is the foundation of the practical rules by which pilots and farmers judge the weather of seasons to come.

The practical rules are not infallible, and a year in which the rules fail is entered as an exception. Exceptions are studied carefully by watchers of the coast, for they are the occasions upon which the discipline learns something new. The most useful of the practical rules are the ones that have been revised in the face of an exception, for the revised rules are the ones tested against the weather itself.

## The Use of the Farmer

The farmer requires a judgement of the weather for planting and harvest, and the discipline maintains a separate set of records for the inland. Inland watchers are the stewards of great farms, millers, and keepers of country houses. Their observations are simpler than those of pilots but no less useful: a watch upon the sky, a note of the rain and wind, a note of temperature, and a judgement of whether the season is early or late. The records of inland watchers are sent to the magistrates of the region, who compare them with coastal records to produce a weather report for the whole region.

The weather of a region can be predicted for a season by the patterns the records show, and a weather report is updated every month. The report is consulted by magistrates and army commanders and is one of the most widely read productions of the practical disciplines. It is not infallible, but it is the best judgement the discipline can offer.

## The Dangers of the Coast

The coast is the most exposed of the lands to the weather, and the discipline maintains a special watch upon storms that come in from the sea. Watchers of the coast are the lighthouse-keepers, harbour pilots, and captains of the small boats that put out to warn larger vessels approaching. They observe the sea, wind, and sky, and raise the signals that the discipline has agreed upon for the warning of sailors. The signals are simple and unambiguous, and a sailor who can read them is given safe conduct into the harbour in almost any weather.

Every loss of a vessel upon the coast is recorded, and the records are consulted by harbour-masters and pilots who must decide whether to permit a vessel to leave the harbour. A vessel that sails into a known storm is recorded as a loss that might have been prevented, and the harbour-master who permits the sailing is judged accordingly. The harbour-master has a duty to refuse to permit a sailing in weather that is clearly dangerous, one of the most demanding responsibilities of the office.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that depends upon the weather, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims. The scholars of [Linked Moons](/content/belief/science/theoretical/linked-moons) consult watchers for patterns of wind that may correlate with the positions of the stars, and the scholars of [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) consult watchers for the conditions under which a celestial observation can be made. Watchers in turn consult scholars for celestial signs that may indicate the coming of a storm.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical consequences of the weather itself. A forecast that is contradicted by the weather is revised; a forecast that is confirmed is entered in the almanac as a useful prediction. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the weather is the final judge, and the weather cannot be argued with. The practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world.

## Significance

Windwatch is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every sailor, farmer, magistrate, and army commander, and its records are the foundation upon which weather judgements of every region are made. The discipline has no famous discoveries and no spectacular disputes with other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of agreement with the weather itself. The almanacs of the great ports and the weather reports of the magistrates are the most widely read of the productions of the practical disciplines, and no responsible authority of any land neglects to consult them.
