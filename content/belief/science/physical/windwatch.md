## Overview

Windwatch is the practical discipline by which pilots, harbour-masters, farmers, and the watchers upon the coast observe and record the direction and the strength of the wind, the gathering and the breaking of storms, and the sequence of weather that marks the passing of a season. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first sailor and the first farmer both required a judgement of the weather. The written records are preserved by the harbour-masters of the great ports, by the pilot guilds of the coastal cities, and by the chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and its own conventions of record, and the records of a careful watcher are consulted by every commander of a fleet and by every steward of a farm.

The discipline is empirical, and its conclusions are tested by the gradual agreement of independent observers in different places. A pilot who reports a wind that the harbour-master cannot confirm loses standing within the guild; a watcher who can stand upon his records when they are checked is consulted by captains and magistrates for decades. The discipline therefore maintains a strict code of observation, with the place, the time, the state of the sky, and the state of the sea all noted alongside the wind itself.

## The Instruments of the Watcher

The basic instrument of the discipline is the wind-vane, which is a flat blade fixed to a vertical shaft and free to swing with the wind. The vane is set upon a tower or a high roof so that it is clear of the buildings that disturb the air, and the direction of the vane is read against a circle of marked bearings set about the shaft. The reading is taken at fixed hours of the watch, and the reading is recorded in the watch-book against the time and the weather. A simple wind-vane is a common object upon the towers of the larger ports, and the readings of the towers are the foundation of the weather records of the coast.

The wind-vane records the direction of the wind, but the strength is recorded by the behaviour of the sea, the bending of trees, or the simpler expedient of a cloth streamer fixed to a pole. The cloth streamer is allowed to fly freely, and its behaviour is judged against a standard set of descriptions: a flag that hangs limp indicates a still air, a flag that lifts from the pole indicates a light wind, a flag that streams out straight indicates a strong wind, and a flag that tears from its fastenings indicates a storm. The descriptions are agreed upon by the harbour-masters and are entered in the watch-book without further measurement. The disciplined watcher can distinguish five or six states of the wind by the behaviour of the cloth, and the distinction is sufficient for the practical needs of the pilots and the farmers.

The more careful pilots of the great ports carry a small instrument that resembles a wind-vane but is fitted with a graduated arc and a sliding weight. The instrument is held with the arc vertical and the vane presented to the wind, and the wind presses the vane against the weight. The strength of the wind is read from the position of the weight on the arc, and the reading is entered in the log alongside the direction of the wind and the state of the sea. The instrument is small enough to be held in one hand, and a careful pilot can take a reading every half-hour through a watch. The readings of the pilots are the most precise that the discipline produces, and the pilots are well paid for the work.

## Watching the Gathering of a Storm

The discipline maintains a set of signs by which the gathering of a storm can be recognised in advance. The fall of the barometer, the rising of the swell upon a calm sea, the appearance of high clouds that move in a different direction from the wind below, the ring of distant thunder, and the behaviour of certain birds and fishes are all recorded as signs, and the signs are entered in the watch-book against the time at which they were first observed. The signs are not infallible, and a sign that is not followed by a storm is noted as a false alarm; a storm that arrives without warning is noted as an exceptional event.

The discipline records the life of a storm from its first appearance to its last. The wind at the beginning of a storm is commonly from a different quarter than the wind at the end, and the change of wind is recorded as a part of the storm. The rain, the lightning, the thunder, the hail, and the snow are all noted alongside the wind, and the sea is described by the height of the waves and by the behaviour of the spray. The records of a complete storm are entered in the watch-book in the order in which the events occurred, and the entries are consulted by the harbour-masters and the pilots who must decide whether a particular day is safe for sailing.

## Records and the Almanacs

The discipline maintains an almanac for each of the principal ports, and the almanac records the weather of the port for the year past. The almanac contains the wind and the weather for each day, the storms that occurred and their behaviour, the temperature of the air and of the water, and any unusual events that the watchers considered worthy of note. The almanacs are compiled by the chroniclers of the harbour-masters and are printed in small numbers for the use of the pilots, the magistrates, and the stewards of the farms. The almanacs are compared from year to year, and the comparison is the foundation of the practical rules by which the pilots and the farmers judge the weather of the seasons to come.

The practical rules are not infallible, and a year in which the rules fail is entered in the almanac as an exception. The exceptions are studied carefully by the watchers of the coast, for the exceptions are the occasions upon which the discipline learns something new. The chronicler notes that the most useful of the practical rules are the ones that the discipline has had to revise in the face of an exception, for the revised rules are the ones that the watchers have tested against the weather itself.

## The Use of the Farmer

The farmer requires a judgement of the weather for the planting and the harvest, and the discipline maintains a separate set of records for the inland. The inland watchers are the stewards of the great farms, the millers, and the keepers of the country houses. Their observations are simpler than the observations of the pilots, but they are no less useful: a watch upon the sky, a note of the rain and the wind, a note of the temperature, and a judgement of whether the season is early or late. The records of the inland watchers are sent to the magistrates of the region, and the magistrates compare them with the records of the coastal watchers to produce a weather report for the whole region.

The discipline holds that the weather of a region can be predicted for a season by the patterns that the records show, and the discipline maintains a weather report that is updated every month. The weather report is consulted by the magistrates and the commanders of the armies, and the report is one of the most widely read of the productions of the practical disciplines. The report is not infallible, but it is the best judgement of the weather that the discipline can offer, and the chronicler notes that the report is consulted by every responsible authority of every land.

## The Dangers of the Coast

The coast is the most exposed of the lands to the weather, and the discipline maintains a special watch upon the storms that come in from the sea. The watchers of the coast are the lighthouse-keepers, the pilots of the harbour, and the captains of the small boats that put out to warn the larger vessels that are approaching. The watchers observe the sea, the wind, and the sky, and they raise the signals that the discipline has agreed upon for the warning of the sailors. The signals are simple and unambiguous, and a sailor who can read the signals is given safe conduct into the harbour in almost any weather.

The discipline records every loss of a vessel upon the coast, and the records are consulted by the harbour-masters and the pilots who must decide whether to permit a vessel to leave the harbour. A vessel that sails into a known storm is recorded as a loss that might have been prevented, and the harbour-master who permits the sailing is judged accordingly. The discipline holds that the harbour-master has a duty to refuse to permit a sailing in weather that is clearly dangerous, and the duty is one of the most demanding of the responsibilities of the office.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that depends upon the weather, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims of their own. The scholars of the [Linked Moons](/content/belief/science/theoretical/linked-moons) consult the watchers for the patterns of the wind that may be correlated with the positions of the stars, and the scholars of the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) consult the watchers for the conditions under which a celestial observation can be made. The watchers in turn consult the scholars for the celestial signs that may indicate the coming of a storm, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical consequences of the weather itself. A forecast that is contradicted by the weather is revised; a forecast that is confirmed by the weather is entered in the almanac as a useful prediction. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the weather is the final judge, and the weather cannot be argued with. The chronicler notes that the practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world, and the authority is one of the reasons that the disciplines are held in such high esteem.

## Significance

Windwatch is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every sailor, farmer, magistrate, and commander of an army, and its records are the foundation upon which the judgements of the weather of every region are made. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of agreement with the weather itself. The almanacs of the great ports and the weather reports of the magistrates are the most widely read of the productions of the practical disciplines, and the chronicler notes that no responsible authority of any land neglects to consult them.
