## Overview

The Study of Waters and Currents is the name given by scholars to the body of practical knowledge by which the mariners of the known world read the seas and govern their passage upon them. It is accounted among the physical sciences, for it proceeds from observation and measurement of the waters, the winds, and the heavens rather than from speculation alone, and its conclusions are tested daily upon the open ocean. The study encompasses the behaviour of the great waters, the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), and the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), together with the narrower passages such as the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), and it is the foundation upon which the maritime trade of three continents is carried.

## The Reading of the Water

The first discipline of the study is the reading of the water itself, by which the experienced mariner judges the depth, the set of the current, and the nearness of shoals without the need of line or lead. The colour of the sea is held to be a trustworthy sign, for the deep blue of the open ocean gives way to greenish hues where the current shoals and to the pale and troubled water that marks a submerged ridge. The temperature of the surface is measured by the hand where the custom permits, and the presence of certain fishes and of drifting weed is noted as an indication of proximity to land or to a favoured fishing ground. The mariners of the Stormfall are reputed among the most skilled in this reading, for the shoals and submerged ridges of that shallow sea leave little room for error, and their boatbuilders have shaped vessels of shallow draught suited to its treacherous floor.

The study further encompasses the observation of the tides, which the mariners of the narrow seas have learned to predict with considerable accuracy by the phases of the moon and the season of the year. The keepers of the port towns maintain records of the high and low water at the turning of the moon, and these records are consulted by the pilots who guide vessels through the straits and the harbour mouths, where a misjudged tide may ground a ship upon a bar or dash it against the rocks.

## The Winds and the Weather

The second discipline is the reading of the winds and the weather, upon which the safe conduct of a voyage depends more than upon any other art. The mariners of the Auric have developed sophisticated methods for anticipating the great storms of that warm ocean, observing the colour of the water and the behaviour of the seabirds to foresee the changing conditions. The seabirds are accounted the surest heralds of weather that no cloud formation can foretell, and the experienced captain will alter his course upon the gathering of certain species upon the water, even beneath a clear sky. The storms of the Auric are spirited but generally brief, building in the heat of the afternoon and dissipning by morning, though the great storms of midsummer may last for days and are respected by even the most seasoned of captains.

The cold waters of the Greyring present a different discipline, for the western coast of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) is subject to frequent fogs and to the long gales that sweep in from the open ocean, and the communities of that rugged shore have built their seamanship upon a wary reading of the grey skies and the behaviour of the whales that they hunt. The folk songs of the western coast, full of lost vessels and brave fishermen, are in truth a corpus of hard-won weather lore set to melody, by which the knowledge of the elders is carried to the young.

## The Measurement of Position

The third discipline is the measurement of position upon the featureless expanse of the open water, by which the mariner determines his place when no land is in sight. The study makes use of the sun at noon and of the fixed stars by night, the elevations of which are observed against the rim of the horizon and recorded in tables kept by the navigators of the great ports. The chronicler notes that the theoretical underpinnings of this measurement are set forth in the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/the-cartography-of-the-spheres), the companion science that treats of the world's shape and the geometry of the heavens, and the two studies are reckoned together by those who practise them, though they are taught as distinct disciplines.

The pilots of the strait towns are among the most accomplished in the measurement of position, for the narrows demand a precise knowledge of the vessel's place relative to shoal and shore, and the pilot guilds maintain vessels built expressly for the work of guiding larger ships through the difficult passage. The [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), whose scattered communities depend upon the safe conduct of the ocean, have likewise produced a school of navigation of considerable repute, and the islanders are noted for their seamanship and the soundness of their vessels in heavy weather.

## Instruments and Records

The study is furthered by a modest apparatus of instruments, chief among them the gnomon and the graduated staff by which the elevation of sun and star is judged, and the timed glass by which the hour of an observation is fixed. The [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes), whose workshops produce fine instruments and mechanisms valued by mariners, are counted among the principal makers of these tools, and a well-made staff or glass is a possession prized by any navigator of standing. The records of observation, kept in the counting houses of the ports and in the hearth-halls of the strait towns, accumulate over generations into a corpus of practical knowledge that the study draws upon, and the captains of the long routes between Aethelar and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) contribute yearly to this store by the logging of their passages.

It is the boast of the study that its conclusions are founded upon the repeated experience of many mariners rather than upon the authority of any single teacher, and disputes within the discipline are settled, where they can be, by further observation upon the water. The chronicler observes that this adherence to observed fact, above the pronouncement of persons, is what distinguishes the physical sciences from the speculative branches of learning, and it is the source of the study's steady and hard-won advance.

## Transmission and Centres of Learning

The study is transmitted chiefly through apprenticeship aboard ship and through the informal schools of the port towns, where the elder navigators instruct the young in the reading of water and sky. The strait towns, the Auric ports, and the harbours of the Stormfall coast are the chief centres of this learning, and a youth who would master the study is expected to sail the waters he means to know, for the study holds that the sea of one region is not the sea of another, and that the currents of the Auric are to be learned upon the Auric and not from any book. The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) dominate the maritime communities of the known world, and the mariners of the Stormfall are renowned for their skill, yet the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) contribute to the study through the metalwork of their instruments, even though their heavy bodies keep them from the sea itself.

## Significance

The Study of Waters and Currents is, in the judgment of the chronicler, among the most consequential of the physical sciences, for upon it rests the entire commerce of the oceans and the narrower seas. Without the mariner's reading of water, wind, and star, the trade that binds [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) one to another would cease, and the isolated communities of the coasts and the islands would be cut off from the wider world. The study is a hard and practical discipline, built upon the losses of those who misread the sea, and its advance is measured not in grand discoveries but in the steady accumulation of observed fact that keeps the ships upon their courses and the harbours full of goods. It is, in the end, the quiet art by which the peoples of the world cross the waters that separate them, and its practitioners are among the unsung makers of the age.
