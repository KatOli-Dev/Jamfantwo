## Overview

Plant-Lore is the body of practical knowledge by which healers, farmers, foragers, and brewers recognise the plants of the field and forest, observe their habits through the seasons, and judge the virtues of leaves, roots, bark, and fruits for food, drink, healing, and dye. The discipline has no single centre of learning, but its accumulated understanding is shared across monastery gardens, the kitchens of great households, city dye-houses, and the workshops of every apothecary. A plant that is misidentified can be useless or fatal, and proper knowledge of plants is among the most important of practical skills.

The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first gatherers of roots and berries preceded the earliest chronicles by many generations. Written records begin with the careful catalogues of monastery gardens, which listed the plants cultivated for food and healing and described the soil, water, and sun each required. The discipline has since been extended to plants that grow wild, plants introduced from distant lands, and the seasons at which each plant is gathered, dried, and prepared.

## The Catalogues of the Plants

The basic tool of the discipline is the catalogue: a list of the plants of a region with descriptions by which each can be recognised. The description includes the shape and colour of the leaf, the height and branching of the stem, the colour and form of the flower, the season at which the plant flowers and fruits, and the soil in which it is most often found. The catalogue also notes any plant that resembles another, for resemblance is the most common cause of misidentification. A practitioner who consults the catalogue is expected to read the description carefully and compare it with the plant in hand before drawing conclusions.

The catalogues are compiled by scholars of the monasteries and academies and revised as new plants are introduced or new observations made. The catalogue of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) is the most comprehensive of the warm coast, and the catalogue of [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) is the most comprehensive of the cold north. The catalogues are exchanged between scholars, producing several useful agreements upon the names and descriptions of plants that grow in more than one region. A careful catalogue is among the most useful productions of the discipline, consulted by every healer and farmer.

## The Virtues of the Plants

The virtues of plants are the qualities that make them useful for food, drink, healing, or dye. The discipline maintains a separate catalogue for the virtues, listing the plants of each region with their uses. The catalogue of food plants is consulted by the stewards of great households and by farmers; the catalogue of healing plants is consulted by healers and apothecaries; the catalogue of dye plants is consulted by city dye-houses; the catalogue of brewing plants is consulted by brewers of inland towns.

The virtues of a plant are not always obvious from its appearance, and the discipline maintains a strict code of testing. A healer who intends to use a new plant prepares a small amount and gives it first to an animal, then to a willing assistant, and only then to a patient. The effect is recorded carefully and entered in the catalogue of virtues with the name of the healer and the date. A healer who uses a plant without testing is judged harshly, for misidentification can be fatal. No virtue of a plant is accepted without the test of repeated observation, and the records of every test are maintained in the archives.

## The Seasons of Gathering

Gathering must be done at the right season, for the virtue of a plant varies with the time of year. The discipline maintains a calendar of gathering listing the plants of each region with the month at which each is to be gathered. The root of a healing plant is gathered when the plant has died back and the virtue has retreated into the root. The leaf is gathered when the plant is in full growth and the leaf has reached full size. The flower is gathered when it has just opened and the colour is at its brightest. The fruit is gathered when it has ripened and the seed within is mature.

The season of gathering is recorded for every plant of every region, consulted by gatherers, healers, and brewers. A gatherer who takes a plant at the wrong season produces poor material, and a healer who uses a poorly gathered plant cannot rely upon its virtue. The season of gathering is as important as the identification of the plant, and the calendar is maintained as carefully as the catalogues.

## The Drying and the Storage

A gathered plant must be dried and stored with care, for its virtue can be lost in the drying. The discipline maintains a set of rules for drying, agreed upon by healers and apothecaries. A leaf is dried in the shade, for the sun bleaches the colour and alters the virtue. A root is dried in the sun, for the root is thick and shade drying too slow. A flower is dried in a dry room with a steady draught, turned each day until brittle. A bark is dried in the sun and stored in a dry place, broken into small pieces before storage.

The method of drying and storage is recorded for every plant, consulted by apothecaries and brewers. An apothecary who dries a plant by the wrong method produces poor material, and every tested method is maintained in the records. The careful work of an earlier apothecary often outlasts the institution that employed him, and the records of the apothecaries of the monasteries are the foundation of modern drying and storage practice.

## The Introduction of New Plants

The discipline maintains a special interest in plants introduced from distant lands, for they may have virtues unknown in the region. Introduction is conducted by merchants who travel between the continents and by the scholars who accompany them. The merchant brings back seeds or cuttings, and the scholar plants them in the garden of the academy and observes their growth through the seasons. The scholar records the soil, water, and sun the plant requires, and the virtue when it is finally gathered.

Introduction is a slow process, and the plant must be observed for several seasons before any conclusions can be drawn. A plant that thrives in the first season may fail in the second. The most useful introductions are those observed for many seasons that have proven their virtue in the region, and the discipline has been cautious in the acceptance of new plants. This caution has prevented many a disaster and is maintained as one of the discipline's most important rules.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the land or the kitchen, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims. The scholars of [Humoural Theory](/content/belief/science/theoretical/humoural-theory) consult healers for the patterns of growth and variation of plants, and the scholars of [Plague and Remedy](/content/belief/science/physical/plague-and-remedy) consult apothecaries for the virtues of the plants used in healing. Healers in turn consult scholars for the principles that may guide the search for new virtues.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by practical use in the kitchen, the dye-house, and the sickroom. A virtue that does not produce the expected effect is rejected; a virtue that produces the expected effect is entered in the catalogue. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the virtue is the test of use, and the test cannot be argued with. The practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world.

## Significance

Plant-Lore is one of the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every healer, farmer, brewer, and cook, and its records are the foundation upon which the kitchens, dye-houses, and sickrooms of every land are stocked. The discipline has no famous discoveries and no spectacular disputes with other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the plants themselves. The catalogues of the monasteries and academies are the most widely consulted of its productions, and no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a catalogue of the plants of its own region.
