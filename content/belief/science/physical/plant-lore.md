## Overview

Plant-Lore is the body of practical knowledge by which the healers, the farmers, the foragers, and the brewers of the known world recognise the plants of the field and the forest, observe their habits through the seasons, and judge the virtues of the leaves, the roots, the bark, and the fruits for food, for drink, for healing, and for dye. The discipline has no single centre of learning, but its accumulated understanding is shared across the gardens of the monasteries, the kitchens of the great households, the dye-houses of the cities, and the workshops of every apothecary of every continent. The practitioners are unanimous in their respect for the subject: a plant that is misidentified can be useless or fatal, and the proper knowledge of plants is among the most important of the practical skills.

The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first gatherers of roots and berries preceded the earliest chronicles by many generations. The written records begin with the careful catalogues of the monastery gardens, which listed the plants that were cultivated for food and for healing, and which described the soil, the water, and the sun that each plant required. The discipline has since been extended to the study of the plants that grow wild in the field and the forest, to the study of the plants that are introduced from distant lands, and to the study of the seasons at which each plant is gathered, dried, and prepared.

## The Catalogues of the Plants

The basic tool of the discipline is the catalogue, and the catalogue is a list of the plants of a region with descriptions by which each plant can be recognised. The description includes the shape and the colour of the leaf, the height and the branching of the stem, the colour and the form of the flower, the season at which the plant flowers and fruits, and the soil in which the plant is most often found. The catalogue also notes any plant that resembles another, for the resemblance is the most common cause of misidentification. A practitioner who consults the catalogue is expected to read the description carefully and to compare the description with the plant in the hand before drawing conclusions.

The catalogues are compiled by the scholars of the monasteries and the academies, and the catalogues are revised as new plants are introduced or as new observations are made. The catalogue of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) is the most comprehensive of the catalogues of the warm coast, and the catalogue of [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) is the most comprehensive of the catalogues of the cold north. The catalogues are exchanged between the scholars, and the exchange has produced several useful agreements upon the names and the descriptions of the plants that grow in more than one region. The chronicler notes that a careful catalogue is among the most useful of the productions of the discipline, for a catalogue is consulted by every healer and every farmer of every land.

## The Virtues of the Plants

The virtues of the plants are the qualities that make them useful for food, for drink, for healing, or for dye. The discipline maintains a separate catalogue for the virtues, and the catalogue lists the plants of each region with the uses to which each plant is put. The catalogue of food plants is consulted by the stewards of the great households and by the farmers of every region, and the catalogue of healing plants is consulted by the healers and the apothecaries. The catalogue of dye plants is consulted by the dye-houses of the cities, and the catalogue of brewing plants is consulted by the brewers of the inland towns.

The virtues of a plant are not always obvious from the appearance of the plant, and the discipline maintains a strict code of testing. A healer who intends to use a new plant for a particular purpose prepares a small amount of the plant and gives it first to an animal, then to a willing assistant, and only then to a patient. The effect is recorded carefully, and the record is entered in the catalogue of virtues with the name of the healer and the date. A healer who uses a plant without testing it is judged harshly by the discipline, for the misidentification of a plant can be fatal. The discipline holds that no virtue of a plant is to be accepted without the test of repeated observation, and the discipline maintains the records of every test in its archives.

## The Seasons of Gathering

The gathering of a plant must be done at the right season, for the virtue of a plant varies with the time of year. The discipline maintains a calendar of gathering, and the calendar lists the plants of each region with the month at which each plant is to be gathered. The root of a healing plant is gathered when the plant has died back to the ground and the virtue has retreated into the root. The leaf is gathered when the plant is in full growth and the leaf has reached its full size. The flower is gathered when the flower has just opened and the colour is at its brightest. The fruit is gathered when the fruit has ripened and the seed within is mature.

The discipline records the season of gathering for every plant of every region, and the records are consulted by the gatherers, the healers, and the brewers. A gatherer who takes a plant at the wrong season is producing a poor material, and a healer who uses a poorly gathered plant cannot rely upon the virtue. The discipline holds that the season of gathering is as important as the identification of the plant, and the discipline maintains the calendar as carefully as it maintains the catalogues.

## The Drying and the Storage

A plant that has been gathered must be dried and stored with care, for the virtue of a plant can be lost in the drying. The discipline maintains a set of rules for the drying, and the rules are agreed upon by the healers and the apothecaries. A leaf is dried in the shade, for the sun bleaches the colour and alters the virtue. A root is dried in the sun, for the root is thick and the shade drying is too slow. A flower is dried in a dry room with a steady draught, and the flower is turned each day until it is brittle. A bark is dried in the sun and stored in a dry place, and the bark is broken into small pieces before it is stored.

The discipline records the method of drying and the method of storage for every plant, and the records are consulted by the apothecaries and the brewers. An apothecary who dries a plant by the wrong method produces a poor material, and the discipline maintains the records of every method that has been tested. The chronicler notes that the careful work of an earlier apothecary often outlasts the institution that employed him, and the records of the apothecaries of the monasteries are the foundation of the modern practice of the drying and the storage of plants.

## The Introduction of New Plants

The discipline maintains a special interest in the plants that are introduced from distant lands, for the new plants may have virtues that are unknown in the region. The introduction is conducted by the merchants who travel between the continents and by the scholars who accompany them. The merchant brings back the seeds or the cuttings of the new plant, and the scholar plants them in the garden of the academy and observes their growth through the seasons. The scholar records the soil, the water, and the sun that the plant requires, and the scholar records the virtue of the plant when it is finally gathered.

The discipline holds that the introduction of a new plant is a slow process, and that the plant must be observed for several seasons before any conclusions can be drawn. A plant that thrives in the first season may fail in the second, and the scholar must be patient. The chronicler notes that the most useful of the introductions are the plants that have been observed for many seasons and that have proven their virtue in the region, and the chronicler observes that the discipline has been cautious in the acceptance of new plants. The caution has prevented many a disaster, and the discipline maintains the caution as one of its most important rules.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the land or the kitchen, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims of their own. The scholars of the [Humoural Theory](/content/belief/science/theoretical/humoural-theory) consult the healers for the patterns of growth and the variation of the plants, and the scholars of the [Plague and Remedy](/content/belief/science/physical/plague-and-remedy) consult the apothecaries for the virtues of the plants that are used in healing. The healers in turn consult the scholars for the principles that may guide the search for new virtues, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical use of the plants in the kitchen, in the dye-house, and in the sickroom. A virtue that does not produce the expected effect is rejected; a virtue that produces the expected effect is entered in the catalogue. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the virtue is the test of use, and the test cannot be argued with. The chronicler notes that the practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world, and the authority is one of the reasons that the disciplines are held in such high esteem.

## Significance

Plant-Lore is one of the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every healer, farmer, brewer, and cook, and its records are the foundation upon which the kitchens, the dye-houses, and the sickrooms of every land are stocked. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the plants themselves. The catalogues of the monasteries and the academies are the most widely consulted of the productions of the discipline, and the chronicler notes that no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a catalogue of the plants of its own region.
