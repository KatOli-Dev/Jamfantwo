## Overview

Sounding the Depths is the practical discipline by which pilots, harbour-masters, fishers, and scholars observe and record the depth of the water, the nature of the bottom, the movement of the tides, the character of the currents, and the behaviour of the waves in the harbours, rivers, sounds, and open sea. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first pilot and the first fisher both required a judgement of the water. Written records are preserved by harbour-masters of the great ports, by pilot guilds of coastal cities, and by the chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and conventions of record, and the records of a careful watcher are consulted by every commander of a vessel and every magistrate of a port.

The discipline is empirical, its conclusions tested by the gradual agreement of independent observers in different places. A pilot who reports a depth that another cannot confirm loses standing within the guild; a watcher whose records withstand checking is consulted by captains and magistrates for decades. The discipline maintains a strict code of observation, with the place, time, state of the sea, and weather all noted alongside the depth.

## The Sounding Line

The basic instrument of the discipline is the sounding line: a cord of measured length with a weight at the end. The cord is marked at every fathom or every two fathoms, and the marks are tested against the standard of length at the beginning of every season. The weight is shaped to sink readily and to bring up a sample of the bottom when drawn up. The pilot lets the weight down from the deck and counts the marks as the weight descends, calling the depth when the weight strikes the bottom. The sounding is entered in the log alongside the time and position of the vessel.

The sounding must be made with care. The line must be held clear of the side of the vessel, for the side can deflect the line and give a false reading. The weight must be allowed to sink freely, and the count must be made by the pilot himself. The pilot must call the depth at the moment of the strike, and the call must be entered in the log at the time of observation. A false sounding can mean the difference between a safe voyage and a wreck.

## The Nature of the Bottom

The discipline maintains records for the nature of the bottom, which form the foundation of harbour and river charts. The watcher draws up the weight of the sounding line and notes the material that clings to it: mud, sand, gravel, shells, stones, or rock. The material is entered in the log alongside the depth and position. The nature of the bottom is as important as the depth, for a vessel that runs aground upon sand may be hauled off at the next tide, but a vessel that runs aground upon rock is in danger of breaking up.

Charts of harbours and rivers are compiled from the sounding records and revised as new soundings are made. The chart of a busy harbour is revised every season, while a quiet river chart is revised every year. Charts are printed in small numbers for the use of pilots and magistrates, consulted by every vessel that enters the harbour. The careful work of a watcher is the foundation of the chart, and the bottom records are maintained as carefully as the depth records.

## The Tides

Tide records underpin the practical rules by which pilots and harbour-masters judge the state of the water at any hour. The tide is the rise and fall of the sea twice each day and a half, differing in height and time from place to place. The watcher observes the height at fixed hours and enters it in the log alongside the time and place, also noting the weather and wind, for wind can drive the water up against the coast or away from it.

Tables of the tides for each principal port are consulted by pilots and harbour-masters. Each table shows the time and height of high and low water for each day, compiled from past records. The tables are revised as new observations are made. Careful observation of the tide is the foundation of harbour navigation, and the tables are maintained as carefully as the charts.

## The Currents

Current records underpin navigation of the open sea. A current is a movement of the water in a constant direction, differing from place to place and season to season. The pilot observes the current by comparing the position of the vessel as determined by the compass with the position as determined by the stars; the difference is the set and drift of the current. These are entered in the log alongside the time and place, forming the foundation of current charts.

Current charts are compiled by scholars of the academies and revised as new observations are made. Each chart shows the direction and strength of the current for each region and season, consulted by every pilot who must cross the open sea. The discipline has produced several useful agreements upon the currents of the great oceans, forming the foundation of modern navigation practice.

## The Waves

Wave records underpin the judgement of the state of the sea. The watcher observes the height of the waves, their period, and the direction from which they come, entering the observations in the log alongside the time and place. The wind is also noted, for it is the principal cause of waves, and the relation between wind and waves is one of the most useful practical rules of the discipline.

Observation of waves must be made from a fixed point, for waves seen from a vessel in motion are confused with the motion of the vessel. The watcher observes from a cliff, breakwater, or vessel at anchor, noting the height against a graduated staff set into the water. Careful observation of waves is the foundation of the judgement of the sea, and the records are maintained as carefully as those of tides and currents.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the sea or the coast, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims. The scholars of [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) consult pilots for the positions of vessels and depths of waters, and the scholars of [Linked Moons](/content/belief/science/theoretical/linked-moons) consult pilots for the times of the tides. Pilots in turn consult scholars for the celestial tables by which the position of the vessel is determined, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical outcome of the voyage. A sounding that does not correspond to the chart is rejected; a current that does not correspond to the log is rejected. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the observation is the test of the sea itself, and the test cannot be argued with. The practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world.

## Significance

Sounding the Depths is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every pilot, harbour-master, fisher, and coastal magistrate, and its records are the foundation upon which navigation of harbours, rivers, and the open sea is maintained. The discipline has no famous discoveries and no spectacular disputes with other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the water itself. The charts of the harbours and the tables of the tides are the most widely consulted of its productions, and no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a careful set of the records.
