## Overview

Weights and Measures is the practical discipline by which the magistrates, the merchants, the money-changers, and the tax-collectors of the known world maintain the standards of length, weight, and volume by which the goods and the coin of every region are measured. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first merchant and the first tax-collector both required a measure of the goods that they handled. The written records are preserved by the magistrates of the cities, by the guilds of the merchants, and by the chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and its own conventions of record, and the records of a careful magistrate are consulted by every merchant and every taxpayer of every land.

The discipline is the foundation of commerce, for commerce requires that two parties agree upon the measure of the goods that they exchange. A merchant who sells by a measure that the buyer does not recognise cannot complete a sale; a tax-collector who assesses by a measure that the taxpayer cannot verify cannot collect a tax. The discipline therefore maintains a strict code of standardisation, and the standards are set by the chief magistrate of each region and copied by the subordinate magistrates of the towns and the villages.

## The Standards of Length

The basic standard of length is the cubit, and the cubit is the distance from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger of a man of average stature. The cubit is divided into spans, palms, and fingers, and the cubit is the foundation of the measurement of cloth, of rope, of timber, and of the smaller distances of the land. The cubit of the academies is set by a master rod of bronze that is kept in the hall of the magistrates, and the master rod is copied by the merchants and the craftsmen of the region. The copy is tested against the master rod at the beginning of every year, and the copy is corrected if the test shows a deviation.

The discipline maintains also the larger standards of length: the rod of ten cubits, the chain of a hundred links, and the league of a thousand paces. The rod of ten cubits is used for the measurement of land; the chain of a hundred links is used for the measurement of boundaries and of roads; the league of a thousand paces is used for the measurement of the distances between the cities. The standards are set by the magistrates and copied by the surveyors and the road-keepers, and the standards are tested at the beginning of every year. The chronicler notes that the careful maintenance of the standards is the foundation of the commerce of the region, and the discipline maintains the standards as one of its most important responsibilities.

## The Standards of Weight

The basic standard of weight is the pound, and the pound is the weight of a defined volume of water under defined conditions of temperature and pressure. The pound is divided into ounces and drams, and the pound is the foundation of the measurement of every good that is sold by weight. The pound of the academies is set by a master weight of bronze that is kept in the hall of the magistrates, and the master weight is copied by the merchants and the money-changers of the region. The copy is tested against the master weight at the beginning of every year, and the copy is corrected if the test shows a deviation.

The discipline maintains also the larger standards of weight: the stone of fourteen pounds, the hundredweight of a hundred pounds, and the ton of a thousand pounds. The stone is used for the measurement of the smaller goods; the hundredweight is used for the measurement of the larger goods and of the coin; the ton is used for the measurement of the cargoes of the ships. The standards are set by the magistrates and copied by the merchants and the harbour-masters, and the standards are tested at the beginning of every year. The chronicler notes that the careful maintenance of the weights is the foundation of the tax-collecting of the region, and the discipline maintains the weights as carefully as it maintains the measures of length.

## The Standards of Volume

The basic standard of volume is the gallon, and the gallon is the volume of a defined weight of water under defined conditions of temperature and pressure. The gallon is divided into quarts and pints, and the gallon is the foundation of the measurement of every liquid that is sold by volume. The gallon of the academies is set by a master measure of bronze that is kept in the hall of the magistrates, and the master measure is copied by the merchants and the brewers of the region. The copy is tested against the master measure at the beginning of every year, and the copy is corrected if the test shows a deviation.

The discipline maintains also the larger standards of volume: the bushel of eight gallons, the barrel of thirty-six gallons, and the tun of two hundred and fifty-two gallons. The bushel is used for the measurement of the grain and the smaller dry goods; the barrel is used for the measurement of the ale and the oil; the tun is used for the measurement of the wine and the larger liquids. The standards are set by the magistrates and copied by the merchants and the brewers, and the standards are tested at the beginning of every year. The chronicler notes that the careful maintenance of the volumes is the foundation of the brewing and the selling of the region, and the discipline maintains the volumes as carefully as it maintains the measures of length and the weights.

## The Master Standards

The master standards are the original measures and weights that are kept in the hall of the magistrates of the chief city of the region, and the master standards are the reference for every copy that is made. The master standards are made of bronze or of stone, and the master standards are kept in a vault that is locked and sealed. The master standards are consulted only at the testing of the copies, and the testing is conducted by the chief magistrate and two witnesses. The testing is recorded in the register of standards, and the register is preserved as a permanent record of the discipline.

The discipline maintains a set of master standards for each of the three continents, and the master standards are compared at intervals by the scholars of the academies. The comparison has shown that the standards of the three continents differ slightly, and the discipline maintains the differences in the register. The chronicler notes that the differences are small and do not affect the ordinary commerce of the regions, but that the differences are important for the scholars who must compare the observations of different regions. The discipline maintains the master standards as one of its most important responsibilities, and the discipline maintains the comparison of the standards as one of the most useful of its international activities.

## The Use of the Merchant

The merchant is the practitioner of the discipline who handles the standards in the daily commerce of the region, and the merchant is consulted by every buyer and every seller of every land. The merchant maintains a set of measures and weights in the shop, and the merchant tests the measures and weights against the local standard at the beginning of every year. The merchant marks the measures and weights with the seal of the magistrate, and the seal is the guarantee that the measure or the weight is true.

The discipline holds that the merchant has a duty to use the standard measures and weights and to refuse any measure or weight that is not sealed. A merchant who uses a false measure or weight is judged harshly by the discipline, and the merchant is barred from the guild. The discipline maintains a register of every merchant of every city, and the register is consulted by the magistrates and the buyers. The chronicler notes that the careful work of a merchant is the foundation of the commerce of the region, and the discipline maintains the standards of the merchant as carefully as it maintains the master standards.

## The Tax-Collector and the Public

The tax-collector is the practitioner of the discipline who handles the standards in the collection of the public revenue, and the tax-collector is consulted by every taxpayer of every land. The tax-collector uses the standard measures and weights of the region, and the tax-collector tests the measures and weights against the local standard at the beginning of every year. The tax-collector enters the assessment in the register of taxes, and the register is preserved as a permanent record of the discipline.

The discipline holds that the tax-collector has a duty to use the standard measures and weights and to refuse any measure or weight that is not sealed. A tax-collector who uses a false measure or weight is judged harshly by the discipline, and the tax-collector is dismissed from office. The discipline maintains a register of every tax-collector of every region, and the register is consulted by the magistrates and the taxpayers. The chronicler notes that the careful work of a tax-collector is the foundation of the public revenue of the region, and the discipline maintains the standards of the tax-collector as carefully as it maintains the standards of the merchant.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the commerce of the region or the inquiry into the world. The scholars of the [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) consult the magistrates for the distances between the cities, and the scholars of the [Humoural Theory](/content/belief/science/theoretical/humoural-theory) consult the tax-collectors for the volumes of the goods that are sold. The magistrates and the tax-collectors in turn consult the scholars for the principles by which the standards are set and maintained, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical use of the measures and the weights in the daily commerce of the region. A measure that does not give the expected volume is rejected; a weight that does not give the expected mass is rejected. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the measure and the weight is the test of use, and the test cannot be argued with. The chronicler notes that the practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world, and the authority is one of the reasons that the disciplines are held in such high esteem.

## Significance

Weights and Measures is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every merchant, every tax-collector, every buyer, and every seller, and its records are the foundation upon which the commerce and the public revenue of every land are maintained. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the measures and the weights themselves. The master standards of the magistrates and the sealed copies of the merchants are the most widely used of the productions of the discipline, and the chronicler notes that no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a careful set of the standards.
