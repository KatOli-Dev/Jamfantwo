## Overview

Weights and Measures is the practical discipline by which magistrates, merchants, money-changers, and tax-collectors maintain the standards of length, weight, and volume by which goods and coin of every region are measured. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first merchant and the first tax-collector both required a measure of the goods they handled. Written records are preserved by magistrates of the cities, guilds of merchants, and chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and conventions of record, and the records of a careful magistrate are consulted by every merchant and taxpayer.

The discipline is the foundation of commerce, for commerce requires that two parties agree upon the measure of the goods they exchange. A merchant who sells by a measure the buyer does not recognise cannot complete a sale; a tax-collector who assesses by a measure the taxpayer cannot verify cannot collect a tax. The discipline maintains a strict code of standardisation, with standards set by the chief magistrate of each region and copied by subordinate magistrates of towns and villages.

## The Standards of Length

The basic standard of length is the cubit, defined as the distance from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger of a man of average stature. The cubit is divided into spans, palms, and fingers, and is the foundation of measurement of cloth, rope, timber, and the smaller distances of the land. The cubit of the academies is set by a master rod of bronze kept in the hall of the magistrates, copied by merchants and craftsmen of the region. Each copy is tested against the master rod at the beginning of every year and corrected if deviation is found.

The discipline also maintains larger standards: the rod of ten cubits, the chain of a hundred links, and the league of a thousand paces. The rod is used for land measurement; the chain for boundaries and roads; the league for distances between cities. Standards are set by the magistrates, copied by surveyors and road-keepers, and tested at the beginning of every year. Careful maintenance of the standards is the foundation of regional commerce.

## The Standards of Weight

The basic standard of weight is the pound, defined as the weight of a defined volume of water under defined conditions of temperature and pressure. The pound is divided into ounces and drams and is the foundation of measurement of every good sold by weight. The pound of the academies is set by a master weight of bronze kept in the hall of the magistrates, copied by merchants and money-changers. Each copy is tested and corrected annually.

Larger standards include the stone of fourteen pounds, the hundredweight of a hundred pounds, and the ton of a thousand pounds. The stone measures smaller goods; the hundredweight measures larger goods and coin; the ton measures ship cargoes. Standards are set by magistrates, copied by merchants and harbour-masters, and tested annually. Careful maintenance of the weights is the foundation of regional tax-collecting.

## The Standards of Volume

The basic standard of volume is the gallon, defined as the volume of a defined weight of water under defined conditions of temperature and pressure. The gallon is divided into quarts and pints and is the foundation of measurement of every liquid sold by volume. The gallon of the academies is set by a master measure of bronze kept in the hall of the magistrates, copied by merchants and brewers. Each copy is tested and corrected annually.

Larger standards include the bushel of eight gallons, the barrel of thirty-six gallons, and the tun of two hundred and fifty-two gallons. The bushel measures grain and dry goods; the barrel measures ale and oil; the tun measures wine and larger liquids. Standards are set by magistrates, copied by merchants and brewers, and tested annually. Careful maintenance of volumes is the foundation of regional brewing and selling.

## The Master Standards

The master standards are the original measures and weights kept in the hall of the magistrates of the chief city of each region, serving as the reference for every copy. They are made of bronze or stone, kept in a vault that is locked and sealed. The master standards are consulted only at the testing of copies, conducted by the chief magistrate and two witnesses. The testing is recorded in the register of standards and preserved as a permanent record.

A set of master standards is maintained for each of the three continents, compared at intervals by scholars of the academies. Comparison has shown that the standards of the three continents differ slightly, and these differences are maintained in the register. The differences are small and do not affect ordinary commerce, but they are important for scholars who must compare observations from different regions.

## The Use of the Merchant

The merchant handles the standards in daily commerce, consulted by every buyer and seller. The merchant maintains a set of measures and weights in the shop, tested against the local standard at the beginning of every year. The measures and weights are marked with the seal of the magistrate, the guarantee that they are true.

The merchant has a duty to use the standard measures and weights and to refuse any that are not sealed. A merchant who uses a false measure or weight is judged harshly and barred from the guild. The discipline maintains a register of every merchant of every city, consulted by magistrates and buyers. The careful work of a merchant is the foundation of regional commerce.

## The Tax-Collector and the Public

The tax-collector handles the standards in the collection of public revenue, consulted by every taxpayer. The tax-collector uses the standard measures and weights of the region, tested against the local standard at the beginning of every year. The assessment is entered in the register of taxes and preserved as a permanent record.

The tax-collector has a duty to use the standard measures and weights and to refuse any that are not sealed. A tax-collector who uses a false measure or weight is dismissed from office. The discipline maintains a register of every tax-collector of every region, consulted by magistrates and taxpayers. The careful work of a tax-collector is the foundation of the public revenue.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches commerce or inquiry into the world. The scholars of [Spherical Cartography](/content/belief/science/theoretical/spherical-cartography) consult magistrates for distances between cities, and the scholars of [Humoural Theory](/content/belief/science/theoretical/humoural-theory) consult tax-collectors for volumes of goods sold. Magistrates and tax-collectors in turn consult scholars for the principles by which standards are set and maintained.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical use of measures and weights in daily commerce. A measure that does not give the expected volume is rejected; a weight that does not give the expected mass is rejected. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the measure and the weight is the test of use, and the test cannot be argued with. The practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world.

## Significance

Weights and Measures is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every merchant, tax-collector, buyer, and seller, and its records are the foundation upon which the commerce and public revenue of every land are maintained. The discipline has no famous discoveries and no spectacular disputes with other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the measures and weights themselves. The master standards of the magistrates and the sealed copies of the merchants are the most widely used of its productions, and no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a careful set of the standards.
