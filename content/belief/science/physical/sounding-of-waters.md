## Overview

The Sounding of Waters is the practical discipline by which the pilots, the harbour-masters, the fishers, and the scholars of the coast observe and record the depth of the water, the nature of the bottom, the movement of the tides, the character of the currents, and the behaviour of the waves in the harbours, the rivers, the sounds, and the open sea of the known world. The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first pilot and the first fisher both required a judgement of the water. The written records are preserved by the harbour-masters of the great ports, by the pilot guilds of the coastal cities, and by the chroniclers of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm). The discipline has its own instruments and its own conventions of record, and the records of a careful watcher are consulted by every commander of a vessel and every magistrate of a port.

The discipline is empirical, and its conclusions are tested by the gradual agreement of independent observers in different places. A pilot who reports a depth that another pilot cannot confirm loses standing within the guild; a watcher who can stand upon his records when they are checked is consulted by captains and magistrates for decades. The discipline therefore maintains a strict code of observation, with the place, the time, the state of the sea, and the state of the weather all noted alongside the depth.

## The Sounding Line

The basic instrument of the discipline is the sounding line, and the sounding line is a cord of measured length with a weight at the end. The cord is marked at every fathom or at every two fathoms, and the marks are tested against the standard of length at the beginning of every season. The weight is shaped so that it will sink readily and so that it will bring up a sample of the bottom when it is drawn up. The pilot lets the weight down from the deck of the vessel and counts the marks as the weight descends, and the pilot calls the depth when the weight strikes the bottom. The sounding is entered in the log alongside the time and the position of the vessel.

The discipline holds that the sounding must be made with care, and the discipline maintains a set of rules for the making. The line must be held clear of the side of the vessel, for the side of the vessel can deflect the line and give a false reading. The weight must be allowed to sink freely, and the count must be made by the pilot himself. The pilot must call the depth at the moment of the strike, and the call must be entered in the log at the time of the observation. A pilot who calls a false depth is judged harshly by the discipline, and the chronicler notes that a false sounding can be the difference between a safe voyage and a wreck.

## The Nature of the Bottom

The discipline maintains a set of records for the nature of the bottom, and the records are the foundation of the charts of the harbours and the rivers. The watcher draws up the weight of the sounding line and notes the material that clings to the weight: mud, sand, gravel, shells, stones, or rock. The watcher enters the material in the log alongside the depth and the position, and the entry is the foundation of the chart. The chronicler notes that the nature of the bottom is as important as the depth, for a vessel that runs aground upon sand may be hauled off at the next tide, but a vessel that runs aground upon rock is in danger of breaking up.

The charts of the harbours and the rivers are compiled from the records of the sounding, and the charts are revised as new soundings are made. The chart of a busy harbour is revised every season, and the chart of a quiet river is revised every year. The charts are printed in small numbers for the use of the pilots and the magistrates, and the charts are consulted by every vessel that enters the harbour. The chronicler notes that the careful work of a watcher is the foundation of the chart, and the discipline maintains the records of the bottom as carefully as it maintains the records of the depth.

## The Tides

The discipline maintains a set of records for the tides, and the records are the foundation of the practical rules by which the pilots and the harbour-masters judge the state of the water at any hour. The tide is the rise and the fall of the sea twice in each day and a half, and the tide differs in height and in time from place to place. The watcher observes the height of the tide at fixed hours, and the watcher enters the height in the log alongside the time and the place. The watcher also notes the weather and the wind, for the wind can drive the water up against the coast or away from it.

The discipline maintains a set of tables of the tides for each of the principal ports, and the tables are consulted by the pilots and the harbour-masters. The table shows the time and the height of the high water and the low water for each day, and the table is compiled from the records of the past. The table is revised as new observations are made, and the revised table is the most useful of the productions of the discipline. The chronicler notes that the careful observation of the tide is the foundation of the navigation of the harbour, and the discipline maintains the tables as carefully as it maintains the charts.

## The Currents

The discipline maintains a set of records for the currents, and the records are the foundation of the navigation of the open sea. A current is a movement of the water in a constant direction, and the current differs from place to place and from season to season. The pilot observes the current by comparing the position of the vessel as determined by the compass with the position as determined by the stars, and the difference is the set and the drift of the current. The pilot enters the set and the drift in the log alongside the time and the place, and the entry is the foundation of the chart of the currents.

The charts of the currents are compiled by the scholars of the academies, and the charts are revised as new observations are made. The chart shows the direction and the strength of the current for each region and for each season, and the chart is consulted by every pilot who must cross the open sea. The chronicler notes that the careful observation of the currents is the foundation of the navigation of the sea, and the discipline maintains the charts as carefully as it maintains the charts of the harbours. The discipline has produced several useful agreements upon the currents of the great oceans, and the agreements are the foundation of the modern practice of navigation.

## The Waves

The discipline maintains a set of records for the waves, and the records are the foundation of the judgement of the state of the sea. The watcher observes the height of the waves, the period of the waves, and the direction from which the waves come. The watcher enters the observations in the log alongside the time and the place, and the entry is the foundation of the records of the sea. The watcher also notes the wind, for the wind is the principal cause of the waves, and the relation between the wind and the waves is one of the most useful of the practical rules of the discipline.

The discipline holds that the observation of the waves must be made from a fixed point, for the waves seen from a vessel in motion are confused with the motion of the vessel. The watcher observes from a cliff or a breakwater or a vessel at anchor, and the watcher notes the height of the waves against a graduated staff set into the water. The chronicler notes that the careful observation of the waves is the foundation of the judgement of the sea, and the discipline maintains the records of the waves as carefully as it maintains the records of the tides and the currents.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the sea or the coast, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims of their own. The scholars of the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/cartography-of-the-spheres) consult the pilots for the positions of the vessels and the depths of the waters, and the scholars of the [The Hypothesis of the Linked Moons](/content/belief/science/theoretical/hypothesis-of-the-linked-moons) consult the pilots for the times of the tides. The pilots in turn consult the scholars for the celestial tables by which the position of the vessel is determined, and the consultation has produced several useful agreements.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical outcome of the voyage. A sounding that does not correspond to the chart is rejected; a current that does not correspond to the log is rejected. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because the test of the observation is the test of the sea itself, and the test cannot be argued with. The chronicler notes that the practical disciplines have a particular authority that arises from this immediate confrontation with the world, and the authority is one of the reasons that the disciplines are held in such high esteem.

## Significance

The Sounding of Waters is among the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every pilot, harbour-master, fisher, and magistrate of the coast, and its records are the foundation upon which the navigation of the harbours, the rivers, and the open sea of every land is maintained. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with the water itself. The charts of the harbours and the tables of the tides are the most widely consulted of the productions of the discipline, and the chronicler notes that no responsible authority of any land neglects to maintain a careful set of the records.
