## Overview

Forge and Flame is the body of practical knowledge by which smiths, glass-makers, cooks, brewers, kiln-keepers, and the more curious of natural philosophers observe and control the behaviour of flame, the transmission of warmth through metal and stone, and the changes that heat can work upon familiar substances. The discipline has no single centre of learning, but its accumulated understanding is shared across the smithies of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), the glass workshops of the coastal cities, the breweries of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), and the kitchens of every substantial household of the known world. The practitioners are unanimous in their respect for the subject: fire is the most useful of the elements and the most dangerous, and the proper management of heat is among the most important of human skills.

The discipline is older than any written record of it, for the first use of fire for cooking and for warmth precedes the earliest chronicles by many generations. The written records begin with the careful observations of the smiths of the great forges, who learned to judge the temperature of a metal by its colour and to adjust the blast of the bellows by the behaviour of the flame. The discipline has since been extended to the study of how heat moves through air and water, how it alters the colour of a flame, and how it changes the volume of the air in a sealed vessel. The observations are painstaking and the conclusions are slow to mature, because a fire must be built and tended for each experiment, and the experiment cannot always be repeated under the same conditions.

## The Behaviour of Flame

A flame is the visible region of a fire, and its appearance is the most immediate indication of the temperature and the condition of the burning material. A small flame, blue or pale yellow, indicates a hot and efficient combustion in which the fuel is consumed rapidly. A large flame, deep yellow or smoky, indicates a cooler combustion in which unburned particles are being carried upward. The smiths judge the heat of a forge by the colour of the metal in it: a dull red is just warm, a bright red is workable, a yellow-orange is near melting, and a white heat is reserved for the most demanding operations. The glass-makers judge their kilns by the colour of the glass itself, and the brewers judge their vats by the sound of the boil.

The colour of a flame is altered by the substances that are introduced into it. Salt thrown upon a fire produces a bright yellow colour, copper produces a green-blue, and iron produces a red-orange. The smiths use these colours to confirm the presence of particular metals in their alloys, and the metallurgists of the dwarven holds use the colours to identify the ores that they are smelting. The discipline holds that the colour is a reliable sign when the conditions of the fire are stable, and that the colour is misleading when the conditions are changing rapidly. A fire that is being newly stoked will show colours that do not correspond to the metal, and the observer must wait for the fire to settle before drawing conclusions.

## Heat in Metal and Stone

Heat moves through metals more readily than through stone, and the difference is the foundation of the design of many practical tools. A copper-bottomed pot heats more evenly than an iron-bottomed pot, and the copper is often clad to a cheaper metal to give the pot durability without sacrificing the heat transfer. A stone hearth holds the warmth of a fire long after the fire is out, and the hearth therefore radiates a steady heat into the room through the evening. The smiths use iron rods to draw the heat away from a particular part of a workpiece, and they use stone settings to support workpieces that must be kept hot for a long time. The dwarven smiths of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) have refined these practices across centuries, and the iron and the steel that emerge from the forges of Stoneheim are the standard against which other smithies are judged.

The expansion of a metal when it is heated is the basis for several practical devices. The tightener that the smiths use to hold a wagon wheel in place is set hot and then cooled, and the contraction of the iron as it cools grips the wooden felloe with a force that no mechanical device can match. The fitting of metal rims to wooden wheels and the setting of metal tyres to stone millstones follow the same principle, and the principle is so reliable that the smiths do not need to measure the temperature: they heat the metal to the colour that experience has taught them to associate with the desired expansion, and they rely upon the contraction to do the rest.

## Heat and Air

The air above a fire expands as it is heated, and the expansion drives a current upward. The chimney of a hearth or a forge exploits this current to carry away the smoke and to draw fresh air into the fire below. A well-designed chimney produces a steady draught without any need for bellows, and a poorly designed chimney produces a sluggish fire and a smoky room. The builders of the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) have made the design of chimneys a special study, for the warm climate of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast demands that the chimneys draw well in still air as well as in wind. The principles of chimney design are simple in theory but demanding in practice, and the discipline maintains its own rules for the dimensions of the flue, the height of the stack, and the size of the opening above the fire.

The expansion of heated air is also the basis of the hot-air balloon that the [gnome](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) workshops have produced for the amusement of the wealthy. The balloon carries a small basket aloft on the heated air of a carefully controlled fire, and the operation requires a steady hand and a careful watch upon the wind. The balloon is not a practical means of transport over long distances, for the heat must be maintained and the balloon cannot be steered, but it provides valuable observations of the direction of the wind at different heights, and the observations are recorded in the field books of the discipline for the use of the pilots and the [Windwatch](/content/belief/science/physical/windwatch).

## Heat and Water

Water is heated more slowly than metal, but it can hold far more heat. The brewers of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) have refined the management of boiling water to a fine art, and the records of the breweries contain tables of the time required to bring a given volume of water to a boil under given conditions of fuel and weather. The cooks of the great households maintain their own traditions of the timing and the temperature of the stews and the broths that they prepare, and the traditions are passed from one generation of cooks to the next as carefully as the chronicles of the academies.

The boiling of water produces steam, and the steam can be used to drive simple engines. The smiths of the coastal cities have experimented with engines that lift water from a lower cistern to an upper one, and the engines have been used in some places to supply the upper rooms of large buildings with water from a single source. The discipline maintains that the engines are at present too unreliable for any but the most particular applications, and that a great deal more observation is required before the engines can be entrusted with the supply of a town. The principle of the engine is clear, and the engineering of the engines will improve as the observations accumulate.

## The Working of the Glass

The glass-makers of the warm coast are the practitioners of the discipline who deal with heat at its most refined. The sand and the ash that they use must be heated to a temperature that is high enough to fuse them, and the molten glass must then be worked while it is at the right consistency for the desired article. The glass-makers judge the temperature by the colour of the glowing material and by the speed at which it flows, and they develop a touch for the material that no written instruction can convey. The apprenticeship of a glass-maker lasts for many years, and the apprentice learns to read the fire as a sailor learns to read the sea.

The discipline maintains that the working of glass is one of the most instructive of the practical arts, for the glass changes visibly as it is heated and cooled, and the changes provide immediate evidence of the behaviour of heat. The glass-makers of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) maintain a school at which the natural philosophers of the academy are welcome to observe, and the observations of the philosophers have confirmed several of the practical rules by which the glass-makers work. The chronicler notes that the practical arts and the theoretical branches have benefited each other in the case of glass-making more than in any other, and the benefit is one of the reasons that the academy of Khalisar is held in such high esteem.

## Dangers and Precautions

Fire is dangerous, and the discipline maintains a strict code of precautions. The hearth is set against a wall of stone or brick, and the floor before it is paved with stone or tile. The chimney is inspected before every winter, and the soot that accumulates within it is cleared at the end of every summer. A spark that escapes the hearth is smothered immediately, and a child that plays too close to the fire is removed without delay. The discipline records every serious burn and every lost building, and the records are consulted by the builders and the hearth-keepers of every town in the known world. The precautions are not innovations but inheritances, and the inheritance has been refined over centuries of practical experience.

The chronicler notes that the discipline of fire and heat is one of the few in which the theoretical and the practical are nearly indistinguishable. The smith who judges a metal by its colour, the brewer who times a boil by the sound of the bubbles, the glass-maker who feels the moment when the glass is ready to work - each of these practitioners is making a theoretical judgment, and each is putting the judgment immediately to the test. The discipline has no reputation for grand theories, but it has an unmatched record of practical success, and the success is the foundation of the prosperity of the smithies, the breweries, and the workshops of every continent.

## Significance

Forge and Flame is the most directly useful of the physical disciplines, for the management of fire is the foundation of every craft that requires warmth, melting, or the alteration of materials. The discipline has no famous disputes and no contested doctrines, because its practitioners agree upon the basic observations and because the practical consequences of any error are immediate. The smithies of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), the glass workshops of the Sahrani coast, the breweries of the inland cities, and the kitchens of every substantial household of the known world all carry forward the discipline in their own way, and the shared experience of fire binds the practical arts into a single body of knowledge that supports the comfort and the prosperity of every land that the fire touches.
