## Overview

The Surveying of Heights and Distances is the practical discipline by which the engineers, road wardens, builders, and pilots of the known world measure the slope and length of a piece of land, the depth of a well, the height of a tower, or the width of a river. Its tools are simple - a measured cord, a plumb line, a graduated staff, a sighting tube, and increasingly the small distance-glasses of the gnome workshops - but the discipline is the indispensable foundation upon which every lasting work of construction, navigation, and boundary-keeping rests. Without accurate measurement, no road can be aligned to a river crossing, no aqueduct maintained at its proper fall, and no treaty boundary marked with confidence. The surveyors are therefore among the most respected of the practical crafts, and the calling has its own schools and guilds in the larger cities of the three continents.

The discipline is empirical, drawing its conclusions from repeated observation rather than from any single principle, and its conclusions are tested by the gradual accumulation of cases in which the predicted and the measured agree. A surveyor who reports a distance that another surveyor cannot confirm on a later visit loses standing within the guild; a surveyor who can stand upon his figures when they are checked is retained by the same towns for decades. The discipline has therefore developed a particular code of record-keeping, with the place, the weather, the instruments used, and the assistants present all noted alongside the numbers themselves.

## The Instruments of the Field

The basic instruments of the surveyor are the graduated cord or chain, the staff, the plumb, and the level. The chain is divided into equal links whose length is set by a master standard held in the chief city of the guild. In the temperate lands of Aethelar, the standard chain is commonly divided into one hundred links, with each link a little less than a hand's breadth, and the entire chain corresponds to the distance that a fit [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) can walk in a measured minute on level ground. The staff is a straight rod marked at every span or foot, and the staff is used to measure the vertical component of a slope and to fix the height of small structures. The plumb is a weight on a cord that hangs true regardless of the surveyor's posture, and the level is a longer version of the plumb used to mark the gentle fall that an aqueduct requires to keep the water moving.

The sighting tube is a more specialised instrument, and its quality is the measure of the surveyor's craft. The simplest tube is a hollow cylinder with sights at either end, and the surveyor uses it to align a far marker with a near reference. The improved sighting tube of the [gnome](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) workshops contains a fine thread that crosses the field of view, and the surveyor uses it to read bearings off distant peaks and towers. The angle is measured against a graduated circle fixed to the tube, and the readings are entered in the field book against the time of day, because the angle changes as the sun moves and the shadows shift.

## Reading a Distance

The first task of the surveyor is to determine the distance between two points, whether the points are the towers of a city wall or the boundaries of a farm. The measurement on level ground is straightforward, with the chain laid out along the line and the count recorded at each full chain. The measurement across uneven ground requires care, for the chain follows the contour of the land and the horizontal distance is shorter than the chain length. The surveyor either breaks the line into short segments of level chain, marks the segments with poles, and adds them at the end, or he reads the slope at each break and reduces the chain length by the calculated proportion.

The measurement across water is harder. Surveyors measure the width of a river by setting out a right triangle upon the near bank and sighting the far bank from the two corners of the triangle. The width is then calculated from the two distances measured on land and the angle between the lines of sight. The method requires clear weather, an assistant who holds a marker precisely, and a measurement of the angle that the surveyor trusts. The width of a wide river such as the [Aurics crossing of the Greyring](content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) is measured only at the narrowest points, for the triangles become impractically long and the small errors in the angle accumulate into large errors in the result.

The measurement of height is the companion skill. A surveyor who wishes to know the height of a cliff or a tower sets the staff upon the ground at a measured distance from the base, sights the top with the tube, and reads the angle from the horizontal. The height is then the tangent of the angle multiplied by the distance. The method requires that the staff be perfectly vertical, that the line of sight be unobstructed, and that the surveyor trust the angle reading. The great towers of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) and the cliffs of the [Frostwall Marches](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) have been measured many times by different surveyors over the centuries, and the values differ by no more than a few feet - a measure of the discipline's reliability when the weather and the instruments are both good.

## Working in the Field

The field work of the surveyor is conducted by teams of two or three, with the principal surveyor in charge of the readings and one or two assistants setting markers, holding the staff, and recording the entries in the field book. The principal surveyor chooses the time of day, the route of the chain, and the position of the sighting tube, and the assistants do not alter these without his direct instruction. The chain is laid out by the principal surveyor and the senior assistant together, and the count is verified by the second assistant. The field book is written in a plain script with no abbreviations that could confuse a reader who has not accompanied the survey, because the records may be consulted decades later when the original surveyor is no longer available.

The surveyor chooses the season and the hour with care. The heat of midday produces shimmering air that makes distant bearings unreliable. The cold of early morning stiffens the assistants and contracts the chain. The surveyor therefore prefers the morning or the late afternoon, when the air is steady and the light is slanted enough to show the contours of the ground. He notes the weather, the cloud cover, the wind, and any unusual condition in the field book, because these may affect the readings and must be available to whoever checks them.

## The Boundary and the Marker

A major use of the discipline is the marking of boundaries, whether between fields, between townships, or between kingdoms. The surveyor accompanies the magistrate to the disputed line and measures the positions of the natural features that the treaty names. A boundary marker is set where the line changes direction or where the natural feature gives a fixed point, and the position of the marker is recorded against the measured distances to two or three landmarks that cannot be moved. The chronicler notes that the records of a careful survey have settled more disputes than any magistrate, because the measured line stands as a witness long after the people who walked it have passed.

The boundary marker itself is chosen to last. A wooden post is acceptable for a private field, but a public boundary deserves a stone. The dwarven communities of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) cut boundary markers from the hard stone of the mountains, and the dwarven markers that record the boundaries of the principal holds have endured for centuries. The human communities of the coast set their markers in dressed granite and inscribe them with the date of the survey and the names of the parties. The cost of the marker is judged against the cost of the dispute it prevents, and no responsible authority neglects the investment.

## Records and the Standards

The discipline maintains standards and a system of oversight that distinguishes the trained surveyor from the amateur. The standards of length are held in the chief cities of the guild, and copies are kept by the local chapters. A surveyor who suspects that his chain has stretched or shrunk during a long campaign brings it back to the chapter house and has it tested against the local standard before beginning any work that requires high accuracy. A surveyor who reports measurements that disagree with those of his colleagues is asked to demonstrate his method and his instruments, and the demonstration is recorded against his standing in the guild.

The records of the guild are open to magistrates, builders, and other surveyors who need them, and the records are kept in duplicate so that a single loss cannot erase a measurement that has been relied upon by the public. The maps of the kingdoms and the principal regions are compiled from these records, and the maps are updated as new measurements become available. A map that records a measurement taken two centuries earlier may still be consulted for a boundary that has not been redrawn, and the chronicler observes that the careful work of an earlier surveyor often outlasts the institution that employed him.

## Reception Among the Learned

The discipline is respected by every branch of learning that touches the land or the sea, though its practitioners rarely make theoretical claims of their own. The cartographers of the [Cartography of the Spheres](/content/belief/science/theoretical/cartography-of-the-spheres) depend upon the surveyors for the measurements that they place upon their charts, and the scholars of the [Hypothesis of the Deep Stones](/content/belief/science/theoretical/hypothesis-of-the-deep-stones) rely upon the surveyors for the elevations that support their arguments about the rising of the mountains. The surveyors in turn use the celestial tables of the cartographers to fix the latitude of their surveys, and they use the measurements of the cartographers to place their boundary markers upon the larger geography.

The discipline does not contend with the theoretical branches, because its conclusions are tested by the practical use of its figures rather than by any philosophical argument. A road built to the wrong width is dangerous; an aqueduct set at the wrong fall does not carry water; a boundary set at the wrong distance is contested by the next generation. The surveyor's reputation rises or falls by the practical use of his figures, and this reliance upon use rather than upon theory is the source of the discipline's quiet authority. The learned academies consult the surveyors when they require a number that must stand the test of repetition in the field, and the surveyors in their turn consult the academies when they require a principle by which to interpret an unexpected measurement.

## Significance

The Surveying of Heights and Distances is one of the most useful and least controversial of the practical disciplines of the known world. Its conclusions enter the daily life of every builder, farmer, sailor, and magistrate, and its records are the foundation upon which the maps, boundaries, and engineering works of the present age are constructed. The discipline has no famous discoveries to its credit and no spectacular disputes with the other branches of learning, because its work is judged by the standard of practical agreement with repeated measurement. In an age of grand theories and contested doctrines, the surveyors offer the patient accumulation of checked figures, and the figures have a permanence that few other productions of the present age can match.
