## Overview

The Cataloguing of Flora and Fauna is the physical science that records the living forms of the known world and arranges them according to observed likeness, difference, habitat, and habit. Its practitioners are found among herb gatherers, hunters, orchard keepers, apothecaries, scholars, and the wardens of forests and game lands. They seek to preserve the names and features of plants and animals without reducing living things to curiosities, for a catalogue is useful only when another observer can recognise the same subject and understand how it behaves. The science therefore attends to season, diet, growth, nesting, flowering, migration, and relation to other life as carefully as it attends to shape.

The discipline expanded when travellers began bringing specimens and descriptions between the continents. A plant known by one name in the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-aethelar-heartwood) might be mistaken for a similar plant in [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), while a beast called harmless in one valley might be dangerous in another because its food or breeding ground differed. The cataloguers responded by establishing shared descriptions and by recording several local names beside a principal term. Their work is empirical in the strictest sense, since every entry must be answerable to a leaf, track, shell, nest, or living creature that can be observed again.

## The Work of Naming

The first duty of a cataloguer is to separate the act of naming from the act of explaining. A reed with a hollow stem, silver underside, and red seed head may be given a common name before anyone knows why it grows beside one stream and not another. An animal may be described by its coat, teeth, call, and spoor while the question of its kinship remains unsettled. The old masters warned that a pleasing story about origin can make a poor guide to recognition. For this reason, the catalogues place visible features and repeated behaviour before claims about hidden nature.

A complete entry gives the common name, known local names, season of observation, range, size, distinguishing marks, and relation to the surrounding land. Plant entries include the shape of root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, or spore, together with the soil and moisture in which each part was found. Animal entries record tracks, droppings, feeding signs, calls, shelter, and the manner of movement as well as the body itself. The [Aethelar Stag](/content/species/beasts/aethelar-stag), for example, cannot be properly distinguished from other large deer by antler shape alone, because age and season alter the antlers. The catalogue must include its winter coat, hoof marks, preferred clearings, and the warning bark by which it responds to danger.

## Specimens and Field Records

The science uses pressed leaves, dried seeds, shed feathers, cleaned bones, casts of tracks, and painted likenesses. A specimen is accompanied by the place and date of collection, since an unlabelled object soon loses most of its value. Living subjects are observed whenever possible, particularly when colour, scent, or movement would be lost in preservation. Cataloguers are expected to state whether they saw a feature directly, received it from a witness, or inferred it from a damaged specimen. Such distinctions prevent a copied error from becoming an accepted characteristic after passing through several books.

The best field records describe the whole setting. A plant that flowers beside a salt marsh may fail in garden soil, and an insect found on one tree may be absent from its close relative even where both grow together. The [Sahrani Coastal Strip](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-sahrani-coastal-strip) has supplied many entries in which wind, salt, and short rains determine the life of a species more strongly than the warmth of the air. In the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-great-grass-plains), cataloguers follow the movement of herds and the flowering of grasses across a wide distance, because a single visit offers an incomplete account of a land whose appearance changes with every season.

Drawing remains a principal instrument. A careful drawing shows the arrangement of leaves, the angle of a joint, or the pattern of a wing more clearly than a hurried description. Colour is recorded through comparisons with prepared cloths and mineral pigments, although no pigment preserves the exact shade of a living flower. The introduction of measured scale has corrected many early drawings in which a small herb was enlarged for clarity and later mistaken for a large species. The chronicler notes that accuracy in a catalogue depends as much upon honest proportion as upon the beauty of the illustration.

## Habitats and Relations

Modern cataloguers increasingly group living things by the places and relations that sustain them. They distinguish forest, grassland, marsh, coast, upland, and desert not as empty settings but as communities of soil, water, light, and life. The [Morvind Snow Cat](/content/species/beasts/morvind-snow-cat) is recorded with the high country that supplies its prey and cover, while the [Storm-Petrel](/content/species/beasts/storm-petrel) is associated with windswept coasts and the open sea. Such entries show that a species cannot be understood by its body alone. The disappearance of one plant may alter the insects that feed upon it, the birds that feed upon those insects, and the people who gather the plant for food or medicine.

The catalogue of a habitat also records signs of disturbance. A cleared slope may lose its shade-loving herbs, a dammed stream may change the nesting places of waterfowl, and overhunted ground may cause a predator to range nearer to settlements. These observations have practical importance for wardens and councils, yet they can also unsettle custom. Hunters may resent a record that limits their season, while orchard keepers may welcome the same record if it explains why a pest has increased. The cataloguers are not judges of every dispute, but their evidence allows a quarrel to concern the land itself rather than only the confidence of the speakers.

## The Peoples as Observers

The science has never belonged to one people. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) preserve long observations of trees, flowering cycles, and forest succession, often extending across the remembered lives of several generations. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) have contributed accounts of cave fungi, mineral-fed mosses, and the small creatures that inhabit deep workings. The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) have supplied descriptions of coastal plants, reef animals, and creatures rarely seen above the water, while the [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs) know the seasonal routes of grassland herds in detail that few mounted travellers can match.

These contributions have forced scholars to revise the old assumption that the best knowledge comes from a library. A field observation made by a fisher or herder may be more exact than a copied passage in a noble collection, particularly when the subject is local and the witness has watched it through many seasons. The present custom is to name the source of an observation and to compare it with other accounts before accepting it. This practice has not removed prejudice from the science, but it has made the cost of careless dismissal more visible.

## Classification and Dispute

Cataloguers disagree over the proper order of living things. One school arranges them by outward form, believing that similar bodies reveal similar manner of life. Another arranges them by function, placing burrowing creatures together even when their bodies differ, because they shape the ground in related ways. A third school gives priority to descent and argues that shared structures point to a common ancestry, though the evidence for such relationships is often difficult to distinguish from resemblance caused by similar conditions. No school has prevailed, and the catalogues commonly preserve several arrangements side by side.

There are further disputes about creatures that do not fit accepted boundaries. The [Amber Crawler](/content/species/beasts/amber-crawler) has led to arguments over whether its hardened shell is a true outer skeleton or a mineral growth acquired from its feeding ground. The [Kryn](/content/species/sapient/kryn) and other peoples have challenged scholars who place intelligent beings within the same lists as beasts, holding that the distinction between a living form and a speaking society cannot be settled by teeth or limb count. Most careful cataloguers now separate biological description from moral and legal status, a distinction that preserves the value of observation without granting a catalogue authority it does not possess.

## Uses and Significance

The practical uses of the science are broad. Apothecaries require reliable distinctions between useful herbs and their dangerous look-alikes. Farmers need to know which insects pollinate a crop and which feed upon its leaves. Hunters and wardens use tracks and calls to estimate the presence of game, while builders and road keepers learn where roots may undermine a bank. Even sailors benefit from the catalogue when they identify seabirds, floating plants, or drifting creatures that indicate the nearness of land.

The catalogues also protect memory. A species may become rare without vanishing from every region, and a plant may survive only in a neglected hollow after being lost from cultivated ground. By preserving descriptions and seeds, the science permits later generations to recognise what their predecessors knew. Its work is patient and sometimes uncelebrated, but the chronicler judges it among the most humane of the physical disciplines. To observe a living thing accurately is to admit that it possesses a history and a place not wholly defined by its usefulness to the peoples who name it.
