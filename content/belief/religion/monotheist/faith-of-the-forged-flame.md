## Overview

The Faith of the Forged Flame is a monotheist tradition of the forge and the workshop, held most strongly among the smiths, the smelters, and the metalworkers of the northern reaches of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the mountain valleys of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). Its central teaching holds that the divine is most truly known in the act of shaping raw matter into useful form, and that the fire upon the forge is the same fire that moves in the heart of every craftsman and the same fire that holds the world in being. The faithful call the divine the Forger, and they hold that the Forger has set all things into the fire that they may be made, in time, into what they are called to be.

## Origins among the Hill Smiths

The earliest practitioners of the faith are said to have been the hill smiths of the northern Aethelaran valleys, whose remote workshops depended upon the careful tending of small fires and whose craft demanded that they attend to flame as other men attend to prayer. The oral traditions of the smiths preserve a founding figure known only as the First Smelter, a personage whose name has been variously located in the upper valleys and whose workshop is held to have been the first in which the fire was kindled with intention and with care. Whether the First Smelter was a single smith or a succession of master smiths is disputed among the elders, and the tradition is content to leave the question open.

The faith spread from the hill smiths into the lowland workshops and into the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim), where it found a sympathetic hearing among the [dwarven](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) smiths whose craft is the foundation of dwarven civilisation. The dwarven master smiths did not adopt the faith whole, for they hold to their own ancient traditions of hearth and stone, but they acknowledged a kinship with the Forged Flame and permitted the keeping of small forges dedicated to the Forger in certain workshops, particularly those that served travellers of mixed faith.

## The Fire That Shapes

The doctrine of the Forged Flame centres upon the figure of the Forger, who is held to be at once the source of all being and the patient artisan who refines what is rough into what is useful. The faithful are taught that every creature and every nation is, in some sense, an unfinished work upon the divine anvil, and that the long labour of the world is the shaping of these unfinished things into their proper forms. This teaching lends an unusual patience to the practitioners, who are counseled against hastening the work of the Forger in others, and a corresponding attentiveness to the labour of their own hands, which they understand to be a small share of that larger work.

The teaching does not deny the reality of suffering or of failure in the forge, for the smith knows that metal may crack and that a piece may be ruined by an ill-tempered blow. The faithful are taught that such losses are part of the discipline of the craft, and that the smith who has not yet ruined a piece has not yet been tested. The elders counsel patience in failure and gratitude for the lessons of the ruined work, and they caution against the bitterness that comes from comparing the practitioner's own imperfect pieces to the polished work of the more experienced hands.

## The Workshop and Its Customs

The workshop of the Forged Flame is called a forge-house, and it is conducted in accordance with a body of customs that are common across the tradition and that the smiths regard as essential to the practice of the craft. The fire is kindled at the opening of each working day with a brief spoken acknowledgment that the flame has returned, and the bellows, anvil, and quenching trough are inspected before the first piece is set upon the anvil. The smiths greet one another with a phrase that means may the metal yield, and the customary reply is may the hand be steady. These customs are observed across the tradition in much the same form, though the precise wording varies by region.

A master smith is an elder of the tradition, and the master's workshop is a place of instruction as well as of craft. The apprentice sleeps in the workshop and learns the customs before being entrusted with hammer or tongs, and the years of apprenticeship are considered a necessary crucible of character as much as of skill. The tradition holds that an apprentice who has been tested by long hours and by repeated failure is better fitted to the master than one who has merely practised the motions of the craft, and the forging of the smith is held to be as important as the forging of the metal.

## Rites and Observances

The principal rites of the Forged Flame are bound up with the working of the forge, and the smiths regard the labour of the day itself as the central observance. The major transitions are marked by the dedication of a finished piece. A first knife or a first horseshoe made by a new apprentice is presented to the workshop in a small ceremony, and the master sets it upon the anvil and speaks a brief blessing before returning it to the apprentice, who is permitted thenceforth to keep it as a sign of the calling. A marriage between two smiths is solemnised at the anvil, and the two are permitted to strike together the first blow upon a shared piece, which they then keep.

Death is marked by the quenching of the smith's personal quenching trough in the presence of the workshop, and a final piece is forged in the name of the departed smith by the apprentices and the master, who present it to the family as a token of the completion of the calling. The tradition does not require the burial of a smith with hammer and tongs, though some families have done so as a private observance, and the practice is regarded with tolerant affection by the elders, who see in it a fitting last mark of the craft.

## Fire-Keepers and Travellers

The tradition is served by elders called fire-keepers, who travel between the workshops and supervise the keeping of the customs and the instruction of apprentices. The fire-keepers hold no worldly authority and they earn their living in the workshops they visit, and they are recognised by the mark of a small hammered token that they carry from workshop to workshop. The institution arose from the need to maintain the unity of the tradition across a wide scatter of hill workshops, for the hill smiths have always been somewhat apart from the lowland guilds and required a separate means of common counsel.

The fire-keepers also serve the travellers of the faith, and it is by the fire-keepers that the small wayside shrines known as forge-marks are maintained. A forge-mark is a humble stone basin with a small iron grate, set beside the road at a suitable distance between the workshops, and it is kept with a store of dry fuel against the need of a smith who has journeyed far and finds himself with a piece to finish in an unfamiliar place. The faith asks nothing of those who do not seek it, and the forge-marks are used chiefly by smiths, though other travellers of any people are permitted to warm themselves at them when the fire is kindled.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Forged Flame has long coexisted with the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame), and the two traditions are sometimes mistakenly thought to be the same. The keepers of each tradition have been careful to mark the difference, for the Still Flame is a household faith of warmth and continuity, while the Forged Flame is a workshop faith of shaping and craft. A family may keep both, and many smiths of the northern workshops do, tending the household fire after the manner of the Still Flame and the forge fire after the manner of the Forged Flame.

The Forged Flame has found a particular welcome among the dwarven smiths of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim), who regard the Forger as a kindred figure to the deep traditions of their own hearths. The dwarven masters have not formally adopted the faith, but they honour it at the great forges of the year, and a dwarven blade finished at one of these moments is considered to carry an uncommon blessing. Among the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the northern valleys the Forged Flame is one of the strongest of the monotheist observances, and it is perhaps the chief rival of the [Church of the Silver Mark](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/church-of-the-silver-mark) for the loyalty of the craftsmen and the traders of the towns.

## Significance

The Faith of the Forged Flame is a working faith, in the most literal sense, and its significance is felt in the workshops and the forges of the northern lands more than in the councils of the realm. Its practitioners are patient of failure and devoted to the long shaping of their pieces, and they have given to the hill country of Aethelar and the mountain valleys of Morvind a tradition of craft that is admired throughout the known world. The faith asks of its smiths only that they attend to the fire and the metal and the work of their hands, and it promises them, in return, that this attention is itself a share in the shaping of the world. In a tradition of many faiths and few certainties, the Forged Flame endures as the religion of those who make, and the smith who strikes a true piece at the anvil is counted, by the elders, to have spoken without words.
