## Overview

The Light of the Still Water is a contemplative monotheist tradition held chiefly by the fisherfolk, river-keepers, and quiet coastal communities of the southern reaches of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Its central teaching holds that the divine is most clearly perceived not in tumult or in proclamation but in the surface of unbroken water at the hour of dawn, and that to sit beside still water and to watch is a form of worship as complete as any rite. The faithful call the divine by a name they do not speak aloud, and they understand the Light of the Still Water to be the same single source that moves beneath all waters, all skies, and all the quiet minds of those who attend to the world without grasping at it.

## Origins among the Marsh Dwellers

The earliest recorded practitioners of the faith were the marsh-folk of the lower river valleys, whose lives depended upon the rhythms of flood and drought and whose only reliable mirror was the standing water of the reed beds. They taught that a watcher who disturbs the surface cannot see the sky that lies within it, and that the divine likewise cannot be apprehended by a soul in agitation. The oral teachings of the marsh communities are the foundation of the faith, and though the tradition later acquired written form, the elders of the marsh have always insisted that the writings are a poor second to the sitting by the water at morning.

The faith spread northward along the river systems and into the sheltered bays of the southern coast, carried by traders who had been taught the sitting by the marsh elders and who found the practice well suited to long hours of waiting between tides. The [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) tolerated the faith within its borders without ever adopting it as a state observance, and the deeper communities of the river country came to honour the Still Water as a household discipline, though not all who honoured it in this manner would have called themselves faithful in any formal sense.

## The Unspoken Name

The Light of the Still Water is revered under a name that is not written and not uttered in ordinary speech, for the tradition holds that any name spoken is a name diminished. The faithful refer to the divine by circumlocutions: the Source, the Depth, the Witness, the Light Beneath, the Stillness Before All Stirring. Each of these is taken to be partial, and the teaching is that the full nature of the Source cannot be contained in any single syllable. The elders caution against the assumption that the unspoken name is a hidden secret, for there is no secret, only an unwillingness to mistake a name for what it points at.

This restraint has sometimes been mistaken by outsiders for the worship of the water itself, but the marsh elders are emphatic that water is a mirror and not the thing mirrored. The divine is held to be the light that the water reflects, the same light that moves in the dawn sky and in the wake of a boat and in the dark of an unlit well. The water is a place of meeting, not an object of devotion, and a watcher who mistakes the place for the presence is gently corrected by the elders, who counsel further sitting and further silence.

## The Daily Sitting

The principal observance of the faith is the morning sitting, a brief practice undertaken by the faithful at the first suitable still water that their morning brings within reach. A household that lives beside a pond or a sheltered reach of river will sit together before the day's work; a fisher will sit at the gunwale of a moored boat; a traveller will pause at a wayside well and look down into it before drawing water. The sitting lasts only as long as the mind remains quiet, and many find this within a few breaths; others sit longer when the mind resists. The teaching is that duration matters less than attention, and that a long sitting with a wandering mind is worth less than a short one with a still one.

There is no required posture and no required prayer, for the tradition holds that words disturb the surface they seek to reflect. Those who have sat for many years report that the practice becomes easier, not harder, and that the world after a morning sitting is clearer and more patient than the world before it. The elders describe the Light as something the soul recognises without having been taught, and they say that the practice succeeds in proportion to the willingness of the practitioner to set aside the noise of the small self.

## Rites of Passage

The faith marks its principal transitions with the same stillness it teaches at all other times. A child is brought to the water within the first month and held so that its gaze meets the surface, and the elders speak a soft blessing that the child will not understand and that the parents will remember. A marriage is solemnised by the two households at a chosen reach of water, and the two are permitted a moment alone at the surface before the witnesses join them. At death, the body is laid upon the water in the manner of the district, with a small offering of flowers or pollen set afloat, and the watchers sit in silence until the offering has drifted beyond sight.

These rites are conducted by the elders of the community, but the tradition has no formal clergy and recognises no single ordained order. The most senior practitioners are given the title of sitter-teacher and are charged with the instruction of the young and the resolution of disputes, but they hold no worldly authority and they continue to earn their living as do their neighbours. A community without an elder of sufficient standing will ask a neighbouring community to lend one for the larger rites, and the lending is held to be a bond of honour between the two.

## Houses of Stillness

The houses of worship of the faith are called still-houses, and they are built around a basin of still water that is fed and tended as carefully as a hearth in another tradition. The still-houses are simple in construction and lacking in ornamentation, for the teaching is that any excess draws the eye from the water that is the centre of the practice. The most ancient still-houses are said to be found in the lower river country, where the marsh communities first established them, and the most frequented is held to lie near the bay of [Sunmere](/content/location/settlement/city/sunmere), though the tradition is indifferent to questions of precedence among its houses.

The still-houses serve also as refuges for the weary and the bereaved, and they are open at all hours to any who seek a quiet place. The keeping of a still-house is considered a privilege rather than a duty, and the older practitioners take turns tending the basin and sweeping the floor in rotation. Visitors of other faiths are welcomed in the same manner, for the teaching extends its hospitality to all who enter in good faith, and the only behaviour that is politely refused is the making of disturbance at the basin by the uninvited.

## Relation to Other Traditions

The Light of the Still Water coexists with many other observances across the southern waters, and it is perhaps least like a religion in the usual sense, for it makes few claims upon the world beyond the practice of its sitters. The faithful are encouraged to honour whatever household faith their family observes, and they do not press the sitting upon those who have not sought it. In the coastal towns the practitioners of the Still Water are known to keep the rites of the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/the-faith-of-the-still-flame) alongside the morning sitting, and the two monotheisms are held by their respective teachers to be quiet cousins, each concerned with a different face of the same unutterable light.

Among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the southern woods the Still Water has found a sympathetic hearing, for the elven contemplative traditions honour stillness in their own manner and hold it to be a discipline of great antiquity. The faith asks little of those who do not seek it, and this restraint has permitted it to persist without becoming a point of contention between the peoples who share the southern waters. The chronicler notes that the marsh elders and the elven forest-keepers have exchanged visits more than once in recent generations, and that each has found in the other a kindred attention to the world without demand.

## Significance

The Light of the Still Water is a modest presence in the religious life of the known world, and it is perhaps for this modesty that it has endured. Its teaching asks of the practitioner only that they sit and watch, and it offers in return the promise that the watching itself is the prize. The still-houses have served for generations as places of quiet in districts where quiet is in short supply, and the daily sitting has given countless fisherfolk and river-keepers a discipline that orders the morning without commanding the day. In a world of many faiths and many claims, the Still Water remains the least demanding of the monotheist traditions, and its presence is felt less in the chronicles than in the surface of a pond at dawn, where a watcher sits and watches, and is content.
