## Overview

The Shield and Song is a syncretic monotheist tradition that joins the discipline of the warrior to the office of the singer, holding that the divine is known in two complementary ways: by the bearing of hardship in the defence of others, and by the maintenance of memory and of meaning through the arts of voice and instrument. Its followers are found across the temperate lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and along the sheltered coasts, particularly among the peoples whose histories are carried in song rather than in scroll. The order teaches a single divine Presence known by the title of the Watcher, and it maintains that the Watcher is honoured equally in the standing of the shield and the raising of the song.

## Founding among the Border Riders

The order traces its origin to a company of border riders who served the marches of the older Aethelaran kingdoms and who were accustomed, in their off-watch, to maintain the songs of their forebears and of the deeds they had seen. The traditions of the order speak of a woman called Vaelren of the Long Watch, who is held to have first bound the riders to the practice of singing the day's losses and the day's mercies into a single account at evening, so that neither the suffering of the one nor the goodness of the other might be forgotten. Whether Vaelren was a single figure or a composite of several riders is left to the chronicler's discretion, and the order is content that the binding should be remembered whether or not the binder can be named.

The order grew along the marches and the river country, taking root in market towns and in field camps alike, and it spread into the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) and the [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) without becoming a state faith of either. The order's relationship to the kingdoms has been one of long cooperation without absorption, and the kingdoms have valued the order for the discipline it has given the rural levies and for the steadying influence of the order's singers in troubled times.

## The Two Halves of the Calling

The doctrine of the order holds that the divine Watcher is revealed in two complementary aspects, which the order names the shield-half and the song-half. The shield-half is the bearing of hardship for the protection of others, the patience of the watcher at the wall, the carrying of the wounded from the field. The song-half is the keeping of memory, the recitation of the names of the dead, the maintenance of the meaning of the community's life through the arts of voice and instrument. The order teaches that neither half is complete without the other, for a defence that forgets its purpose becomes mere endurance, and a song that is unaccompanied by action becomes mere ornament.

The faithful are required, by custom rather than by formal vow, to attend to both halves of the calling in the measure of their station. A farmer who has not the training for the shield-half may yet keep the song-half by maintaining the local songs and the memory of the local dead. A soldier may attend to the shield-half in campaign and to the song-half in the evening, when the day's account is reviewed. The order does not require of its followers that they be equally skilled in both, but it does require that they regard both as essential to the community's life.

## Watch and Song

The central observance of the order is the evening review, which the faithful keep in their households or in their companies. At the close of the day, the household or the company recalls together the events of the day, giving thanks for the mercies and observing the losses. A short song of account is sung, naming the day's sick and recovered, the day's fed and unfed, the day's spared and the day's fallen. The order teaches that this review is essential to the community's memory, and that a household which neglects it loses, by degrees, the meaning of its own life.

The review is not a confession and not a punishment, and the order is careful to mark the difference. The sins of the day are observed but not adjudicated, and the remedy is held to lie in the change of conduct on the morrow rather than in any further observance at the evening. The order teaches that the review is meant to keep the account honest, so that the community may know what it has done and may be moved, in good time, to mend what has been done amiss.

## The Standing of the Watch

The shield-half of the calling is observed in the standing of the watch, which the order maintains at certain border crossings and certain sacred sites, and which any follower may take up for a season in service to the order. The standing of the watch is a discipline of exposure and patience, and the order teaches that the watcher who has stood through a long night has learned something that the day cannot teach. The standing is not a military service in the ordinary sense, but it has at times served the kingdoms in cooperation with their own garrisons, particularly at the crossings of the more remote border rivers.

A watcher who serves a season with the order is held to have earned the small mark of the standing, a hammered pin that is given without ceremony at the close of the season. The pin is worn at the cloak's shoulder, and it is a mark of respect rather than of rank. The order does not maintain a standing army and does not recruit, but it welcomes those who seek it, and the discipline of the watch is regarded by its practitioners as a steadying experience for any calling that demands endurance.

## The Singers and the Memory Houses

The song-half of the calling is observed chiefly in the houses of memory, the order's quiet establishments where the local songs and the local traditions are maintained. The houses of memory are found in the larger market towns and at the more important river crossings, and they are kept by the order's singers, who are recognised by the small wooden clasp they wear at the throat. The singers travel between the houses and between the orders of the faithful, carrying the songs from one district to the next, and they are regarded by the order as the keepers of its living memory.

A singer of the order is required to learn by heart a substantial body of songs and to maintain the accuracy of their transmission from one generation to the next. The tradition holds that a song learned imperfectly is a song lost, and the singers are trained accordingly. The order's houses of memory are open to all who seek a quiet place, and the singers are instructed to teach any who ask without charge, for the maintenance of the community's memory is held to be too important a duty to be turned into a trade.

## Rites of the Community

The order marks the major transitions with rites that join the shield-half and the song-half in a single observance. A child is presented to the order at a household review and given a small name-song, which the household thereafter sings at each evening review until the child is old enough to learn its own part. A marriage is solemnised by the singing of a binding song at the ceremony and by the joining of the two shields of the households in a public act of the standing. At death, the departed is named in the evening review and a song of release is sung by the household, and the order teaches that the name of the departed, once sung at the review, becomes part of the community's enduring memory.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Shield and Song has long coexisted with the other monotheist traditions of the continent, and it has been particularly close to the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame) and the [Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/forged-flame). The keepers of the Still Flame teach the tending of the household fire, and the Forged Flame teaches the discipline of the workshop, but the Shield and Song teaches the joining of hardship and memory, and it is the joining that the order regards as its contribution to the wider life of the continent's faithful.

Among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) the order has found a sympathetic, if cautious, hearing. The elven traditions of the long song are kindred to the order's houses of memory, and the two have exchanged visits and songs more than once in recent generations. The order regards such exchanges as a privilege rather than a claim, and it asks nothing of the elven people beyond the courtesy of the meeting. The chronicler notes that the order has been perhaps the least insular of the Aethelaran faiths, and that its mixture of the warrior and the singer has made it a natural companion of the continental roads.

## Significance

The Shield and Song endures as a working faith of the temperate lowlands, and its significance is felt in the market towns and the river country of Aethelar more than in the courts of the great. Its followers are patient of hardship and devoted to memory, and they have given to the rural communities they serve a discipline that joins endurance to meaning. The order asks of its practitioners that they bear what they are called to bear and that they sing what they are called to sing, and it promises them, in return, that the bearing and the singing are themselves a share in the Watcher's keeping of the world. In a tradition of many faiths and varied claims, the order stands for the joining of action and of memory, and the household that keeps the evening review is held, by the order, to keep something essential to the life of any community.
