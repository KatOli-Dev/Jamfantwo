## Overview

The Star-Keepers is a contemplative monotheist tradition of the night sky, held chiefly by the navigators, astronomers, and quiet scholars of the coastal cities and the inland observatories of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Its central teaching holds that the divine is most clearly known in the watching of a single star that does not move with the turning of the heavens, and that the keeper who attends to this star with patience will come to perceive, behind the noise of the shifting constellations, a corresponding stillness that the rest of the sky conceals. The faithful call the divine the Unmoving, and they hold that the Unmoving has set a single sign in the heavens to which the soul may attend when the world's brightness becomes too loud for quieter perception.

## Origins among the Harbour Meters

The keepers trace their origins to a small company of harbour meters who served the older ports of the eastern Aethelaran coast, where the determination of a ship's place at sea depended upon the careful measurement of the stars at the hour of the lower meridian. The meters taught that the night sky was a kind of veil, like the noise of the day, and that behind the veil a single star held its place without turning. Whether the meters were a single guild or a succession of senior practitioners is left open, and the keepers are content that the discipline should be remembered whatever the case may be.

The keepers spread along the trade routes of the coast and into the inland observatories that the astronomers of the river country were beginning to maintain, and the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) and the [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) both permitted the keeping of the practice within their borders without ever adopting it as a state observance. The keepers regarded the permission as a kindness and have never sought more, content that the trade routes and the observatories should provide enough of an audience for the discipline to be maintained.

## The Unmoving Star

The central doctrine holds that the divine is signified by a single star, fixed in the heavens, that does not share in the turning of the night sky and that may be found by the keeper who has the patience to attend to the turning. The teaching is not a literal instruction to look for a star of such and such a position, for the doctrine holds that the precise location of the star is known to the keeper who has discovered it and that the discovery is part of the discipline rather than a result the meter may report in advance. The elders speak of the unmoving star as the sign rather than the thing signified, and they counsel that the keeper should not mistake the sign for the Unmoving itself.

The keepers maintain a number of observatories in the older cities, where the night sky can be watched without the disturbance of the city's lamps, and the elders teach that the maintenance of these places is one of the chief labours of the discipline. The observatories are supported by the labour of the keepers and by the small offerings of the faithful of the coastal towns, and the keepers regard the support as a sign of the wider community's interest in the night sky. The senior keepers are given the title of watcher of the sign, and they are charged with the oversight of the observatories and the instruction of the novices.

## The Hour of the Lower Meridian

The principal observance of the keepers is the watch at the hour of the lower meridian, a discipline of attention undertaken in the small hours of the night when the turning of the heavens has brought the unmoving star to the lowest point of its passage across the sky. The watch is conducted in silence and in the dim red light of a small lamp that is screened from the observer's eye, and the keeper is permitted the company only of a senior watcher who attends to the discipline without conversation. The watch is not a punishment, and the keepers are careful to mark the difference, for the watch is held to be the chief instrument by which the unmoving star is discerned behind the shifting constellations.

A novice is admitted to the watch after a probation of some seasons at an observatory, during which the candidate learns the maps of the night sky and the methods by which the senior watchers distinguish the moving from the unmoving. The watch is undertaken at the observatory under the counsel of the senior watcher, and the keepers counsel the novice that the first watch is seldom the watch at which the unmoving star is perceived. The elders teach that the perception comes slowly and that the keeper should attend to the turning of the sky as it is, without grasping at the unmoving star before the discipline has ripened.

## The Reading of the Maps

The keepers maintain a tradition of careful cartography of the night sky, in which the positions of the principal stars and the turning of the heavens are set down in volumes that the observatories keep and compare. The tradition has given rise to a substantial body of astronomical work, and the keepers regard the cartography as a discipline of attention that complements the discipline of the watch. The elder keepers teach that the maps are a worthy guide to the novice but that the unmoving star cannot be found by the use of the maps alone, for the maps record the turning of the sky and not the unmoving within it.

The mapping is undertaken by the assistants of the observatories, who are charged with the labour of the observation and the record. The senior watchers counsel the assistants to attend to the distinction between the moving and the unmoving in their records, and the assistants are taught that a map that records only the moving is a faithful map of the night sky but an insufficient guide to the unmoving star. The keepers' tradition of careful cartography has been of use to the wider world, and the navigators of the strait and the oceans have benefitted from the maps that the observatories keep.

## The Discipline of the Coast

The keepers maintain a small presence in the harbour towns along the eastern Aethelaran coast, where the navigators of the longer voyages come to learn the methods of observation that the keepers have refined. The discipline of the coast is a shorter and more practical version of the observatory watch, and it is taught by harbour keepers who are appointed by the senior watchers of the nearest observatory. The harbour keepers counsel the navigators to attend to the difference between the moving and the unmoving in their nightly observations, and they teach that a navigator who has learned the discipline is better fitted to the determination of a ship's place than one who has not.

The discipline of the coast is not a matter of doctrine, and the keepers are careful to mark the difference between the practical instruction of the harbour and the contemplative watch of the observatory. A navigator who has been taught by the harbour keepers is not thereby a member of the keepers, and the keepers do not press the contemplative watch upon those whose calling is the sea rather than the sky. The arrangement has been a worthy one for the navigators and for the keepers, and the harbour discipline has served the wider world without disturbing the contemplative order of the observatories.

## Rites and Transitions

The keepers mark the major transitions with rites that are spare and quiet, and the elders counsel against any elaboration that might disturb the stillness the rites are meant to honour. A child is presented to the observatory within the first year and is held by the senior watcher at the unmoving lamp, and a small star-map is placed in the child's hand as a token of the calling. A novice is admitted to the order by being given a probation at the observatory and a senior watcher under whom to learn the watch, and the novice is permitted thereafter to attend the hour of the lower meridian as the discipline requires.

Marriage is not encouraged by the keepers, though it is permitted for those whose calling requires it, and the keepers counsel the married faithful to maintain their watches at the observatory in the seasons set apart for the discipline. Death is marked by the placing of the departed's star-map upon the records of the observatory and the observing of a brief watch in the departed's honour, and the senior watcher speaks briefly of the departed's watching before the records are closed. The keepers teach that the departed's watch has not ended but has passed into the common observation of the observatory, and the placing of the map upon the records is held to be the final act of the departed's calling.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Star-Keepers has long coexisted with the other contemplative traditions of the continent, and it has been particularly close to the [Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/unwritten-name), whose scholars maintain their own discipline of restraint and careful reading. The two paths are not the same, for the Doctrine is a path of careful reading in the cloister, while the Star-Keepers is a discipline of nightly watching at the observatory, but the teachers of each have been careful to mark the kinship between them. A senior reader of the Doctrine will sometimes attend the observatory watch, and a senior watcher will sometimes attend the conference of readers, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions.

The keepers have found a sympathetic hearing among the [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the strait, whose naturalists maintain their own careful records of the sky as seen from the waters. The merfolk scholars have exchanged star-maps with the keepers more than once in recent generations, and each has found in the other a kindred disposition to patient observation. The keepers regard this kinship as a privilege rather than a claim, and they ask nothing of the merfolk people beyond the courtesy of the exchange. The keepers have been among the most patient of the Aethelaran faiths, and their quiet attendance to the night sky has been a kind of lamp to the harbours and the oceans.

## Significance

The Star-Keepers is a contemplative presence in the religious life of Aethelar, and its significance is felt in the observatories and the harbour towns of the eastern coast more than in the courts of the great. Its practitioners are patient of watching and devoted to the long discipline of the meridian, and they have given to the wider world maps of the night sky that the navigators regard as among the most accurate they have. The keepers ask of their faithful that they attend to the difference between the moving and the unmoving in their nightly observation, and they offer in return the long watch by which the unmoving star is discerned and the world is set, in its silence, into the larger order of the sky. In a world of many faiths and many claims, the keepers remain the quiet attendance of those who have learned that the night holds a single sign, and the watcher who keeps the meridian is held, by the elders, to have set the watch without breaking the silence.
