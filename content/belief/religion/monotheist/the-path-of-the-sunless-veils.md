## Overview

The Path of the Sunless Veils is an ascetic and contemplative monotheist tradition held chiefly by the cloistered communities of the inland valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and along the quieter roads of the southern ranges. Its central teaching holds that the divine is hidden from view by the world's brightness and by the noise of its commerce, and that the soul must learn to veil itself from the world's distractions before it can attend to the dimmer light that the veils conceal. The faithful call the divine the Veiled One, and they hold that the Veiled One has wrapped the world in successive coverings, each of which must be lifted in its order by the practitioner who has the patience for the labour.

## Origins among the Inland Hermits

The path traces its origins to a community of hermits who withdrew from the older market towns of the central highlands and took up residence in the quiet valleys where the sun's rays reach only briefly in the winter months. The hermits taught that the world of bright surfaces and loud voices was a kind of veil, and that the soul which learned to be still would perceive, behind the veil, a corresponding stillness that was more ancient and more patient than any of the day's doings. The community's founders are remembered only by the title of the First Veilers, and whether the title belonged to one or to several is left to the chronicler's discretion, and the path is content that the teaching should be remembered whatever the case may be.

The path spread quietly through the inland valleys, and it found a hearing among the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the hill towns whose lives were already spare and whose disposition to solitude made the discipline of the veils a natural extension of the year's round. The path did not spread into the market cities with any speed, for the cities' brightness was the very veil the path counselled against, and the elders of the path were content with the slower growth. The [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) tolerated the path in its borders without adopting it, and the cloistered communities have regarded the kingdom's indifference as a kindness rather than a slight.

## The Successive Veils

The doctrine holds that the world is wrapped in a succession of coverings, each more subtle than the last, and that the practitioner's task is to learn to lift them in order. The first veil is the noise of the world's commerce, the busy traffic of news and of opinion that reaches the soul before it can attend to anything else. The second veil is the lure of the world's pleasure, the brightness of its appearance and the warmth of its regard, which holds the eye and prevents the soul from withdrawing. The third veil is the fear of the world's empty spaces, the dread of the quiet places and of the long still hours that the practice requires.

The elders teach that these three are the veils commonly spoken of, and that beyond them lie further veils that the practitioner will discover in the course of the long retreat. The doctrine holds that the veils are not to be rent but to be lifted, and that the soul which tries to break the coverings rather than gently remove them will find itself in a worse state than if it had not begun. The teaching is one of patience and of attention, and the elders counsel the novice to lift each veil as it presents itself, without grasping at veils yet unseen.

## The Cloister of Veils

The central observance of the path is the long retreat, a discipline of withdrawal that is undertaken at the cloister of veils for a season of weeks or months. The retreat is conducted in silence and in the dim light of a small chamber that is curtained against the day's brightness, and the practitioner is permitted the company only of a senior veiler who attends to the discipline without conversation. The retreat is not a punishment, and the path is careful to mark the difference. The retreat is held to be the chief instrument by which the veils are lifted in their order, and the elders regard it as the most demanding and the most rewarding of the path's disciplines.

A novice is admitted to the retreat after a probation of some seasons at the cloister, during which the candidate tends the small lamps and the curtains of the chambers and is instructed in the doctrine of the veils. The cloister is supported by the labour of the community and by the small offerings of the faithful of the hill towns, who regard the retreat as a service to the wider faith and who contribute accordingly. The senior veilers are given the title of keeper of the veils, and they are charged with the oversight of the retreats and the instruction of the novices, though they hold no worldly authority and they continue to maintain themselves by their hands.

## The Veiling of the Self

The path teaches that the lifting of the veils is impossible without a corresponding veiling of the self. The practitioner must learn to wrap the small self in a comparable obscurity, so that the noise and the brightness of the small self do not reach outward to replace the veils that have been lifted. The elders speak of this as the veiling of the self, and they counsel the novice that the discipline is to be taken slowly and without display. A practitioner who returns from the retreat and speaks at length of his or her experience is regarded by the elders as having missed the chief point, for the veiling of the self continues outward into the household and the workshop, and the practitioner who is unaware of this continuation has not yet completed the discipline.

The veiling of the self extends to the practitioner's dress and habit, for the path counsels simplicity in the clothing and a quietness in the speech. The faithful do not wear a special habit at all times, but they are accustomed to keep a simple cloak for the days of retreat and a quieter form of address when the discipline is uppermost. The elders caution against the temptation to make a display of the simplicity, for the path regards such a display as a kind of brightness, and brightness is the very veil the discipline counsels against.

## Rites of the Path

The path marks the major transitions with rites that are spare and unobtrusive, and the elders counsel against any elaboration that might bring the brightness of the world into the moment. A child is presented to the cloister within the first year and given a small veil of white linen, which the elders bless and return to the parents, who are charged to keep it until the child is old enough to use it in a first retreat. A novice is admitted to the cloister by being given a chamber and a senior veiler under whom to serve the retreat, and the novice is permitted thereafter to undertake the long retreat under counsel.

Marriage is not encouraged by the path, though it is permitted for those whose calling requires it, and the path counsels the married faithful to maintain their retreats at the cloister in the seasons set apart for the discipline. Death is marked by the placing of the departed's white veil upon the cairn of the cloister and the observing of a brief silence by the community in the departing's honour. The path teaches that the departed's veils have been lifted in their order and that the departed has at last entered the light that the veils conceal, and the placing of the veil upon the cairn is held to be the final act of the departed's calling.

## The Wanderers and the Quiet Houses

The path is served by a class of wandering veilers who travel between the cloisters and the hill towns, maintaining the discipline in the wider community and recruiting novices for the long retreat. The wanderers are recognised by the small white staff they carry and by their custom of speaking softly and at length only when the company requires it. The wandering office arose from the need to maintain contact between the cloisters and the wider community of the hill towns, whose distance from one another would otherwise have permitted the discipline to drift apart over the years.

The path maintains also a number of quiet houses in the hill towns, where the faithful of the surrounding countryside may make a brief retreat without undertaking the long withdrawal of the cloister. The quiet houses are humble establishments that lack the chamber and the silence of the cloister but provide a few days of quiet and counsel to those who cannot leave their households for long. The keepers of the quiet houses are appointed by the senior veilers of the nearest cloister, and the office is regarded by the path as a worthy complement to the long retreat.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Path of the Sunless Veils has long coexisted with the other contemplative traditions of the continent, and it has been particularly close to the [Doctrine of the Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/the-doctrine-of-the-unwritten-name), whose scholars maintain their own discipline of restraint. The two paths are not the same, for the Doctrine is a doctrine of careful reading in the cloister, while the Path of the Veils is a discipline of bodily retreat, but the teachers of each have been careful to mark the kinship between the disciplines. A senior reader of the Doctrine will sometimes undertake the long retreat at the cloister of the Veils, and a senior veiler will sometimes attend the conference of readers, and each is welcomed by the other with the courtesy of kindred traditions.

The path has found a sympathetic hearing among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the southern woods, who maintain their own disciplines of withdrawal in the deep forest. The elven contemplatives and the keepers of the veils have exchanged visits more than once in recent generations, and each has found in the other a kindred disposition. The path regards this kinship as a privilege rather than a claim, and it asks nothing of the elven people beyond the courtesy of the meeting. The chronicler notes that the path has been perhaps the most patient of the Aethelaran faiths, and that its slow growth in the hill country has been a quiet counterweight to the louder claims of the trading cities.

## Significance

The Path of the Sunless Veils endures as an ascetic presence in the religious life of Aethelar, and its significance is felt in the cloisters and the hill towns of the inland country more than in the courts of the great. Its practitioners are patient of withdrawal and devoted to the long discipline of the veils, and they have given to the hill communities they serve a quietness that the elders regard as the chief fruit of their keeping. The path asks of its faithful that they attend to the difference between lifting the veils and breaking them, and it offers in return the long retreat by which the lifting is learned and the soul is wrapped, at last, in the corresponding stillness of the Veiled One's own nature. In a world of many faiths and many claims, the path remains the quiet restraint of those who have learned that brightness is a covering and that what it conceals is more patient than the day.
