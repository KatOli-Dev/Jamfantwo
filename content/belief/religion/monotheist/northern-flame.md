## Overview

The Northern Flame is a monotheist tradition of hearth and cold country, held chiefly by the long-settled communities of the lowland valleys of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) and by the winter-keepers of the northern reaches of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Its central teaching holds that the divine is most clearly known in the steady keeping of a fire through a long night, and that the covenant between the keeper and the flame is a daily renewal of the larger covenant by which the world is sustained through its winters. The faithful call the divine the Covenant, and they hold that the Covenant has pledged itself to the world's continuance so long as the world's keepers keep their trust.

## Origins among the Long-Winter Peoples

The covenant traces its origins to the deep settlements of the Morvindic lowlands, where the winters are long and the fuel is dear and the survival of a household has always depended upon the careful tending of a single fire through the cold months. The elders of the covenant teach that the faith was first set down by a line of winter-keepers who taught their neighbours the discipline of the banking and the renewal, and who held the fire through seasons when the cold was so fierce that even the well-stocked households of the valleys feared for their stores. Whether the winter-keepers were a single line or a succession of elders is left to the chronicler's discretion, and the covenant is content that the discipline should be remembered whatever the case may be.

The covenant spread along the sheltered valleys of Morvind and into the northern lowlands of Aethelar, carried by settlers and by the quiet traffic of the winter roads rather than by any ambitious effort of mission. The [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) permitted the covenant within its borders without ever adopting it as a state observance, and the kingdoms of the southern Aethelaran lowlands regarded the covenant as a kindred tradition to the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame) of the river country. The covenant has found a sympathetic hearing among the [dwarven](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) communities of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), whose traditions of hearth and tunnel share the covenant's concerns.

## The Daily Banking

The central observance of the covenant is the daily banking, the careful evening practice by which the household's fire is reduced to a bed of embers that will hold through the night, and the morning practice by which the fire is renewed from the embers and brought up to a steady flame for the day's work. The banking is the chief instrument of the covenant's discipline, and the elders teach that a household which neglects the banking has neglected the larger covenant by which the world is sustained through its winters. The morning renewal is held to be a small replica of the world's renewal, and the elders counsel that the attention paid to the renewal is itself a form of prayer.

A household that keeps the covenant is expected to set aside a watch each evening, that the banking may be done with care and that the embers may be tended through the long hours of the night. The watch is the family's own, and the elders teach that the practice of the watch is one of the chief fruits of the covenant's discipline, for it joins the household together in a common labour that nothing else quite accomplishes. The watch is not a heavy one, and the elders counsel that the watching be shared among the household so that each learns the discipline and none is burdened beyond the strength of the night.

## The Covenant of the Long Winter

The doctrine holds that the divine has entered into a covenant with the keepers of the world, pledging to sustain the world through its winters so long as the keepers sustain their fires. The teaching is presented in the language of promise and of trust, and the elders are careful to mark that the covenant is not a transaction in which the keepers earn the world's continuance by their labour. The covenant is held to be a gift freely given and freely kept, and the keepers' part is not the cause of the world's survival but the daily acknowledgment that the survival rests upon a trust that the keepers do not control.

The doctrine teaches that a household which permits its fire to go out is not punished for the failure, for the covenant is a discipline of trust rather than of transaction, but the household is gently recalled to its labour by the elders of the community. The elders do not impose any formal censure upon the household, but they counsel that the next winter's banking be undertaken with greater attention and that the household accept from its neighbours the small help that the covenant expects its members to provide. The chronicler notes that this counsel has sometimes been mistaken by outsiders for a kind of social pressure, but the elders regard it as the natural consequence of a discipline that is shared rather than imposed.

## The Hearth-Halls of the North

The houses of the covenant are the hearth-halls of the north, the steady establishments where the larger community gathers to keep the covenant through the long winter. The hearth-halls are kept burning through every month of the cold season, and the elders teach that the maintenance of these fires is one of the chief labours of the covenant's discipline. The hearth-halls are supported by a small levy upon the households of the surrounding community, and the labour of the keeping is shared among the faithful according to a rotation that has been observed for many generations.

A hearth-keeper is appointed by the elders of the community from among the senior members of the covenant, and the office is regarded as one of the more demanding of the lay disciplines. The keeper is charged with the maintenance of the hall's fire and the instruction of the juniors, and the elders counsel that the keeper be a household of proven steadiness and known patience. The hearth-halls are open to all who seek refuge from the cold, and the covenant regards the keeping of this openness as one of its principal obligations.

## Rites and Transitions

The covenant marks the major transitions with rites that join the household and the hearth-hall in a single observance. A child is presented to the hearth-hall within the first year and is held by the keeper at the central fire, and the elders speak a brief blessing that the child will not understand and that the parents will remember. A novice is admitted to the covenant by being given a watch at the household fire and a senior keeper under whom to learn the banking, and the novice is permitted thereafter to share in the discipline of the hearth-hall.

A marriage between two members of the covenant is solemnised at the hearth-hall and by the joining of the two households' fires in a single new banking, and the keepers speak a brief blessing as the new fire is banked. Death is marked by the placing of the departed's banking-tongs upon the hearth of the household and the observing of a brief silence at the next morning's renewal, and the elders teach that the departed's covenant has not ended but has passed into the common keeping of the household. The practice of returning the tongs to the hearth is held to be one of the gentler of the covenant's customs, and the elders regard it as a fitting mark of the calling.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Northern Flame has long coexisted with the other monotheist traditions of the continent, and it has been particularly close to the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame), whose hearth-keepers teach a kindred discipline of the household fire. The two traditions are not the same, for the Still Flame is a faith of the temperate river country whose concerns are with the warmth and continuity of the home, while the Northern Flame is a faith of the long winter whose concerns are with the steady keeping of a fire through the cold months. The keepers of each tradition recognise the kinship and have exchanged visits more than once in recent generations.

Among the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) the covenant has found a sympathetic hearing, for the dwarven traditions of hearth and tunnel share the covenant's concern with the steady keeping of a flame through hardship. The dwarven master smiths have not formally adopted the covenant, but they honour it at the great hearths of the year, and a dwarven blade finished at one of these moments is considered to carry an uncommon steadiness. The chronicler notes that the covenant has been perhaps the steadier of the northern faiths, and that its quiet continuity through the long winters has been a kind of lamp to the surrounding country.

## Significance

The Northern Flame is a working faith of the long-settled lowland communities of the north, and its significance is felt in the hearth-halls and the households of the valley country more than in the courts of the leagues and the realms. Its practitioners are patient of the cold and devoted to the daily labour of the banking, and they have given to the lowland communities they serve a steadiness that the elders regard as the chief fruit of their keeping. The covenant asks of its faithful that they attend to the difference between the discipline of trust and the discipline of transaction, and it offers in return the long covenant by which the world is sustained through its winters and the household is sustained through the long night. In a world of many faiths and many claims, the covenant remains the steady banking of those who have learned that the renewal of the morning depends upon the trust of the night before, and the keeper who sets the embers with care is held, by the elders, to have spoken a word that the long winter will not silence.
