## Overview

The Faith of the Still Flame is among the most widespread of the monotheistic traditions observed across the known world, and it is chiefly practised by the settled [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) populations of the temperate river valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar). Its central teaching holds that behind the multitude of fires, lights, and hearths that warm the world there burns a single, undivided source, a Still Flame that is neither kindled nor extinguished by the hands of mortals. The faithful hold that all earthly fires are fragments of this one light, and that to tend a hearth with care is to honour the greater fire that sustains all living things. The faith is sober and unhurried in temper, concerned more with the quiet ordering of household and community than with spectacle or proclamation, and it has proved well suited to the agricultural peoples among whom it took root.

## Origins and Early History

The earliest certain records of the Still Flame are found among the chronicles of the river valleys of central Aethelar, where the faith appears already well established by the time the first great human kingdoms arose. Scholars of the faith trace its formative period to the long winters of the northern riverlands, when the keeping of a household fire through the cold months was a matter of survival as much as devotion. According to the traditions preserved by its elders, the faith was first set down by a line of hearth-keepers who travelled between the valleys teaching that the flame upon the hearth and the warmth of the body and the light of the sun were all of one nature. Whether these hearth-keepers were a single order or a succession of teachers is disputed, and the faithful themselves are content to leave the question open, holding that the truth of the Flame does not depend upon the name of its first messenger.

In the centuries that followed, the Still Flame spread along the river systems of Aethelar and into the coastal plains, carried by settlers and by the steady movement of peoples rather than by conquest. The [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael) and the [Kingdom of Aldreth](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-aldreth) both came to count the faith among the observances of their courts, though neither kingdom made it the sole religion of the realm, and the Flame has never been imposed by law upon those who hold to other ways. The faith reached [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) through trade and migration, where it found a modest following among the lowland communities, and it is known in scattered form along the eastern coasts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), though there it contends with older and more deeply rooted traditions of the southern cities.

## Doctrine of the Single Light

The doctrine of the Still Flame is contained in a body of teachings handed down by the hearth-keepers and recorded in plain volumes kept in the houses of worship. The central article is the unity of the light: that there is one Flame, without beginning and without end, and that the fires of the world are its reflections. The faithful are taught that the Flame is present in every hearth, every lamp, and every sunrise, and that to diminish a fire through carelessness is to slight the source itself. From this single article follow the lesser teachings that order the life of the believer, chief among them the duty of hospitality, the keeping of the household, and the respectful tending of the dead, whose passing is understood as the return of their inner flame to the undivided source.

The faith admits of no pantheon and recognises no lesser divinities, and it is in this singularity that the Still Flame is distinguished from the polytheistic observances of other peoples. The hearth-keepers teach that to seek the Flame in many gods is to mistake the reflections for the light, though they are careful to counsel respect toward those who hold otherwise, for the Flame is said to be patient with error and slow to anger. On matters of the world beyond the household, the doctrine is restrained, concerning itself chiefly with the conduct of the living rather than with elaborate accounts of the afterlife, and the faithful are instructed to dwell upon the flame before them rather than upon speculations they cannot test.

## Rites and Observance

The rites of the Still Flame are few and ordinary, and this simplicity is counted among its strengths. The chief observance is the kindling and tending of the household fire at the opening of each day, accompanied by a brief spoken thanksgiving that the flame has returned. At the close of day, the fire is not simply let to die but is banked with intention, that its embers may hold through the night, and the faithful regard the morning rekindling from living embers as a small replica of the world's renewal. Communities gather at the houses of worship upon the turning of the seasons, where a single great flame is maintained through the observance and portions of it are borne home by the gathered to relight their own hearths.

Marriage, birth, and death are each marked by a rite of the flame. A child is presented to the light within days of birth, and a marriage is solemnised before the shared hearth of the two households. At death, the body is given to the fire in the manner of the region, and the ashes are kept or scattered according to local custom, the act understood as the returning of the individual flame to its source. The hearth-keepers themselves undergo a longer training in the keeping of the great flames of the houses of worship, and they are charged with the instruction of the young and the maintenance of the records, though they hold no worldly authority and are expected to labour with their hands as do other folk.

## Houses of Worship and Centres

The houses of worship of the Still Flame are called hearth-halls, and they are built to hold a central fire that is kept burning without ceasing. They are plain structures of stone and timber, lacking the towers or ornamentation favoured by some other traditions, for the faith teaches that the Flame needs no adornment to be honoured. The greatest of the hearth-halls stand in the older river cities of Aethelar, and the most ancient is said to lie in the heart of the [Kingdom of Vael](/content/government/national/kingdom-of-vael), though the faithful dispute which hall may claim true precedence, and the question is of little moment to the practice of the faith. Travellers report hearth-halls of varying size in the towns of the southern plains and along the eastern Sahrani coast, each maintained by its local keepers according to the same plain discipline.

The hearth-halls serve also as places of refuge in hard weather and as centres of the duty of hospitality, for the faith holds that none who come to a flame in need should be turned away. It is common for the halls to keep a store of fuel and food against the winter, and the keeping of this store is among the foremost responsibilities of the keepers. In this practical charity the Still Flame finds its widest expression, and many who hold to other beliefs nonetheless speak well of the hearth-halls and the warmth they extend without condition of creed.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Still Flame has long coexisted with the polytheistic and animistic observances of the world's peoples, and its teachers have generally counselled tolerance, holding that the Flame is not diminished by the differing names others give to the light. In the river valleys and coastal plains of Aethelar, households are known to keep the rites of the Still Flame alongside local customs of the fields and the waters, and the hearth-keepers have not ordinarily objected, so long as the central teaching of the one light is not openly denied within their halls. The faith has at times been contrasted with the older pantheons of the plains and the [desert](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/the-spirits-of-the-kharesh), but such contrasts have rarely issued in strife, for the Flame's doctrine is quiet and its followers few in number relative to the populations among which they dwell.

Among the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) and the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves), the Still Flame has found little purchase, each people holding to its own deep-rooted ways, though individual dwarven and elven travellers have been known to honour a hearth-fire out of courtesy when guests in a human hall. The faith asks nothing of those who do not seek it, and this restraint has permitted it to persist across many centuries and many changes of realm without becoming a source of contention between the peoples of the world.

## Significance

The Faith of the Still Flame is a modest but enduring presence in the religious life of the known world, and its influence is felt less in the courts and the chronicles than in the daily keeping of the households of Aethelar and the lands beyond. Its teaching of the single light has given the settled agricultural peoples a unifying metaphor for the warmth and continuity of the home, and its houses of worship have served for generations as refuges, schools, and centres of neighbourly care. The faith asks little and promises little beyond the steady return of the flame each morning, and it is perhaps for this reason that it has outlasted grander and more demanding creeds. In a world of many peoples and many beliefs, the Still Flame endures as a quiet constant, tended as faithfully in the humblest cottage as in the greatest hearth-hall, and its light, however plainly regarded, is one that few of the riverlands would willingly see extinguished.
