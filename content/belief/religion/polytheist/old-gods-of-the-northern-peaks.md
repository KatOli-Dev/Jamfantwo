## Overview

The peoples of the cold interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) have long lived beneath the shadow of the high peaks, where the winters are long, the darkness deep, and the storms that sweep down from the summits can destroy the unprepared. The belief system that grew up among the highland communities of this region holds that the peaks themselves are the dwelling of ancient powers, and that the survival of those who occupy the high country depends upon keeping these powers favourably disposed. The principal deities are the Snow-Mother, who covers the mountains in winter and releases them in spring; the Wind-Walker, who governs the gales that scourge the upper slopes; and the Long-Night, the power of the polar darkness that descends upon the interior for weeks at a time. The dwarven high clans who hold the principal passes and the human communities of the snow-bound valleys are the chief adherents, and a smaller number of [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines) from the cold steppes have adopted portions of the observance.

The faith is austere, and the chroniclers note that its austerity is matched by the climate it describes. There are no elaborate temples or a complex priesthood; the shrines are the peaks themselves, the glaciers, and the high meadows where the communities graze their flocks during the brief summer. The rites are simple and tied directly to the survival needs of the highland communities: the protection of the watch, the safe movement of the flocks, the endurance of the long winter. The teaching holds that the cold interior is governed by powers that are neither generous nor cruel but exacting, and that the proper disposition toward these powers is one of sober respect rather than of fervent devotion.

## The Greater Powers of the Ice

The Snow-Mother is the first among the greater powers, the goddess of the snow that blankets the peaks and the ice that bridges the deep chasms. She is invoked at the first snowfall and at the last melting of the winter, and her festival falls at the midwinter, the darkest time of the year, when the people of the high interior most need the reassurance that the snow will not last forever. The Wind-Walker is the second, the god of the high winds and the great storms that descend from the peaks and sweep across the valleys. He is honoured at the height of the storm season, when the watchkeepers of the high country sound the alarms and the communities secure their shelter. The Long-Night is the third, the power of the polar darkness that holds the interior in unbroken night for the weeks of deep winter. The Long-Night is not feared but respected, for the faith teaches that the darkness is necessary to the renewal of the land and that the people who endure it with patience will be rewarded when the light returns.

Beneath these three are set the gods of the individual peaks, glaciers, and valleys, and the catalogue of these local powers is long. The dwarven clans maintain lists of the gods of the principal summits of the Frostwall Marches and the surrounding ranges, recording them alongside the names of the passes and the water sources that lie beneath each peak. A new name is added only when a summit has been known across several generations and the community can attest to its consistency, and the practice ensures that the local gods are those that the people have actually lived with rather than abstract inventions.

## The Long Winter and the Brief Summer

The religious year of the northern peaks is dominated by the winter, which occupies the greater part of the year in these latitudes. The observance of the winter is bound up with the custom of the long-night gathering, held at the principal hall of the high clan at the midwinter. The gathering is a multi-day event during which the stories of the past year are told, the deeds of the watch are recounted, and the young are taught the old songs that name the spirits of the peaks in order. The gathering is also the occasion for the settlement of disputes between the clans and for the formalisation of the seasonal movements of the flocks.

The brief summer occupies only a fraction of the year, and the rites of the summer are correspondingly simple. The rite of the first lambing marks the beginning of the high pastures, and it is conducted at the customary shrine of the valley where the flocks are brought to graze. The rite of the last snow falls at the end of the summer, when the herds are brought down from the high meadows and the watchkeepers prepare the passes for the return of the winter. The chronicler notes that the two rites of snow - the first and the last - form the pivot of the religious year, and their observance with strict regularity is held to be the foundation of the community's continuity through the cold months.

## The High Clans and the Watch

The [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) are the principal political and military entity of the cold interior, and the clans are inseparable from the faith. The watch is the central duty of the clans, and the watchkeepers of the high country are charged with the observation of signs in the sky and the snow that warn of approaching storms, of the movement of enemy parties, and of the conditions of the passes. The watch is conducted from the high points of the escarpment, and the signal fires that the clans maintain are the chief means of communication across the broken country. The [Frostwall Fire Signs](/content/language/pseudo/frostwall-fire-signs) are the code by which the watchkeepers convey information, and the code is observed with great strictness; the failure to maintain the signals at the customary hours is regarded not merely as a neglect of duty but as a breach of religious obligation, for the watch is the service of the community to the gods of the peaks.

The watchkeepers are drawn from the warrior class of the clans, but their role is more than martial. They are also tasked with the reading of the weather, and their knowledge of the sky and the patterns of the storm is considered sacred. The chronicler records that the stormwatchers of the high country have developed a highly refined understanding of meteorological phenomenon, and that their predictions are often relied upon by the lowland communities of the coasts, who have no comparable means of foreseeing the gales that may descend from the interior.

## The High Pasture and the Flocks

The flocks of the high interior are the economic foundation of the clans and of the human communities of the snow valleys, and the care of the flocks is a central religious duty. The flocks are moved to the high pastures at the beginning of the summer and brought down again before the first snowfall of the autumn. The movement is governed by a body of customary law that determines the order of the clans, the timing of the crossing of the passes, and the distribution of the grazing lands. The customary law is administered by the elders of the clans, and disputes over the grazing rights are settled at the gathering of the clans, which is held each year at the principal shrine of the high country.

The faith teaches that the high pasture is the inheritance of the clans and the snow-valley communities in common, and that no single clan or household has the right to monopolise it. The chronicler notes that this principle has prevented the kind of territorial conflicts that have destroyed other pastoral communities, and that the steady succession of herds across the high meadows has maintained the fertility of the pastures for centuries. The rite of the first lambing is the chief observance connected with the flocks, and the lambing is blessed by the elder who leads the procession to the high pasture. The first wool of the season and the first milk are offered at the shrine, and the offering is held to ensure the fertility of the herd for the coming year.

## The Dwarven Dimension

The dwarven high clans are the principal adherents of the Old Gods of the Northern Peaks, and their observance of the faith is closely tied to the [Old Gods of the Mountains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-mountains), the dwarven reverence for the stone. The two faiths are not contradictory; the gods of the ice are the powers of the surface of the cold interior, and the gods of the stone are the powers of the rock beneath the ice. The dwarven communities honour both sets of powers, and the chronicler records that the dual observance is considered the mark of the complete dwarf. The gods of the ice and the gods of the stone are held to be kin, for both are powers of the mountain, one above ground and one below, and the dwarven clans maintain that a community that honours only one cannot fully understand the nature of the mountain.

The dwarven contribution to the high country extends beyond religious practice. The dwarven high clans are the principal builders of the passes and the bridges of the cold interior, and the road networks that connect the remote valleys to the principal halls of the Frostwall Clans are largely the product of dwarven engineering. The chronicler notes that the maintenance of these roads is funded jointly by the clans and by the tolls levied upon the caravans that use them, and that the tolls are considered a form of payment to the gods of the peaks for the safe passage they provide.

## The Lupines and the Hunt

The [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines) of the cold steppes are the principal non-dwarven adherents of the faith, and their observance is adapted to the life of the hunt rather than the life of the high pasture. The lupine communities are hunters of the high land, and the hunt is central to their way of life. The lupine elders observe the rite of the first kill each season, offering the first portion of the prey to the shrine of the peak that governs their territory. The faith holds that the land provides the game, and that a hunter who takes more than is needed or who wastes the flesh of the quarries offends the Snow-Mother.

The relationship between the lupine hunters and the dwarven high clans has been generally peaceful, and the chronicler records several instances in which the lupines have warned the clans of the approaches of large herds of game or of the movement of enemy parties. This mutual assistance has been formalised through customary agreements that govern the sharing of hunting grounds and the protection of the passes during the hunt season. The lupines do not observe the full rites of the northern peaks; they take only those portions of the faith that are relevant to the life of the hunter, and the dwarven clans accept this without objection. The chronicler attributes the peaceful coexistence of the two peoples in part to the recognition that each is the master of its own domain and that the high country accommodates both the herder and the hunter.

## The Sacred Peaks and the Forbidden Places

The highest summits of the cold interior are considered the dwelling of the gods in their most immediate form, and the principal of these peaks are sacred centres of pilgrimage. The pilgrimage to the summit of a sacred peak is arduous and dangerous even in good weather, and the chronicler records that several pilgrims have lost their lives attempting the ascent. The pilgrimage is held to be a test of devotion, and those who complete it are regarded by the community as having received the blessing of the gods. The forbidden places are the highest peaks, those where the gods are held to dwell in their most terrible form, and these are not visited at all. The chronicler notes that the distinction between the sacred and the forbidden peaks is maintained through longstanding oral tradition, and that the boundary is respected even by those clans that do not make pilgrimages to the lesser summits.

## Relation to Other Beliefs

The Old Gods of the Northern Peaks is a local faith of the cold interior, and it does not claim universal authority. The dwarven clans who practice it have long traded with the lowland communities of the coasts, and the traders observe the customs of the lowlands when they travel there, honouring the gods of those lands in the manner that the lowland peoples themselves observe. The monotheistic missionaries who have visited the high country have made little progress among the dwarven clans, for the faith of the stone and the faith of the single light are sufficiently different in emphasis that the dwarves find little common ground, though the chronicler notes that no hostility has resulted from the encounter. Among the human communities of the snow valleys, the conversion to the Still Flame has been more successful, partly because the human peoples of the interior are less bound to the dwarven tradition of the stone and partly because the austere character of the Still Flame appeals to a people accustomed to austerity.

The faith shares certain practical customs with the [Old Gods of the Mountains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-mountains) of the stoneworkers, particularly the reverence for the mountain and the custom of leaving offerings at the summit. But the two traditions have developed along separate lines, for the northern peaks are a distinct region with a distinct climate and a distinct people, and the faith has adapted accordingly to the specific conditions of the high interior.

## Significance

The Old Gods of the Northern Peaks is the working faith of the peoples of the cold interior, austere and practical, concerned with the immediate tasks of survival in a harsh environment. It has endured in the high country where other faiths have failed, not because of the brilliance of its doctrine but because of its close fit to the life of the people who practice it. The watchkeepers of the high peaks, the herders of the high pastures, the hunters of the cold steppes - all find in the Old Gods a set of beliefs that reflects the reality of their daily existence and offers guidance that is directly applicable to it. In a world of elaborate theology and competing creeds, the faith of the northern peaks remains grounded in the snow and the stone, and its endurance is a testament to the power of belief shaped entirely by its landscape.
