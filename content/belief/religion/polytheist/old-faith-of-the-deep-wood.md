## Overview

The ancient temperate woodlands of the known world are inhabited and managed by the elven people, and their way of life is inseparable from a set of beliefs concerning the forest and its powers. These beliefs have no single name among the elves themselves, who simply refer to them as the old way, but chroniclers call them the Old Faith of the Deep Wood. At its centre is the Forest Mother, the living presence of the wood, who is held to be the source of the trees, the streams, and the creatures that dwell within the canopy and the understory. Beneath her are counted the spirits of particular trees, of moss and fern, of river and clear-cut, and of the small animals whose daily labours shape the forest floor.

The faith is not organised around a priesthood or a fixed liturgy. It is transmitted through the daily life of the elven communities, in the work of the foresters, the knowledge of the elders, and the quiet observances that mark the turning of the seasons. Every elven settlement in the temperate woodlands carries a form of it, though the exact practices differ from one woodland to another according to local conditions. The chroniclers note that the most complete forms are preserved in the [Aethelar Heartwood](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/aethelar-heartwood), where the elder communities have maintained the faith in continuity with the earliest known elven settlements on the continent.

## The Living Wood

The forest is not treated as a resource but as a living entity with its own purposes and rights. The Forest Mother is conceived as the encompassing presence of the wood itself, present in every tree, every stream, every stretch of fallen log covered in moss. She is honoured at the turning of the seasons, and her festival falls at the unfolding of the first leaves in spring. The eldest member of each community conducts rites at the great trees that define the boundaries of the settlement, acknowledging that the trees are not merely shelter but kin.

Beneath the Forest Mother are set the specific powers of individual trees and plants. The god of the oak, the goddess of the silver elm, the spirit of the ironwood - each is recognised in the groves where that tree predominates. The spirits of the streams, the mosses, and the ground cover are localised to the particular hollows and ridges where they dwell, and their names are maintained by the elders in lists that are updated only reluctantly. A new name is added only after the spirit has been known across several generations, and the practice ensures that the catalogue of lesser powers grows slowly and carefully.

## Rites of the Forest Year

The elven calendar follows the forest cycle rather than the agricultural year. The rite of the leafing marks the spring renewal and is the principal festival of the faith. The elders gather in the principal clearing of the woodland and sing the old songs that name the spirits of the forest in order, from the canopy trees down to the mosses. The rite of the flowering celebrates the spring ephemerals that carpet the forest floor before the canopy closes above them, and it is a quieter observance attended mainly by women and children. The rite of the fruiting honours the mast - acorns, beechnuts, and other tree seeds that fall in autumn and sustain the winter. The rite of the leaf-fall marks the return of the year's growth to the soil.

A distinctive practice is the presentation of the young to the sapling, performed when a child reaches the age of understanding. The eldest member of the community leads the child to a young tree and asks the Forest Mother to receive the child as a new growth of the wood. The child touches the bark and receives a small token - a leaf pressed between flat stones or a fragment of bark from a fallen branch - that is kept for life. The chronicler records that this practice strengthens the emotional tie between the individual and the forest, and it is cited as one of the reasons elven communities show such remarkable continuity within a single woodland across many generations.

## Stewardship as Religious Practice

The management of the forest is itself a form of devotion. The elves do not clear land in the manner of the human farmers, and they do not fell living trees for timber except in the most limited circumstances and with formal rites of thanks. They gather fallen wood for fuel and fallen branches for the crafting of tools, and they shape the growth of the forest through selective planting and the careful pruning of valuable trees. The result of this patient stewardship is a woodland that is richer and more diverse than any purely natural forest. A visit to an elven-managed grove reveals a density of life that is difficult to match in untouched areas, for the elves understand that a healthy forest requires both continuity and change, and they manage accordingly.

The chronicler observes that the elven approach to forestry has no precise equivalent among other peoples. The human foresters of the surrounding lowlands take timber by cutting and replanting, and the dwarven quarries of the mountain edges remove stone in quantity. The elves take only what falls and shape what grows, a method that demands patience and a long view of the landscape. The faith teaches that the forest is a trust, not a possession, and the elves who hold to this teaching maintain their settlements in the wood for centuries without exhausting their surroundings.

## The Ancient Trees and the Old Groves

The greatest expressions of the faith are the ancient trees and the old groves of the central heartwood. The ancient trees are the eldest of the canopy species, oaks and elms and ironwoods that have stood for centuries and perhaps millennia. They are held to be the pillars of the forest and sometimes the dwelling of the Forest Mother herself. The old groves are the untouched remnants of the original woodland, places where the canopy has never been broken and the understory has developed according to its own law. These are the sacred centres of the faith, and the elders of every elven community make pilgrimages to the principal old groves at the turning of the year.

There is no question among the elves that the ancient trees and old groves belong to all their kind in common. The communities of the central heartwood have agreed through long custom that no tree within the principal groves may be felled and no old growth disturbed, and this agreement is considered as fundamental as any law. The chronicler notes that the preservation of these areas has made the Aethelar Heartwood the most extensive and oldest continuous temperate forest in the known world, and that the elves' attachment to the old wood is one of the defining features of their civilisation.

## The Forest Edge and the Human Settlements

The boundary between the elven forest and the human settlements of the lowlands is a zone of considerable interaction. The human woodcutters and farmers of the forest edge trade with the elves for rare woods, medicinal herbs, and crafted goods, and the elves permit the humans to harvest fallen timber and gather surplus mast within the agreed boundaries. The relationship is governed by a body of customary law that has been refined over centuries and that balances elven protection of the deep wood with human need for resources. Breaches of this law - the cutting of protected trees, the overharvesting of medicinal herbs - have been the occasion of dispute and, on occasion, of conflict, but chroniclers record that most disagreements are settled through formal negotiation between the elders of the forest and the local human authorities.

The [Sylvan Court](/content/government/local/sylvan-court) is the principal governance body of the elven forest, and its authority extends to the regulation of the woodland and the mediation of disputes between the elves and the surrounding communities. The court does not maintain military force; its power derives from tradition, from the strategic knowledge of the terrain, and from the willingness of the surrounding human settlements to negotiate rather than provoke the elves into defensive action. The chronicler notes that the Sylvan Court is the oldest continuous political entity in Aethelar, and that its stability is largely attributable to the shared interest of the elven and human communities in preserving the boundary.

## Elves Beyond the Central Woodlands

The Old Faith of the Deep Wood is maintained by elven communities far beyond the central heartwood. The communities of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) observe it in a form adapted to the cold northern forests, where the trees are fewer and the winter is longer. The elven settlements of the [Thessaran Basin](/content/location/settlement/region/thessaran-basin) follow a version suited to the warm lowland forests of the south, with denser canopy and a greater range of species. The chronicler records that these scattered communities maintain a quiet correspondence with the central heartwood, and that the elders of the remote communities pilgrimage to Aethelar at the turning of the year to confirm the customary observances and to discuss any emerging disputes.

This network of correspondence has preserved the essential unity of the faith across great geographical distances, and it has allowed different communities to share knowledge about forest management, medicinal plants, and the movements of game without compromising local adaptation. The chronicler attributes to this system much of the durability of the elven presence in the temperate forests of the known world.

## Relation to Other Beliefs

The Old Faith of the Deep Wood does not claim universality, and the elves do not seek converts among the human, dwarven, or other peoples who dwell near their woodlands. The elf approach is one of guarded coexistence, in which the faith's teachings are kept within the community but its practical benefits - the maintenance of a healthy forest, the regulation of hunting and gathering - are shared with neighbouring settlements. The monotheistic missionaries who have visited the elven communities have been received politely but have found little ground for argument, for the elves do not deny the singular light that the missionaries speak of; they simply find it irrelevant to the life of the forest.

The faith shares certain animistic assumptions with the [Old Gods of the Mountains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-gods-of-the-mountains) of the dwarves, and the elves and dwarves have developed a quiet understanding about their shared mountain and forest territories. The chronicler notes that neither people tries to impose their beliefs on the other, and that the boundary between the forest and the quarry is maintained with mutual respect. The faith is more distant from the [Faith of the Sea Mother](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-sea-mother) and the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), which are concerned with water rather than wood, but the different water-faiths and the deep wood faith observe the same principle of local adherence: each power governs its own element, and no one claims authority beyond its domain.

## Significance

The Old Faith of the Deep Wood is inseparable from the identity of the elven people and the preservation of the ancient temperate forests. It is a faith without hierarchy or doctrine, sustained by daily practice and by the patient labour of the foresters who manage the woodlands they consider their charge. Its practical outcome - the maintenance of some of the most biodiverse and oldest continuous forests in the known world - stands as the chronicler's strongest testimony to the wisdom of its teachings. Where other peoples see nature as something to be conquered or exploited, the elves see a living trust, and their forests endure because of that vision. The faith is not universal, and the elves do not expect it to be; it is the old way of the deep wood, and as long as the great trees stand, it will endure with them.
