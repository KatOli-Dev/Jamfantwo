## Overview

The farming communities scattered across the temperate and warm lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the agricultural districts of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) have long observed a body of belief concerned with the sun, the rain, the grain, and the soil. Chroniclers refer to this body of belief as the Wheel of the Sun and Field, a name that reflects its central metaphor: the agricultural cycle as the turning of a great wheel that determines the prosperity and the survival of every community that depends upon the harvest. The principal powers of the faith are the Sun-Walker, governing the light that ripens the crop; the Rain-Bringer, governing the water that nourishes it; and the Earth-Mother, governing the soil that receives the seed. Beneath them are the gods of the particular crops, the soil, and the seasonal weather, each acknowledged by the farmers of the fields where that power is most relevant.

The faith is the inheritance of the rural communities rather than of any learned or priestly class. It is transmitted through seasonal custom, through the practical instructions passed from elder to youth at the sowing and the reaping, and through the rites conducted by the village elder at the customary shrine. The faithful are almost entirely human, though a few of the pastoral peoples of the adjacent grasslands have adopted fragments of the observance, particularly the custom of setting aside the first sheaf as an offering and the practice of storing grain for the lean season as an act of religious duty. There is no scripture, for the fields themselves are regarded as the text, and no temple in the sense of a dedicated building; the village shrine - a marked stone, an old tree, or a spring beside which the community has long gathered - serves as the focus for the rites.

## The Powers of the Agricultural Year

The Sun-Walker is the first among the greater powers, the god of the sunlight that is essential to every crop and to the warmth of the growing season. The Rain-Bringer is second, the power of the rain and the dew that sustains the seed through the early months of its growth. The Earth-Mother is the third, the goddess of the soil and the furrow and the harvest that closes the cycle. These three are acknowledged collectively at the village shrines at the turning of the seasons, but each also has her or his own day of particular observance: the Sun-Walker at the summer solstice, the Rain-Bringer at the onset of the wet season, the Earth-Mother at the harvest.

Beneath them are counted the gods of the individual crops, the soil types, and the weather patterns that govern each particular region. The god of wheat, the goddess of the barley, the spirit of the lentil, the guardian of the dry soil, the warden of the wet - the catalogue is long and localised. The elders of the more particular communities maintain lists of these powers in small books kept by the shrine, and new names are added only when a crop or a soil type has been cultivated in a region for several generations. The practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that to name a power without having lived with its effects is to invite neither favour nor misfortune but only the confusion of the young.

## The Rites of the Agricultural Cycle

The religious year follows the agricultural cycle, and the principal rites are fixed to the sowing and the reaping of the grain. The rite of the first ploughing marks the opening of the soil in spring, and it is conducted by the village elder at the principal field of the community. The first furrow is turned as the elder speaks the traditional words, asking the Earth-Mother to receive the seed and the Sun-Walker to ripen it. The rite of the sowing follows shortly after, when the seed is cast into the furrow, and the community gathers to offer the blessing of the harvest before the first green shoot appears.

The green tide of late spring, when the first crops push above the soil, is marked by the rite of renewal, a quiet observance attended by the women and the children of the village and focused upon the blessing of the growing crop. The harvest festival is the principal observance of the faith and the occasion of the greatest communal gathering. The first sheaf is cut with special care, carried in procession to the village shrine, and left as an offering. The last sheaf is set aside for the poor of the village, a custom held to represent the gods' own share of the harvest.

## The Storehouse and the Duty of Restraint

The storehouse is the practical heart of the faith, and the custom of laying up grain against the famine is considered a religious obligation. The community is expected to set aside a portion of each harvest in a common store, to be drawn upon in the bad years or distributed to those who have lost their own grain to drought or flood. The store is administered by the village elder, and the chronicler records that communities that have observed this custom faithfully have survived famines that destroyed those that relied solely upon the goodwill of their neighbours. The faith teaches that the storehouse is the gods' provision for the lean season, and the neglect of the store is regarded as a breach of duty to both the divine and the community.

A related teaching is the restraint in the taking of the harvest. The faith holds that the gods lend the land their bounty for the keeping of the people, not as an unqualified gift, and that to take the last seed or to sell the grain of the poor for profit is to offend the Earth-Mother. The customary law of the farming communities generally incorporates this teaching, and the laws of several villages in [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) specifically forbid the sale of seed grain for the purpose of ensuring that each household retains enough to sow the following year.

## The Village and the Shrine

The village is the normal unit of organisation for the faith, and the shrine is the focal point of communal life. The shrine is not a building in the formal sense; it is a marked place, usually a standing stone or an ancient tree or a spring, at the edge of the cultivated land where the community may gather to conduct the seasonal rites. The shrine is tended by the elders, and the tending is a responsibility shared among the households of the village. In some regions the shrine is simply the oldest tree in the field, marked by a circle of stones and known by the name of the particular power that is held to dwell there. In others it is a stone erected at the boundary of the common land, serving also as a marker in disputes over property.

The shrine is not a place of private devotion but of communal observance, and the rites conducted there are always attended by the whole community. The chronicler notes that this communal character is one of the reasons the faith has endured: it is not held by a class apart but lived by everyone who tills the soil, and the elder who leads the rites is not a priest in the sense of a separate caste but the most experienced member of the village chosen for his knowledge of the seasons and the land.

## The Fairs and the Markets

The Wheel of the Sun and Field is also the faith of the fairs and the markets of the farming communities, and the fairs are the principal occasions for the exchange of grain and other agricultural goods between neighbouring villages. The fairs are held at the customary seasons, typically after the harvest and before the storing of the grain, and they serve both the practical function of trade and the religious one of reaffirming the bonds between the communities that share the same cycle of seasons. The markets of the larger villages and the nearby towns are more frequent and are less formal in character, but they too are held beneath the blessing of the gods of the field, and the fairer dealing is considered a matter of religious obligation.

The chronicler records that the fairs have also served as occasions for the exchange of knowledge between the farming communities, as the seed varieties, soil management techniques, and irrigation methods of one region are shared with those of another. This exchange has done much to improve agricultural productivity across the lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the chronicler credits the fair system with spreading such innovations more rapidly than would have been possible through isolated village practice alone.

## The Year of the Wheel

The religious year of the Wheel is the agricultural year, observed in every season and in every farming region of the known world. Spring is the season of the first ploughing and the sowing, and its festivals are the most numerous and the most closely observed. Summer is the season of the green tide and the ripening, and the summer festivals are quieter, focusing upon the blessing of the growing crop and the maintenance of the storehouse. Autumn is the season of the harvest, and the harvest festival is the principal celebration, attended by the communities of the surrounding area as well as by the village itself. Winter is the season of the rest, when the fields lie fallow and the communal labour is directed toward repair of the buildings, the tools, and the fences, and the long nights are given to the telling of the old stories and the keeping of the community's memory.

The cycle is not observed in identical form in every region. The farmers of the northern lowlands have a shorter summer and a longer winter, and their rites of the harvest are compressed into the weeks of ripening. The farmers of the southern lowlands enjoy a milder winter and a longer growing season, and their rite of the sowing is extended across a broader period of preparation. The faith holds that the cycle is one in substance and many in form, reflecting the adaptation of a single metaphor to the varied climates of the farming world.

## Relation to Other Beliefs

The Wheel of the Sun and Field is held by the farming communities as the faith of the cultivated land, and the faith does not claim authority over the grazing lands or the deep wood or the mountains. The farmers who trade with the pastoral peoples of the grasslands observe the customs of the [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-pantheon-of-the-plains) when they travel among them, and the faith makes no objection. The monotheistic teachers of the [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame) who have visited the farming villages have found the faith accessible but distinct, and the chronicler records that in several villages the households have adopted the rites of the Still Flame alongside the older observances of the field, treating the two traditions as complementary rather than contradictory.

The faith shares certain practical customs with the [Faith of the River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/faith-of-the-river-mouths), particularly the recognition of seasonal closure for the protection of breeding stock, and the two faiths have developed a quiet understanding between the river communities and the farming villages that share the same floodplains. The chronicler notes that the Wheel does not contend with the other polytheistic faiths in a doctrinal sense; each is understood to govern its own domain, and the farmers accept the authority of the gods of the mountains and the gods of the water in their respective territories without seeking to extend the authority of the Sun-Walker, the Rain-Bringer, or the Earth-Mother beyond the field.

## Significance

The Wheel of the Sun and Field is the working faith of the farming communities of the temperate and warm lowlands, and its influence is felt not in grand temples or theological treatises but in the daily rhythm of the sowing and the reaping. Its emphasis on the storehouse and the duty of restraint in the taking of the harvest has contributed materially to the resilience of the rural communities it serves, and its communal character has helped bind the scattered villages into a web of mutual obligation and shared practice. The chronicler sees in the faith one of the most enduring and least displaced of the religious traditions of the known world, sustained not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the farmers who depend upon it for their livelihood and their livelihood's continuation in the years to come.
