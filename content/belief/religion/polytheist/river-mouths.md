## Overview

The great rivers of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), their tributaries, and the estuaries where they meet the sea have long served as the arteries of travel, trade, and sustenance for the peoples who dwell along their banks. The faith that grew up among these river communities is centred on the [Potamia](/content/belief/deity/potamia), the goddess of the moving water that carries the silt that fertilises the fields and the fish that feed the villages. Beneath her are set the powers of the estuary, where the fresh and the salt water mingle; the flood-bringer, who brings the spring waters down from the high country; and the spirits of the particular rivers, tributaries, and fish species that sustain each local community.

The adherents of the faith are predominantly human settlements along the riverbanks, but several other peoples also observe portions of it. The [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers maintain their own form of the observance, the [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the interior waters have adapted it to the still waters of the lake and pond, and the [lacertians](/content/species/sapient/lacertians) of the wetlands honour the spirit of the marsh at the edge of the river. The faith is transmitted through the river communities rather than through a separate caste, and the elder of each village conducts the seasonal rites at the customary ford or shrine. There is no central authority or single scripture; the river itself is the text, and the knowledge of the elders is the teaching.

## The Powers of the Moving Water

The Potamia is the principal power, conceived as the living presence of the water itself, the flow from the high country to the sea. She is invoked at the flood and at the drought, and her festival falls at the height of the spring runoff when the rivers are at their fullest and the fish are running upstream to spawn. The [Estuary Walker](/content/belief/deity/estuary-walker) is the second, the god of the brackish water where the river meets the sea, and he is honoured at the turning of the tide by the fisherfolk who operate in the shallows. The [Deluge](/content/belief/deity/deluge) is the third, the power of the high water that descends in the spring and can either fertilise the fields or destroy them, depending upon its intensity and duration. He is honoured with a combination of gratitude and caution, for the flood is both necessary and dangerous.

Beneath these are counted the spirits of the individual rivers, the tributaries, and the principal species of fish. The river of a given valley has its own spirit, and the communities of that valley maintain the name and the customary offerings in small books kept by the elder. The spirits of the fish are localised to the species that are most important for the diet of the community - the trout, the salmon, the pike, the carp depending on the region. This localisation is both a strength and a source of occasional conflict between neighbouring villages that share a river but differ in the dominant species.

## Rites of the Water

The religious year follows the river cycle. The rite of the first flood marks the breaking of the winter snow in the high country and the descent of the high water down the valley. The elder stands at the principal ford and speaks the traditional words, asking the Potamia to carry the flood gently and to water the fields without destroying them. The rite of the spawning is conducted when the principal fish species begin their run upriver, and it is the duty of the fisherfolk of the community to attend the rite before the net is set for the season. The harvest of the river is the principal festival, celebrated at the drawing of the nets in the late summer, when the community gathers at the riverbank to offer the first fish caught at the season to the Potamia.

A distinctive observance is the rite of the closed waters, a seasonal closure period during which fishing is prohibited to protect the breeding stock of the principal species. The closure is enforced by the customary law of the river communities as a religious obligation, and the river communities that have adhered to the closure have maintained richer fish stocks over generations than those that have abandoned it. The closure is not absolute - the elders may grant permits for the taking of fish from certain species during the closure period if the community faces hunger, but the granting of such permits is rare and communities who grant permits too freely often find the fish stocks depleted in subsequent years.

## The Boatbuilders and the Netmakers

The boatbuilders and the netmakers of the river communities are central to the faith, for their crafts are the means by which the river's bounty is drawn to shore. The boats of the river communities take many forms: the dugout for the shallows, the longboat for the open water, the barge for carrying cargo, and the small ferry craft for crossing. Each boat is blessed by the elder of the community at the launching, and the blessing is a formal acknowledgement that the boat is a gift of the Potamia and that the builder is her instrument. The nets are blessed at the time of their weaving as well, and the netmaker is required to observe the customary words as each knot is tied, believing that the care of the maker is passed to the net in the water.

The boatbuilding and netmaking traditions are closely guarded secrets of the families who practice them, and the knowledge is passed from parent to child in a manner similar to the transmission of the faith itself. The boats and nets are maintained through the seasons, and the repair of a damaged net or the re-caulking of a leaking boat is accompanied by a small rite of renewal, acknowledging that the craft, like the river, is always in motion and always requiring attention.

## The Ford and the Bridge

The crossings of the river are the principal points of exchange between the communities on the two banks, and the fords and the bridges are sacred to the faith. The fords are the natural shallow places where the river may be crossed without the aid of a structure, and the bridges are the principal works of the river communities, built to carry traffic across the wider reaches of the main channel. Both the ford and the bridge are blessed at their opening by the elder of the community, and the blessing is the formal acknowledgment that the crossing is the inheritance of the community and the gift of the Potamia.

The fords and bridges have long been maintained by common labour between the communities of the two banks, and this common labour is the foundation of the customary law of the river. The maintenance of the ferry services, the payment of tolls, and the management of the waters are all governed by agreements that have been negotiated between the communities on either side, and the agreements are renewed at the seasonal gatherings of the river peoples. The faith teaches that the crossing is a shared trust, and that no community on the river has the right to deny passage to another without causing harm to the Potamia herself.

## The Estuary and the Meeting of the Waters

The estuary is the principal temple of the faith, and the estuaries of the great rivers are the gathering points of the river communities at the turning of the season. The principal estuaries of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the coastal regions of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) serve as the centres of the joint observance between the river peoples and the seafaring communities, and the estuary is understood as the dwelling of the Estuary Walker. The estuary is not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for it is the natural dwelling of the divine, and the rites are conducted upon the shore and in the shallow water of the estuary with the customary observances.

The estuary is also the meeting-place of the river peoples and the seafaring peoples, and the exchange that takes place there is the foundation of the wider trade of the region. The fisherfolk of the river meet the fisherfolk of the sea at the estuary, and the meeting is the occasion of the exchange of the harvest of the river for the harvest of the sea. The joint management of the estuary fishing grounds is one of the most practical applications of the faith, for it requires the river communities and the sea communities to share knowledge about the habits of the fish, the patterns of the tides, and the seasonal movements of the species that inhabit both the fresh and the salt water.

## The Naga and the Warm Rivers

The [naga](/content/species/sapient/naga) of the warm southern rivers maintain the oldest continuous form of the faith, and their observance is the model upon which the human communities of the central rivers have patterned their own practices. The naga are themselves river-dwellers, inhabiting the water rather than the banks, and their understanding of the movements of the fish and the quality of the water is regarded by the human communities with a mixture of respect and dependence. The naga observe the Potamia at the customary seasons of the warm river, and they maintain the closed waters with a strictness that the human communities of the north have sometimes struggled to match.

The relationship between the naga and the human river communities is one of mutual dependence: the naga know the deep waters and the breeding grounds of the fish, and the humans control the fords and bridges and the harvesting of the riverbank. The two peoples have maintained a quiet coexistence for centuries, with occasional disputes over the boundaries of the fishing grounds but no sustained conflict. The joint observance of the festival of the waters at the height of the summer is the principal occasion of the formal meeting between the naga and the human communities, and the festival is marked by a grand procession along the riverbank that includes participants from both peoples.

## The Kappa and the Interior Waters

The [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the interior waters have adapted the faith to the still waters of the lake and the pond, and their observance is centred on the [Limna](/content/belief/deity/limna) rather than the Potamia. The kappa communities are dwellers of the still water, and their understanding of the lake ecosystems is deep, though their knowledge is often inaccessible to the human communities who live on the shores and do not share the kappa's ability to move freely beneath the surface. The kappa observe the rite of the breaking of the ice at the first thaw of the spring, and they maintain the closed waters to protect the fish of the lake during the spawning season.

The relationship between the kappa and the human lake communities is generally peaceful. The kappa have been known to warn the humans of approaching droughts and the changes in the water level that precede them. The humans have in turn shared the produce of the harvest with the kappa in the lean months, and the exchange is the foundation of a quiet reciprocity that has sustained the lake communities through difficult seasons. The faith teaches that the still water is the inheritance of the many peoples of the interior, and this principle has prevented the kind of territorial conflicts that have plagued other river communities.

## Relation to Other Faiths

River Mouths is a local faith of the water, and it does not claim authority beyond the river. The human communities that observe it readily adopt the customs of the fields when they travel inland, and the faith makes no objection to this flexibility. The monotheistic missionaries of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame) who have visited the river towns have encountered some receptivity, particularly among the traders of the lowland river ports, but the faith has not displaced the older observances. The river peoples find the Potamia more immediately relevant to their daily concerns than a distant singular light, and conversion to the Still Flame among the river communities has been limited to the younger generation, who often practise both religions in a manner the elders consider pragmatic if not entirely consistent.

The faith shares certain practical customs with the [Sea Mother's Court](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/sea-mothers-court), particularly the recognition of seasonal closure for the protection of breeding stock and the ceremonial blessing of boats. The two faiths have developed a quiet understanding between the river-mouth and the sea-coast communities, and the joint observance of the festival of the waters at the height of the summer is the principal occasion of their formal gathering.

## Significance

River Mouths is a working faith, inseparable from the daily life of the communities that depend upon the river for their sustenance and their transport. Its reverence for the breeding cycles of the fish, its formalisation of sustainable practice through religious obligation, and its integration of the naga and kappa knowledge into the daily practice of the river communities have contributed materially to the prosperity of the river regions of the known world. The faith is one of the most practical and least displaced of the religious traditions, sustained not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the fisherfolk and the farmer who depend upon the river for their livelihood and whose livelihood's continuation depends upon the river remaining healthy. The faith endures in the river and at the estuary, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the moving water.
