## Overview

The Spirits of the Kharesh is the name given by chroniclers to the body of animistic belief held by the nomadic and settled peoples of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), the great desert interior of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). It is a faith of place rather than of person, in which the desert itself is understood to be inhabited by a multitude of spirits, each bound to a dune, a well, a standing rock, or a stretch of open sand. The [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), whose oral traditions are reckoned among the richest of the known world, preserve the legends of these spirits, and the [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) and the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) who wander the erg alike hold that the desert is far from empty, and that the traveller who ignores its spirits does so at his peril.

## The Nature of the Desert Spirits

The faith teaches that the Kharesh is alive with powers that are neither gods nor mortal, but the lasting presences of the land itself. Each well is held to have its guardian, each massif its own spirit of stone, and the open dunes a wandering power that shifts with the sand. The salawa, whose oral traditions speak of their having been born from the sand and the wind, regard the desert as a living parent, and they treat the spirits as kin of a distant and exacting sort. The humans of the caravans hold much the same belief in plainer terms, reckoning the spirits among the hazards and the helpers of the crossing, to be placated where they are hostile and thanked where they are kind.

Unlike the greater pantheons of the settled peoples, the spirits of the Kharesh are profoundly local, and none is held to rule over the whole of the desert. The chronicler notes that this locality is the mark of an old animism, akin in kind to the [Old Pantheon of the Plains](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/old-pantheon-of-the-plains), though the two faiths differ in temper, for the plains spirits are generous and the desert spirits are sparing, as befits the lands they inhabit. The desert peoples say that a spirit gives nothing it has not first been shown respect, and that the reckless traveller offends the land itself rather than a distant deity.

## The Guardian of the Well

Chief among the desert spirits in the daily life of the peoples are the guardians of the wells and the oases, upon whom the survival of every party depends. The nomads hold that each reliable water source has its spirit, and that the spirit must be acknowledged before the water is drawn, lest it withhold its bounty or lead the unwary into a dried and fatal hollow. The ritual is small and practical: a few drops are poured upon the sand, a word of thanks is spoken, and the party rests a moment before filling its skins. The elders teach that this observance is the first discipline of the desert child, for to forget the guardian of the well is to forget the difference between life and death upon the sand.

The settled communities of the rocky uplands, whose terraced gardens depend upon the springs that emerge at the base of the massifs, observe a similar reverence toward the spirit of the spring, and the cultivation of the oasis is understood as a compact with the land, in which the people tend the garden and the spirit sustains the water. The chronicle of the upland settlements records that the gardens have endured for centuries without failure, a fact the inhabitants attribute as much to the keeping of the compact as to the skill of their husbandry.

## The Wandering Powers of the Erg

Beyond the settled spirits of well and rock, the faith reckons wandering powers that move with the dunes and the seasonal winds. These are held to be the least predictable of the desert's inhabitants, and the cause of the mirages, the sudden storms, and the disorientation that has destroyed many a caravan. The nomads speak of a power of the deep erg that can lead a party in circles until its water is spent, and they guard against it by the careful reading of landmark and star, and by the singing of the old route-songs that name the waypoints in order, that the mind may not be turned from the true path.

The [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the alliance of the inland caravan cities that depends upon the desert routes, honours these powers in its own manner, for the safe conduct of the caravans is the pact's lifeblood, and its guides are trained from youth in the observances that keep the wandering powers at a distance. The pact maintains way-stations at the older wells where the guardians are accounted most ancient, and the keeping of these stations is reckoned a religious as much as a commercial duty.

## Rites and the Oral Tradition

The rites of the desert spirits are carried entirely in the oral traditions of the peoples, for the faith has no written scripture and no house of worship, the desert itself being its temple. The storytellers and the elders are the keepers of the legends, and the transmission of the traditions from one generation to the next is accounted the most important responsibility of the elder, above even the governance of the kindred. The songs of the desert name the spirits of particular places, and a traveller who knows the song of a well is reckoned to hold a claim upon its favour, much as the pastoralists of the plains hold a claim upon the spirit of a hill by the knowing of its name.

The Assembly of the Sands, the great gathering of the Kharesh Tribes at a designated oasis, is likewise an occasion for the recitation of the traditions, and the elders of every kindred gather to confirm the legends and to settle such disputes as arise between the accounts of different families. The chronicler observes that this oral custody of the spirits is remarkably stable, for the harshness of the desert punishes error harshly, and the peoples have therefore been careful to preserve the exact knowledge of the land and its powers across the generations.

## Relation to Other Faiths

The Spirits of the Kharesh is generally unconcerned with the creeds of other peoples, for its spirits are held to be bound to the desert and to hold no sway beyond it. The desert peoples who travel the caravan routes are known to observe the customs of the [Doctrine of the Open Hand](/content/belief/ideology/doctrine-of-the-open-hand) in their dealings with strangers, and the two traditions sit easily together, for the open hand asks nothing of the divine and the desert spirits ask nothing of the stranger. The monotheistic [Faith of the Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/faith-of-the-still-flame) has made little headway in the deep Kharesh, where the immediacy of the land and its powers leaves little room for a distant and singular light, though a few of the coastal settlements that border the desert have taken up the Flame alongside the older observances.

The faith stands in a rough kinship with the plains pantheon to the north, and the chroniclers who first set down the beliefs of the world reckoned the two among the old animisms of the open lands, the one of the grass and the other of the sand. The desert peoples themselves draw no such comparison, holding their spirits to be peculiar to the Kharesh and answerable to no power of the plains.

## Significance

The Spirits of the Kharesh endures as the living faith of the desert and its peoples, and its influence is written in the survival of the caravans and the endurance of the oasis gardens across the long ages of Sahrana's history. For the nomads and the settled folk of the uplands alike, the spirits are not a matter of doctrine but of daily practice, present in every drawing of water and every crossing of the erg, and the faith gives the harsh land a face and a will that the peoples have learned to honour. In a world of growing kingdoms and swelling cities, the old animism of the Kharesh remains the quiet inheritance of those who walk the sand beneath the open sky, a living reminder that the empty places of the world are inhabited by powers older than any realm and closer to the earth than any crown.
