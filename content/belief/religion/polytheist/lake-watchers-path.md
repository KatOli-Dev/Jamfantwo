## Overview

The inland lakes of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the still waters of the interior have long served as sources of sustenance for the communities that settle upon their shores. The body of belief that grew up among these lake communities is centred on the [Limna](/content/belief/deity/limna), the goddess of the still water that reflects the sky and provides the fish, the waterfowl, and the fertile silt that sustains the surrounding settlements. Beneath her are set the powers of the marsh, the ice, and the particular species of fish and birds that inhabit each lake, and the faith is transmitted through the daily practice of the lake communities rather than through a formal teaching or priesthood.

The faith draws adherents from the human communities of the lake-shore, the [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes, and the [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the still waters. Each people observes a form of the faith adapted to their particular relationship with the lake, and the diversity of observance is one of the faith's strengths, for it allows the different peoples who dwell around the lake to share a common spiritual framework without requiring them to adopt each other's practices. There is no central authority, no scripture, and no dedicated building; the lake itself is the temple, and the elders of each community conduct the seasonal rites at the customary shore or island.

## The Powers of the Still Water

The Limna is the principal power, conceived as the living presence of the still water, the reflection, and the depth. She is invoked at the calm of the evening when the lake is at its stillest, and her festival falls at the breaking of the ice in the spring, when the lake becomes accessible to the fisherfolk and the communities can resume their full seasonal activities. The [Quagmire](/content/belief/deity/quagmire) is the second, the god of the wetland that borders the lake and the creatures that inhabit the reed and the mud, and he is honoured at the season of the migration when the waterfowl arrive at the lake in large numbers. The [Ice Breaker](/content/belief/deity/ice-breaker) is the third, the power of the ice that forms upon the lake in the cold months and the breaking of which in the spring releases the lake for the fishing season.

Beneath these are counted the gods of the particular lakes, islands, inlets, and species of fish and waterfowl. The community of each lake maintains the names of its local powers in small books kept by the elder, and the names are updated only when a power has been known across several generations. The catalogue of local gods is long, with distinct entries for the lake of the eastern valley, the lake of the northern basin, and the smaller ponds and marshes that feed into the larger waters. This localisation is both a strength and a feature of occasional confusion, as the names of lakes that are only distantly related share similar roots in the local tongues.

## Rites of the Lake Year

The religious year of the inland lakes follows the cycle of the water. The rite of the breaking of the ice is the principal observance of the faith and marks the end of winter and the reopening of the lake for fishing. The elder of the community stands at the principal shore and speaks the traditional words, acknowledging that the lake has released its ice to the warmth of the spring and that the community may again draw upon its bounty. The rite of the spawning follows the return of the fish to the inlet streams, and it is conducted by the fisherfolk of the community before the nets are set. The harvest of the lake is the second principal observance, celebrated in the late summer when the fish stocks are at their peak and the waterfowl begin their southward migration.

A distinctive practice is the rite of the first catch, performed at the first fishing of each season. A young fisher of the community is presented to the lake by the elder, who speaks the traditional words of welcome, asking the Limna to receive the candidate as a child of the water and grant safe passage. The young fisher touches the surface and receives a small token - a piece of polished lake stone, the scale of the first fish caught, or a waterfowl feather - that is kept for life. This practice, while simple, has done much to strengthen the emotional bond between the young and the lake, and lake communities that observe it faithfully report lower rates of accidental drownings among the young than those that neglect it.

## The Marsh and the Anurans

The [anurans](/content/species/sapient/anurans) of the marshes are the principal non-human adherents of the faith, and their observance is adapted to the wetland environment that borders the lake. The anuran communities are dwellers of the marsh and the still water, and the marsh is held to be the inheritance of the anuran communities. The anuran communities honour the Quagmire at the customary seasons, and the anuran communities offer the first fish of the catch at the customary shrine of the community. The marsh is not the exclusive domain of any single people; rather, it is understood as a shared space in which each people occupies its own niche, and the faith teaches that the marsh, like the lake, is a trust that is shared among the peoples who depend upon it.

The relationship between the anuran communities and the human lake communities is generally peaceful. The anurans have been known to warn the humans of approaching droughts and the changes in the water level that precede them. The humans have in turn shared the produce of the harvest with the anurans in the lean months, and the exchange is the foundation of a quiet reciprocity that has sustained the lake communities through difficult seasons. The faith holds that the marsh is the inheritance of the many peoples of the still water, and this principle has prevented the kind of territorial conflicts that have plagued some river communities in the known world.

## The Kappa and the Still Waters

The [kappa](/content/species/sapient/kappa) of the still waters are also adherents of the faith, although their observance is adapted to the life of the deep water rather than the shore. The kappa communities are dwellers of the lake and the pond, and their understanding of the lake ecosystem is deep, particularly their knowledge of the fish species and the winter conditions that are often hidden from the shore-dwellers. The kappa observe the breaking of the ice at the first thaw, and they maintain the customary quotas that govern the fishing of the lake during the spawning season. Kappa knowledge of the lake is often inaccessible to the human communities, and the kappa are sometimes reluctant to share it, given the history of exploitation by shore-dwelling peoples in other parts of the known world.

The relationship between the kappa and the human lake communities has improved considerably in recent years, as the formal agreements between the lake peoples have provided clearer protections for the kappa's rights to the underwater territories. The joint management of the lake is one of the most practical applications of the faith, as it requires the lake communities to share knowledge about the movements of the fish, the depth of the ice, and the seasonal changes in the water chemistry. The system has been remarkably successful, with the lakes of the central and northern lowlands maintaining stable fish populations and waterfowl numbers for centuries under the joint administration of the human, anuran, and kappa communities.

## The Watch on the Lake and the Ice Breaker

The watch is the central duty of the inland lake communities, and the watchkeepers are responsible for monitoring the conditions of the water and relaying warnings about storms, the approach of dangerous ice, and the movement of the waterfowl. The watch is conducted from the principal shore and the island shrines, and the watchkeepers are often the same elders who conduct the seasonal rites. The ice-breaker is the duty of keeping the channels open through the ice during the winter, allowing the fish to survive the cold months and the villagers to draw water from the lake. The ice-breaker is performed by a dedicated team of workers from the community, and the work is considered a form of service to the Limna, for the ice-breaker ensures that the lake remains alive and accessible even in the depths of winter.

The watch and the ice-breaker are among the most practical applications of the faith, and the communities that have maintained them faithfully over the generations have been more resilient through bad seasons. The watch is also a source of valuable information for the surrounding farming communities, as the lake communities are often the first to observe the weather patterns that precede the spring rains and the autumn storms.

## The Lake Islands and the Sacred Coves

The islands of the great inland lakes are the principal sacred centres of the faith, and they serve as the gathering points for the lake communities at the turning of the year. The islands are not built upon in the manner of the temples of the land, for they are understood as natural dwellings of the divine, and the rites are conducted upon the shore and the interior of the islands with the customary observances. The sacred coves are the sheltered places along the lake-shore where the rites that require the quiet of the water are conducted, and the coves are held in common by the lake communities.

The lake islands have long been centres of learning and cultural exchange among the lake communities, and the gathering of the communities on the islands has done much to preserve the unity of the faith across the wide extent of the interior waters. The island shrines are maintained by the communities in common, and the maintenance is considered the responsibility of all the lake peoples, regardless of their species or their place of origin. The faith teaches that the island is the dwelling of the divine in the still water, and the lake communities are required to honour the island and the cove with the customary observances.

## Relation to Other Beliefs

The Lake Watchers' Path is a local faith of the still water, and it does not claim authority beyond the lake. The human communities that observe it readily adopt the customs of the fields when they travel inland, and the faith makes no objection to this flexibility. The monotheistic missionaries of the [Still Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/still-flame) who have visited the lake towns have encountered variable receptivity, with some communities adopting portions of the Still Flame observance alongside the older customs. The people of the lake find the Limna more immediately relevant to their daily concerns than a distant singular light, and conversion among the lake communities has been limited to the younger generation, who often practise both religions in a manner the elders consider pragmatic.

The faith shares certain practical customs with the [River Mouths](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/river-mouths), particularly the recognition of seasonal closure for the protection of breeding stock and the joint management of the fishing grounds. The two faiths have developed a quiet understanding between the river and the lake communities, and the shared observance of the festival of the waters at the height of the summer is the principal occasion of their formal gathering. The faith is more distant from the [Forest Way](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/forest-way) of the forest-dwellers and the [Stone Reading](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/stone-reading) of the stoneworkers, for the two faiths occupy distinct environments: the still water and the wood, the stone.

## Significance

The Lake Watchers' Path is the faith of the interior peoples, the communities whose lives are shaped by the slow rhythms of the still water. It is a faith that asks little of its adherents beyond daily attentiveness and respect for the cycle of the seasons, and it offers nothing that contradicts the practical needs of the people who practice it. The joint management system that it has produced among the human, anuran, and kappa communities is one of the most successful examples of cross-species governance in the known world, and the stability of the inland lake fish stocks and waterfowl populations is a testament to its effectiveness. The faith is one of the most practical and least displaced of the religious traditions of the known interior, sustained not by a ruling class or a priesthood but by the steady labour of the lake-fishers and the farmer who depend upon the lake for their livelihood and whose livelihood depends upon the lake remaining healthy. The faith endures, in the still water and at the breaking of the ice, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the lake.
