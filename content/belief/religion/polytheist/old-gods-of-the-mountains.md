## Overview

The dwarven clans of the high ranges and the deep halls have long held that stone and metal are not lifeless substance but the hardened bodies of ancient powers. This is not a faith centred upon shrines or temples but a way of reading the mountain for signs of the living will beneath it. The principal powers are the smith-god who shapes veins of ore, the keeper of the dark who tends the galleries no surface light has reached, and the miner-god who opens new ways through solid rock. Below them are counted a host of local spirits bound to individual peaks, caves, and quarries.

The tradition stretches back into the period of recorded dwarven history with no clear beginning, and its teachers refuse to say when the first clan began to honour the stone. Some of the human shepherds of the upper slopes have adopted fragments of the observance, particularly the rites of the first strike and the custom of leaving a portion of each vein untouched, but the full discipline remains the inheritance of the stoneworkers. A small number of elven foresters who dwell upon the high ridges have also taken up portions of the reverence for the stone, acknowledging that the mountain is shared between those who dwell within it and those who tend its upper slopes.

## Doctrine of the Living Stone

The mountains do not yield their wealth freely, and the teaching centres upon the obligation owed by the worker to the stone. The smith-god is the first among the greater powers, held to dwell within the forge-fire and the anvil. A new hearth is kindled with a short invocation, and the first casting of each season is offered to the smith-god before the metal is shaped. The miner-god occupies the deeper passages where no shaft or stair has yet been cut, and a blow struck into virgin rock is preceded by the customary acknowledgement that the worker enters as a guest, not an owner. The keeper of the dark is honoured in the unlit workings, where lamps burn poorly or not at all and the worker must move solely by memory and by sound. The chronicles from Karhold record that the keeper is also the one who guides lost workmen to the light when they have wandered too far down a collapsed gallery.

Beneath these three are set the local spirits of the mountain. Each vein of ore has its own spirit, and the stoneworkers name them according to the rock that holds them, the ore that colours them, and the direction of the gallery in which they lie. The names are passed from master to apprentice and are maintained in case books kept by the elder masons. A new spirit is added only after a working has existed across several generations and the community can attest to its continuity. The practice is conservative by design, for the teaching holds that to name a spirit without having lived with it is to invite neither favour nor misfortune but only forgetfulness.

## The Closed Vein and the Offering

The custom of the closed vein is one of the most visible markers of the faith in the dwarven holds. When an ore seam is exhausted or becomes too poor to sustain safe working, the community does not abandon it immediately. Instead, a set portion is deliberately left in place, and the spirit of the vein is thanked and released from further claim. The offering is not a gift intended to buy future wealth; it is rather an admission that the mountain gives according to its own schedule and that the workers are temporary custodians of whatever runs. Mines where the custom is observed have historically maintained steadier output across decades than those where it was neglected, though the chroniclers caution that correlation and causation are not always easy to separate.

The offering is recorded in the hall books of each community and counted among the chief rites of the working year. It is often paired with the festival of the closing of the heights, held at the first snowfall of autumn, during which the surface dwellers and the underground clans come together for a shared meal and the reading of the year's accounts. The dual observance ensures that neither the mountain nor the surface is treated as the exclusive domain of any single class.

## Festivals of the Turning Year

The religious calendar of the mountains tracks both the surface seasons and the rhythms of the workings below. The festival of the opening of the heights follows the spring thaw and marks the return of traffic between the galleries and the world above. Wardens clear the upper roads, and the elders of the surface clans process to the principal shrines on the ridges before returning to the halls. The festival of the first ore celebrates the first substantial strike of the season, typically a rich seam discovered in the winter months, and the ore is carried in procession to the shrine of the miner-god at the mouth of the gallery.

The festival of the closing of the heights closes the annual cycle, as described above, and serves as the occasion for arbitration. Disputes over property boundaries, debt, or mining rights that cannot be settled in the regular halls are brought before the council of elders at this gathering, where the judgment of the mountain is sought through a combination of customary law and public deliberation. The practice gives the festival a civic function beside its religious one.

## The High Places and the Surface Communities

The upper slopes of the mountain range are the dwelling of the shepherds, whose flocks are moved to the high pastures during the warmer months. The shepherds maintain their own set of customs centred on the care of the herd, the marking of the mountain passes, and the protection of the grazing lands. They do not share the dwarven doctrine of the living stone in its full form, but they honour the spirit of the peak upon which their herds graze with a small token of milk and the first clipped wool. The chroniclers note that the boundary between the dwarven halls and the shepherds' pastures has rarely been contested, as both peoples accept that each is the master of its own domain and that the mountain accommodates both.

Elven foresters dwell upon the upper forest line, where the timber thickens and the air grows cooler. They do not enter the galleries, and the dwarvens do not fell the ancient trees, and the customary boundary between the two domains is observed through a combination of practical restraint and formal agreements reached at the festivals. The elven adherence to the faith is limited to the spirits of the cliff and the scree that border the treeline, and the elves acknowledge these spirits as kin to the spirits that the dwarves honour in the stone.

## Learning and the Transmission of Practice

The teaching of the mountains is not contained in books but in the hands and in the halls. Apprentices are placed with a master mason or miner and learn through observation and labour. A young stoneworker does not read doctrine; he learns to read rock. The colour of a fracture, the grain of a seam, the sound a pick makes upon solid stone versus hollow ground - these are the early lessons. The theoretical instruction comes later, when the apprentice is trusted to keep a book and to record the names of the local spirits that he and his master encounter.

The case books of the elder masons form a dispersed archive across the holds. Some communities maintain copies of copies, and long-distance correspondence between the major guilds allows traditions discovered in one range to be tested against the stone of another. The chroniclers record that such exchange has occasionally produced friction, for a practice accepted in Karhold may seem unnecessary or even hubristic to the elders of a smaller hold who have known only their own stone. The discipline has survived these frictions without fracturing, owing to the shared recognition that the mountain is not the same anywhere.

## Relation to the Faiths of Other Peoples

The dwarven observance does not extend beyond the stone, and the stoneworkers do not travel the lowlands in search of converts. When dwarves trade with the human settlements of the valley or the river towns of the interior, they observe the customs of those places with the same care they bring to their own, but they do not adopt the faiths they encounter as one adopts new tools. The monotheistic teachers who have visited the holds in recent centuries have found the stone-bound faithful unmoved, though also not hostile. The chronicler attributes this to the fact that the dwarven faith makes no universal claims; it concerns itself only with the mountain, and a doctrine that does not reach beyond stone leaves little ground for conflict.

Among the nomadic peoples of the plains and the desert, the old gods are respected as the powers of the high country, but they hold no followers among them. The desert caravans that pass through the mountain passes learn to honour the spirits of the passes at the customary stations, but they do not join the deeper rites of the holds. The faith therefore exists in a state of quiet coexistence with most of the other beliefs of the known world, and its endurance is in part a testament to its territorial focus.

## Significance

The Old Gods of the Mountains is a faith shaped entirely by its landscape. It is austere, particular, and deeply practical, concerned more with the care of the stone and the stewardship of the workings than with abstract doctrine. Where the monotheistic creeds and the open pantheons of the lowlands vie for the loyalty of entire populations, the old gods occupy the mountain and ask no more of those who dwell below it than courtesy at the pass and respect for the boundary. For the stoneworkers themselves, the discipline of the stone is not merely a set of observances but a way of seeing the world through the lens of rock and ore, and it endures as one of the most distinctive and least displaced of the living traditions of the known world.
