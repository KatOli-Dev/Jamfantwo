## Overview

Sky Walkers' Way is the name given to the polytheistic body of belief observed among the pastoral and nomadic peoples of the southern grasslands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and, in altered form, among kindred peoples elsewhere in the known world. It is a faith of many spirits rather than one, and its gods are not remote rulers seated above the world but active presences within the land itself: the spirit of the open sky, the spirit of the grazing herds, the spirit of the standing stone, and a host of lesser powers that govern the wind, the rain, and the turning of the seasons. The pastoralists hold that the plains are alive with these spirits, and that the welfare of the herds and the people depends upon keeping them duly honoured through song, offering, and the careful management of the grazing lands.

## Origins and the Peoples of the Plains

The pantheon is old beyond the reach of written record, and its origins are entangled with the earliest movements of the pastoral peoples across the southern grasslands. The [centaurs](/content/species/sapient/centaurs), whose oral traditions speak of their creation by the spirits of the plains, are among the peoples who hold to the oldest forms of these beliefs, and their kindreds preserve songs that name the spirits of particular hills, rivers, and stretches of turf as kin to their own lineages. The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) who graze the southern plains alongside the centaur kindreds share much of the same pantheon, though the human settlements of the region have blended the old spirits with the customs of the cultivated land, and the faith as practised in the towns differs in degree from that of the open country.

Beyond Aethelar, echoes of the plains pantheon are reported among the nomadic peoples of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) and in the steppe traditions of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), though scholars differ as to whether these are branches of a single ancient faith or separate growths that arose from the common experience of a life lived close to the open land. What is agreed is that the pantheon is a faith of the horizon, suited to peoples whose wealth is measured in moving herds and whose eyes are accustomed to the long flat line where the earth meets the sky.

## The Greater Spirits

The pantheon is headed by a small number of greater spirits, each governing a broad aspect of the plains life. The [Sky Walker](/content/belief/deity/sky-walker) is accounted first among them, the spirit of the open heavens and the weather, who sends the rains that green the grass and the winds that sweep the long grass flat. The [Pasara](/content/belief/deity/pasara) is the spirit of the grazing beasts and of fertility, invoked at the birthing of calves and lambs and at the founding of a new herd. The [Stone Watcher](/content/belief/deity/stone-watcher) is the spirit of the standing stones and the buried dead, who keeps the boundaries of the land and the memory of the ancestors. To these three are reckoned a fourth in some traditions, the [River Speaker](/content/belief/deity/river-speaker), spirit of the waters that cut the plains, honoured by those kindreds whose grounds lie along the great rivers that thread the grasslands from north to south.

Beneath the greater spirits are counted a multitude of lesser powers, many of them local to a single hill, spring, or stand of trees. The pastoralists hold that to know the name of the spirit of a place is to hold a claim upon its favour, and the elders of a kindred are charged with the remembering of these names and the teaching of them to the young. The [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) of southern [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) is said by the desert peoples to be thronged with such local [Kharesh Spirits](/content/belief/religion/polytheist/kharesh-spirits), and the caravan songs of that region name them by the hundred, a testament to the patience of those who first walked the sand.

## Rites and the Management of the Land

The rites of the plains pantheon are bound up with the management of the grazing lands, for the spirits are held to be pleased by the careful tending of the earth and angered by its waste. The seasonal movement of the herds is accompanied by offerings at the standing stones that mark the old boundaries, and the first greening of the grass after the cold months is greeted with a song of thanksgiving to the Sky Walker and the Pasara alike. Births, marriages, and the passage of the dead are each marked by rites addressed to the appropriate spirit, and the elders who lead these rites are likewise the keepers of the kindred's knowledge of the land and its waters.

A distinctive teaching of the pantheon is the duty of restraint in the use of the plains, founded upon the belief that the spirits lend the grass and the beasts to the people only for their keeping. The pastoralists hold that to overgraze a ground is to offend the Pasara and to invite the thinning of the herds, and the management of grazing by rotation, observed among the centaur kindreds and the human pastoralists alike, is defended not merely as good husbandry but as a religious obligation. In this the plains faith is unusual among the beliefs of the world, for it joins devotion directly to the stewardship of the land, and the grasslands long inhabited by its peoples show the mark of this care in their enduring fertility.

## Centres and Wandering Shrines

The faith has no great houses of worship, for its gods are the land itself and its shrines are the standing stones, the springs, and the ancient trees that dot the plains. Certain stones are accounted older and more sacred than others, and the kindreds gather at these on the turning of the year to renew their bonds with one another and with the spirits of the place. The [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), which unites many of the human pastoral communities of the southern grasslands, is said by some to have grown from such gatherings, though the confederation itself makes no religious claim and counts among its members those who hold to other ways.

Wandering singers carry the songs of the pantheon between the kindreds, and it is through their travelling that the names of the spirits are kept consistent across the broad expanses of the grasslands. These singers are regarded with particular respect, for they are the living memory of the faith, and a kindred that has lost its singer is held to be in danger of forgetting the spirits of its own ground.

## Relation to Other Beliefs

Sky Walkers' Way is generally tolerant of other faiths, for its spirits are understood to be local and particular rather than universal claimants to the whole of creation. The pastoralists have long traded and intermarried with the settled [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the river valleys, some of whom keep the rites of the Still Flame alongside the older customs of the fields, and the two traditions have coexisted without great friction where the land is shared. The faith stands in sharper contrast to the monotheistic creeds, which deny the multiplicity of the divine, yet even here the contrast has rarely hardened into hostility, for the plains peoples are accustomed to the diversity of the land and see no reason that the spirits of one ground should command those of another.

Among the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the northern forests and the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain halls, the plains pantheon has found little adherence, each people keeping to the observances of its own ancient home, though elven travellers of the forest edge have been known to leave a token at a standing stone out of courtesy to the spirits of the open country.

## Significance

Sky Walkers' Way endures as one of the oldest and most widespread of the polytheistic faiths of the known world, and its influence is written not in monuments of stone but in the continuing health of the grasslands it teaches its people to tend. For the pastoralists and the centaur kindreds, the spirits of the plains are not a matter of doctrine but of daily life, present in every rainfall and every calving, and the faith gives shape to the rhythms by which these peoples measure their years. In an age of growing kingdoms and swelling cities, the old pantheon remains the quiet inheritance of those who still follow the herds beneath the open sky, a living reminder that the world is inhabited by powers older than any realm and closer to the earth than any crown.
