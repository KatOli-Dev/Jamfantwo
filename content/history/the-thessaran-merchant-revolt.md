## The Uprising of the Counting Houses

The Thessaran Merchant Revolt was a rebellion of the merchant houses of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) against the council of the League in the late fifth century of the present age. The revolt was the culmination of a generation of growing tension between the merchant houses, which controlled the trade of the League, and the council, which imposed taxes and regulations upon the trade. The revolt lasted for two years and ended with a settlement that reshaped the governance of the League.

The Thessaran League was a confederation of city-states that controlled the southern coast of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the principal trade routes of the [Lesser Inland Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/lesser-inland-sea). The League was governed by a council of magistrates drawn from the member cities, which regulated the trade and the navigation of the League's waters. The council imposed taxes upon the merchants of the League and required them to obtain licences for their vessels and their cargoes.

## The Grievances of the Merchants

The grievances of the merchants against the council were numerous and had accumulated over several decades. The principal grievance was the level of taxation, which the merchants regarded as excessive and unjust. The council imposed taxes upon the import and the export of goods, upon the use of the harbours, and upon the passage of vessels through the League's waters. The taxes consumed a substantial portion of the profits of the merchants, and the merchants argued that the council was taking too much and returning too little.

The merchants also complained about the regulations that the council imposed upon the trade. The council required the merchants to obtain licences for their vessels, to submit their cargoes to inspection, and to use the League's standard weights and measures. The regulations were intended to ensure the quality and the fairness of the trade, but the merchants regarded them as burdensome and unnecessary. The merchants argued that the regulations favoured the larger houses at the expense of the smaller ones and that the inspectors were corrupt and arbitrary in their judgments.

The third grievance was the exclusion of the merchants from the governance of the League. The council of the League was composed of magistrates drawn from the member cities, and the magistrates were chosen from the nobility and the landed gentry rather than from the merchant class. The merchants had no representation on the council, and they had no voice in the decisions that affected their trade. The merchants argued that the council did not understand the needs of the trade and that the council's decisions were made in the interest of the nobility rather than the interest of the League as a whole.

## The Outbreak of the Revolt

The revolt broke out in the autumn of the year 478, when the council of the League imposed a new tax upon the export of grain from the League's ports. The tax was substantial, and the merchants of the principal grain-trading houses refused to pay it. The council responded by seizing the grain of the principal houses and by ordering the arrest of the leaders of the merchants.

The merchants of the principal houses met in secret and agreed to resist the council by force. They organised a militia from their employees and their supporters, and they seized the principal harbour of the League's capital, the city of [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara). The harbour was the centre of the grain trade, and the merchants who controlled it could disrupt the trade of the entire League.

The council responded by summoning the militia of the member cities, but the militia was reluctant to act against the merchants, who were the principal employers of labour in the cities. The council was forced to negotiate with the merchants, and a period of tense negotiations followed. The merchants presented their demands to the council: the abolition of the new grain tax, the reduction of the existing taxes, the reform of the inspection system, and the representation of the merchants on the council.

## The Course of the Revolt

The negotiations between the council and the merchants continued through the winter and into the following spring. The council was divided between those who favoured a compromise with the merchants and those who favoured a more forceful response. The merchants were also divided, with the larger houses favouring a comprehensive settlement and the smaller houses favouring a more radical reorganisation of the League.

The negotiations broke down in the spring of 479, when a faction of the council that favoured a forceful response persuaded the council to arrest the leaders of the merchants who were engaged in the negotiations. The arrests were carried out in secret, and the leaders were imprisoned in the council's fortress. The merchants responded by declaring a general strike, closing the shops and the warehouses of the capital and refusing to handle any goods until the leaders were released.

The strike was effective, and the trade of the League ground to a halt. The council was unable to collect the taxes, the merchants were unable to trade, and the citizens of the capital were unable to obtain the goods that they needed. The council was forced to release the leaders of the merchants and to resume the negotiations.

The second round of negotiations was more productive than the first. The council agreed to abolish the new grain tax and to reduce the existing taxes by a modest amount. The council also agreed to reform the inspection system by appointing independent inspectors who were not subject to the influence of the nobility. The council refused, however, to grant the merchants representation on the council, arguing that the constitution of the League did not provide for such a change.

## The Settlement

The negotiations continued through the remainder of 479 and into 480, with both sides making concessions and holding firm on their principal demands. The merchants accepted that the council would not grant them representation but insisted upon the establishment of a separate body that would represent the interests of the merchants in the governance of the League.

The compromise that emerged from the negotiations was the establishment of the Chamber of Commerce, a body of merchants elected by the members of the principal houses that would advise the council on matters of trade and commerce. The chamber would have the right to be consulted on all proposed taxes and regulations, and the council would be required to give reasons for any decision that went against the chamber's advice.

The compromise was accepted by both sides, and the revolt ended in the summer of 480. The leaders of the merchants were pardoned, the grain that had been seized was returned, and the new grain tax was abolished. The Chamber of Commerce was established and held its first meeting in the autumn of the same year.

## The Aftermath and the Legacy

The aftermath of the revolt was a period of reconstruction and reform in the Thessaran League. The council implemented the reforms that had been agreed in the settlement, and the Chamber of Commerce became an established part of the governance of the League. The chamber was not a revolutionary change, as the council retained the ultimate authority, but it gave the merchants a voice in the decisions that affected their trade.

The revolt also had a lasting effect upon the relations between the merchants and the council. The merchants had demonstrated that they could disrupt the trade of the League and that they could organise themselves effectively in the pursuit of their interests. The council had learned that the merchants could not be ignored and that the prosperity of the League depended upon the cooperation of the merchant houses.

The legacy of the revolt is visible in the institutions of the Thessaran League in the present age. The Chamber of Commerce remains a part of the governance of the League, and the merchants continue to be consulted on matters of trade and commerce. The revolt is remembered in the chronicles of the League as a turning point in its history, a moment when the merchants asserted their place in the governance of the League and secured a voice in the decisions that affected their trade.
