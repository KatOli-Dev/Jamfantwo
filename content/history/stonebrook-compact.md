## The Highland River Agreement

The Stonebrook Compact is the name given by the chroniclers of the highlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) to the river agreement modelled upon the [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact), regulating the navigation, the fishery, and the use of the banks of the principal river of the highland region. The compact was negotiated between the magistrates of the highland towns and the representatives of the mining and forestry communities that depended upon the river for the transport of goods and for their livelihood.

The compact was adopted in the middle period of the recorded history of the highlands and followed the same structure as the Greywash Compact, establishing similar rules for the use of the river and similar offices for the administration of those rules. The compact was the first river agreement of the highlands and served as the model for later agreements in the neighbouring valleys.

## The Conditions of the Highland River

The river of the highlands, known as the Stonebrook for the rocky bed over which it flows through the upper reaches, presented challenges that differed from those of the lowland rivers. The Stonebrook was a fast-flowing mountain river with a steep gradient, shallow channels, and numerous rapids that made navigation difficult. The principal use of the river was not navigation in the ordinary sense but the floating of timber from the highland forests to the towns of the lower valleys and the transport of ore from the mines of the upper reaches.

The fishery of the Stonebrook was less productive than the fisheries of the lowland rivers, but it supported a small community of fisherfolk who took the mountain trout and the freshwater eels that migrated up the river in the spring. The banks of the river were used by the mining and forestry communities for the sorting and loading of timber and ore, and by the farmers of the narrow valleys for grazing and for the cultivation of the steep slopes.

The conflicts that arose from the competing uses of the river were less intense than those of the lowland rivers, but they were sufficiently serious to require a comprehensive agreement. The principal conflicts were between the timber floaters, who required the free passage of the river for their logs, and the fishing communities, whose nets and weirs obstructed the passage. The timber floaters also required the right to use the banks for the sorting and loading of timber, which brought them into conflict with the farmers who used the same banks for grazing.

## The Provisions of the Compact

The Stonebrook Compact established a system of rules and institutions adapted to the conditions of the highland river. The compact defined the rights of passage for timber and ore, the regulation of the fishery, and the use of the banks in terms that reflected the practical needs of the highland communities.

The passage provisions of the compact established the right of free passage for timber and ore upon the river, subject to the payment of a small transit fee at the principal towns of the lower valley. The fee was set at a rate sufficient to maintain the clearing of the channel and the removal of obstructions that impeded the passage of logs and barges.

The fishery provisions of the compact regulated the taking of fish from the river, prohibiting the use of nets that obstructed the passage and requiring the fishing communities to remove their weirs during the seasons when the timber was floated down the river. The fishery provisions were less restrictive than those of the lowland agreements, reflecting the smaller scale of the highland fishery.

The bank use provisions of the compact established the rights of the timber floaters and the miners to use the banks for the sorting and loading of goods, while reserving the rights of the farmers to graze their animals on the banks during the seasons when the timber and ore were not being transported.

## The Office of the Highland River Warden

The office of the river warden established by the Stonebrook Compact was modelled on the office of the Greywash Compact but adapted to the conditions of the highlands. The warden was appointed by the magistrates of the principal highland towns and was given the authority to enforce the provisions of the compact, to settle disputes between users of the river, and to report to the magistrates on the state of the river.

The warden was supported by a small staff of deputies who patrolled the river and inspected the banks. The warden also maintained a register of the timber and ore shipments that passed down the river and the fishing permits that had been granted.

The warden's authority was more limited than that of the lowland wardens, reflecting the smaller scale of the highland economy and the lesser intensity of the conflicts that arose upon the river.

## The Compact and the Highland Communities

The Stonebrook Compact established not only the rules for the use of the river but also the institutions through which the mining, forestry, and farming communities could participate in the regulation of the river. The compact required the magistrates of the highland towns to consult with the elders of the mining and forestry communities before making any revision of the rules, ensuring that the voices of those who depended upon the river were heard in the deliberations of the magistrates.

The compact also established an annual assembly of the representatives of the communities that used the river, at which the state of the river was reviewed and the accounts of the transit fees were examined. The assembly was a practical institution that gave the communities a stake in the success of the compact and a forum in which disputes could be resolved before they became serious.

## The Implementation and Revision

The implementation of the compact required the cooperation of the mining and forestry communities, who had previously used the river without any formal regulation, and the acceptance of the compact by these communities was essential to its success. The magistrates of the highland towns consulted extensively with the elders of the mining and forestry communities before the compact was adopted, and the provisions of the compact were adjusted to accommodate the concerns of those communities.

The compact has remained in force since its adoption and is the foundation of public order for the Stonebrook and its tributaries. The compact is one of several river agreements that have been modelled upon the [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact), and it is regarded by the magistrates of the highlands as a successful adaptation of the Greywash model to the conditions of a mountain river system.
