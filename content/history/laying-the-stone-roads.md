## Paved Roads Across the Highlands and Valleys

Across several generations of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace), the magistrates of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar), [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm), and the other principal cities undertook the construction of a continuous network of paved roads across the inland valleys and the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands). The term Laying the Stone Roads is a loose one; the construction proceeded in distinct phases over more than a century. The roads replaced the older unpaved tracks that had served the merchants since the founding of the cities and became the foundation of the land commerce of the present age.

The construction was financed by the transit fees established by the [Twin-Reaches Peace](/content/history/twin-reaches-peace), contributions from the magistrates of the principal towns, and labour required from villages along the routes. The financing represented a compromise between the need to maintain the roads and the need to keep transit fees at a level merchants could afford.

## Foundations, Surfaces, and Drainage

The roads were built by master masons and road wardens of the cities. Each road was built upon a foundation of large stones set into a trench, covered with a layer of smaller stones packed tight, and finished with a layer of gravel rolled smooth. The surface was crowned slightly to allow water to run off into ditches cut along the sides, which were deep enough to carry the heaviest rain and connected to streams the road crossed.

The engineering was adapted to the terrain. Engineers of the Stoneheim Highlands cut roads into the rock of the passes and built retaining walls to hold the road in place upon steeper slopes. In the wettest sections they added stone culverts beneath the road to carry the water of the mountain streams. Engineers of the warm coast laid roads across the plains and built causeways across marshes and river mouths, using bundles of brushwood as a foundation where the ground was too soft to bear the weight of the stone. The engineers of the two regions learned from each other, laying the foundation of the road-building tradition of the present age.

## Stone Spans and Dwarven Tunnels

The roads required bridges and passes where they crossed rivers or mountains. Bridges were built of stone and timber, set upon piers founded upon the bedrock of the river. The largest of the bridges required piers that were sunk into the riverbed by means of cofferdams, a technique that the engineers learned from the shipbuilders of the coast. Those that have endured for several centuries include the bridge of the Greywash at [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara), the bridge of the [Stonespire](/content/location/natural/feature/stonespire) at the head of the pass, and the bridge of the [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) at the northern mouth of the strait.

The passes were cut through the rock of the mountains by dwarven engineers of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds). The dwarven engineers cut the road into the rock of the steepest sections and cut tunnels through ridges that would otherwise have been impassable. The longest of these tunnels, at the northern approach to Stonespire Pass, extends for several hundred paces through solid granite. The passes are the most remarkable works of the road-building programme and a measure of the skill of the dwarven engineers.

## Road Wardens and Continuous Care

Road maintenance was the responsibility of road wardens appointed by the magistrates of the principal towns. The wardens inspected the roads, repaired surfaces, cleared ditches, and maintained bridges and passes. They were sustained by a small toll collected at the gates of the principal towns, which paid their wages and those of the labourers working under their direction. Each warden was assigned a section of road of roughly a day's travel on either side of the town, and the wardens were required to inspect their sections at least once a week.

Continuous maintenance has been the key to the durability of the roads. The roads would have been destroyed by weather and traffic within a generation if maintenance had been neglected. The care with which the magistrates and councils have administered the maintenance reflects the public spirit of the cities.

## Shelters and Licensed Lodging

The roads were furnished with quarters and inns at intervals of a day's ride. A quarter was a small stone building maintained by the road wardens, providing shelter for travellers caught upon the road in storms. Each quarter was stocked with firewood and dry provisions and was kept locked, with the key held by the warden of the nearest section. An inn was a larger building maintained by a private innkeeper under licence from the magistrates, providing lodging and food for travellers and their animals. The quarters and inns are the foundation of the safety of the roads, relied upon by travellers of the present age as they were by travellers of the Long Peace.

The innkeeper was licensed by the magistrates of the principal town; the licence set the price and quality of lodging and food. Road wardens inspected inns at regular intervals, and innkeepers who violated the terms of the licence were brought before the magistrates. The licensing system has been the foundation of the reputation of the inns of the principal roads.

## Post-Plague Deterioration and Restoration

The roads fell into disrepair in the generations following the [Stone Plague](/content/history/stone-plague), as the loss of population reduced the labour available for maintenance and the traffic the roads carried. The decline was gradual and not uniform across the network. The principal roads of the trade routes were maintained by the magistrates even in the worst years, but secondary roads fell into disrepair and bridges and passes were neglected. Some of the smaller quarters were abandoned altogether and were not reoccupied for several generations.

The roads were repaired in the generations following recovery from the plague. The repair was financed by transit fees and contributions from merchants who used the roads and was conducted by master masons and road wardens of the cities. The work of repair involved not only resurfacing the roads and rebuilding the bridges but also clearing the ditches that had silted up and rebuilding the retaining walls that had collapsed. Repair has been continuous ever since; the roads of the present age are those the master masons of the Long Peace built and the road wardens of the present age maintain.

## Enduring Gift of the Long Peace

Laying the Stone Roads is among the most useful and least controversial works of the Long Peace. The roads, bridges, passes, quarters, and inns are the inheritance the Long Peace has bequeathed to the present age and the measure of the prosperity it established. The merchants and travellers mark the anniversaries of the completion of the principal sections with ceremonies attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every city of the three continents. These ceremonies are among the most important public observances of the present age.
