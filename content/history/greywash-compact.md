## The River Agreement of the Long Peace

The Greywash Compact is the agreement signed by the magistrates of the principal towns along the [Greywash River](/content/location/natural/feature/greywash-river) in the middle of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace) for the management of the river, the protection of the fisheries, and the regulation of navigation. The treaty was negotiated by [Oren Greywash](/content/people/historical/oren-greywash), the magistrate who served the council of the river towns for more than thirty years. The compact took its name from the river at whose confluence the magistrates convened to sign it, and it is recorded in the chronicles of the academies of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) as the most comprehensive of the river agreements of the present age.

The treaty was negotiated over several years through mediators of the academies. These mediators included scholars who had studied the older river agreements in force since the founding of the cities, bringing a knowledge of traditions that the parties had observed for several centuries. The negotiation is regarded as a model of patient and careful diplomacy.

## Inadequate Older Accords

The Greywash is one of the principal rivers of the three continents. It rises in the southern flank of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) and flows south and west to the sea at [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara). Several tributaries join the main course along its length, including the Tarnbrook from the eastern highlands and the Millwater from the central plains. The river is navigable for most of its length and has been the principal highway of the inland valleys since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era). It supports a substantial fishery, particularly of silverfin and river trout, that has been the foundation of prosperity for the towns along its banks.

By the middle of the Long Peace, the older river agreements had become inadequate. The growth of trade had increased traffic upon the river, leading to disputes between merchants of the upper and lower towns over the right of way at narrow reaches and over the delays caused by the loading and unloading of cargo at the smaller wharves. The fishery had been over-fished in some sections, causing disputes between fishermen who blamed one another for the decline of the catch and between fishermen and farmers of the banks who complained that the nets damaged the irrigation channels. The magistrates of the principal towns judged that a new agreement was required and convened at the confluence of the Greywash to sign the treaty.

## Navigation, Fishery, and River Management

The Greywash Compact is a long document covering navigation, the fishery, the banks, and the waters of the river. It establishes the rules of navigation: larger vessels have the right of way at narrower reaches, and smaller vessels may not impede them. Vessels travelling downstream must yield to those travelling upstream at the sharper bends. The compact establishes the rules of the fishery: each town is entitled to a certain number of nets in the section of the river within its bounds, and the nets must be of a pattern approved by the magistrates to ensure that undersized fish are not taken. It establishes the rules of the banks: farmers may not plough to the edge of the river nor divert water for irrigation without the consent of the magistrates, and the ditches that drain the fields must be kept clear of the fishing waters.

The treaty also establishes the office of the river warden, appointed by the magistrates of the principal towns to oversee implementation. The river warden inspects the river at regular intervals, reports to the magistrates on the state of navigation, the fishery, and the banks, and has the power to fine violators or bring them before the magistrate of the nearest town. The office of the river warden has been the foundation of the success of the treaty.

## Public Ceremonies and Academic Approval

The Greywash Compact was received with approval by the magistrates and merchants of the river towns, expressed through public ceremonies that included a procession upon the river, a service in the temples, and a reading of the terms in the public squares of the principal towns. The scholars of the academies preserved the text in their archives. The treaty is consulted by every magistrate who must judge a dispute upon the river and is studied as a model of patient diplomacy as part of the curriculum of the academies.

## The River Warden's Work

Implementation required several years of careful work by the magistrates and the river warden. The river warden inspected the river section by section, recording the state of navigation, the fishery, and the banks. Violators were brought before the magistrates, who imposed the prescribed fines. The river warden also mediated disputes between merchants and fishermen, bringing parties to settlements permitted by the treaty. The river warden reported to the magistrates at regular intervals, and the magistrates reviewed the reports and revised regulations as needed. Revisions have been few and carefully considered.

## Model for Later River Agreements

The Greywash Compact has been in force for several centuries and remains the foundation of public order for the river towns of the present age. It has been revised from time to time to reflect changes in the trade and the fishery, but revisions have been few and carefully considered. The magistrates mark the anniversaries of the signing with appropriate ceremonies.

The compact has served as a model for river agreements on the other principal rivers of the three continents, including the Silverwater Accord on the eastern coast and the Stonebrook Compact in the highlands. These agreements have followed the structure of the Greywash Compact, establishing similar offices of river warden and similar rules for navigation and fishing. The treaty is the foundation of public order for the rivers of the present age and the measure by which magistrates and councils judge public acts concerning the rivers.

## Foundation of Order on the Greywash

The Greywash Compact is the most comprehensive of the river agreements of the present age and the foundation of public order for the river towns for several centuries. Navigation, the fishery, the banks, and the waters of the river are managed in accordance with its terms, and the office of the river warden is the foundation of its implementation. The magistrates mark the anniversaries of the signing with ceremonies attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every town of the river. These ceremonies are among the most important public observances of the river towns.
