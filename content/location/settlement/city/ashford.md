## Major River Crossing of the Northern Valleys

Ashford is a northern city of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) situated at a major river crossing in the northern river valleys. The city stands where the river is at its narrowest and most shallow, and the ford that gave the city its name has been a crossing point since the earliest days of human settlement. The stone bridge that now spans the river is one of the oldest and most substantial in the north. The roads that converge on Ashford from all directions make the city a natural hub of communication and trade between the northern valleys and the rest of the continent.

## Ford, Stone Bridge, and the Converging Roads

The ford at Ashford was the original reason for the settlement and has been the most important point of passage on the northern river since humans first inhabited the region. The river is broad and deep for most of its course, but at Ashford it contracts and shallows, and the bed is firm enough to support wagons and livestock when the water is low. The stone bridge built at the crossing in a later age is wide enough for two wagons to pass and stands as one of the finest examples of northern engineering. It was constructed by dwarven engineers brought from the mountain holds, who shaped the voussoirs and set the piers on bedrock stabilised with driven piles.

The roads that converge on Ashford form the other foundation of the city's prosperity. The crossing is the meeting point of routes that connect the northern valleys to each other and to the rest of the continent. The north road runs to [Thornwick](/content/location/settlement/city/thornwick) and the older settlements of the valley, the south road runs to the central grasslands and the southern cities, the east road runs to the coast and the ports of the eastern shore, and the west road climbs into the foothills of the mountain spine. The traffic through Ashford is constant and varied, and the city's inns, taverns, and stables form a significant part of its economy.

The maintenance of the roads is a shared responsibility among the cities and settlements that depend on them. The road wardens of Ashford patrol the sections within the city's territory, repairing surfaces and maintaining the bridges and culverts that carry the roads across streams and gullies. The wardens also ensure traveller safety, and their presence deters the bandits who occasionally prey on northern road traffic. Each warden is assigned a defined section of road and is required to inspect it weekly, filing a written report with the guildhall on the condition of the surface and any needed repairs.

## Two Banks, Two Bridges, and the Daily Market

Ashford is a bustling, well-connected city whose architecture reflects its function as a crossroads settlement. The city is built on both banks of the river, connected by the original stone bridge and a second, newer bridge built to accommodate the growing traffic of recent centuries. The streets are broad and well-paved to accommodate the wagons and livestock that pass through. The public buildings are situated near the bridge, where traffic is heaviest and commerce greatest. The guildhall is the most prominent public building, where the merchants and craftsmen who govern the city meet to conduct the business of the corporation that administers the crossing and the roads.

The market square of Ashford is one of the busiest in the north, and the market is held daily, as the constant road traffic ensures a steady supply of goods and customers. The population is chiefly [human](/content/species/sapient/humans), with a small community of [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) involved in metalwork and the maintenance of the bridge and roads. The dwarven stonemasons of Ashford are the heirs of the engineers who built the original bridge, and the maintenance of the structure has passed down through generations within the same families. The inns of Ashford are among the finest in the northern valleys, and the innkeepers are accustomed to the needs of merchants, drivers, and travellers who pass through the city on the converging roads.

## Primary Hub of Northern Communication

Ashford is the primary hub of communication and trade in the northern river valleys. Its bridge and roads are the arteries through which the commerce of the north flows. The city is the most important settlement of the region after [Thornwick](/content/location/settlement/city/thornwick), and its position at the river crossing has given it a strategic significance recognised since the earliest days of human settlement. Ashford is a key point in the road network of the [Northern Marches](/content/government/national/northern-marches), and its prosperity is a direct consequence of the traffic that passes through its streets and over its bridges.

## From Herdsmen's Crossing to the Dwarven Bridge

Ashford was founded at the ford that gave the city its name, a crossing that had been used by herdsmen and traders since before any permanent settlement existed. The first inhabitants were travellers and herdsmen who used the crossing, and the settlement that grew around it was a collection of inns and stables serving the valley traffic. The village grew into a town as trade in the northern valleys developed. The construction of the stone bridge in a later age transformed the crossing from a seasonal passage to a year-round route. The bridge was built by dwarven engineers, whose expertise is evident in the quality of the construction and the durability of the structure, which has required only routine maintenance in the centuries since its completion.

The growth of the road network converging on Ashford was the other great factor in the city's development. The roads connecting the northern valleys to the rest of the continent all pass through the crossing, and the city has grown and thrived on the traffic that flows along them. The second bridge was built in a later century to accommodate increasing traffic, and the two bridges now span the river in parallel, carrying the commerce of the north between the two halves of the city. The second bridge is a plainer structure than the first, built for function rather than appearance, but it has proved equally durable and now carries as much traffic as its older companion.
