## A Toll-House at the Crossing of the Vintner's Vale

At the centre of the [Vintner's Vale](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/vintners-vale), where the paved road of the [Laying the Stone Roads](/content/history/laying-the-stone-roads) crosses the river by the great bridge that the vintner-houses of the towns erected in the early generations of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace), the [Vintners and Cellarers](/content/government/local/vintners-and-cellarers) maintains a small toll-house and watchpost called the Vintner's Bridge Toll. The Council raised the toll-house in the same season as the bridge and has kept it in repair ever since. Within its walls dwells a small garrison of watchmen who oversee the crossing and collect the modest levy that funds the upkeep of both bridge and road.

Merchants and travellers who journey along this route regard the post with considerable favour, for the revenues gathered here have sustained the bridge across several centuries and contributed no small portion to the flourishing of the towns of the vale. A traveller approaching from either direction sees the toll-house first as a dark speck against the grey stone of the bridge, then as a solid building whose chimney-smoke promises shelter and order.

## The Five Stone Arches and the Sworn Examiners

Five broad arches of stone carry the road across the river. The vintner-houses raised this structure jointly, each contributing according to the size of its vineyards, and the Council has overseen its upkeep from that time to the present. Land trade throughout the vale depends upon these arches; without them merchants would be forced to ford the river at uncertain crossings or delay their journeys by many days. Grain from the eastern fields, timber from the upland forests, casks of wine from every vintner-house all cross by this bridge.

The watchmen inspect the arches at regular intervals and repair the stonework as the inspections require. Each season the master of the Council dispatches a sworn examiner to measure the settlement of the piers and the wear upon the keystones. The records of these examinations, preserved in the Council archives at the principal town-house, document no significant deterioration since the third generation of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace), when a spring flood scoured the mortar from the westernmost pier and necessitated its repointing.

## The Two-Storey Stone Building and the Iron-Bound Chest

A small stone building of two stories stands at the northern end of the bridge. It contains the office where the toll is collected and the dwellings in which the watchmen live. The builders employed the same stone and manner of construction as the bridge itself, so that the toll-house appears to grow from the same foundation as the arches. Mullioned windows of horn admit light to the upper chamber where the senior watchman keeps his quarters. A stout door of oak, banded with iron and fitted with a lock of cunning workmanship, guards the entrance.

The master of the Council, in consultation with the sworn members, sets the toll. Every merchant who crosses with a laden cart must pay it. The rate has remained stable for several generations: a single copper mark per cart, half that for a pack-horse, nothing for a man on foot or a rider. The master audits the accounts twice a year, comparing the sums collected against the tallies of crossing recorded by the watchmen. The Council's scribes enter every payment into ledgers bound in calfskin, stored in an iron-bound chest that sits in the corner of the toll-office, its key kept on a chain about the senior watchman's neck. No discrepancy has been found in the accounts since the thirty-seventh year of the Long Peace, when a junior clerk was discovered to have mis-entered seventeen payments and was dismissed from the Council's service.

## The Upper Windows, Pigeons, and Daily Messengers

The watchmen maintain at the toll-house a post serving as the principal point of surveillance along the road. From the upper windows a watchman can observe the approaches in both directions for nearly half a league. He notes the passage of every merchant, pedlar, messenger, and traveller, and reports any incident to the master of the Council by mounted messenger. In winter, when snow lies deep upon the road and travel slows to a crawl, the watchmen keep a small coop of pigeons for urgent messages. The birds are seldom used save in the deepest cold, but their presence reassures the towns that the post will not be isolated when the roads become impassable.

The post also serves as the principal point of contact between the Council and the magistrates of the towns of the vale. The watchmen transmit the orders of the master to the magistrates and carry the magistrates' reports back to the master. This regular correspondence binds the vale together in a network of communication. A messenger leaves the toll-house for one of the towns every morning, and a messenger returns every evening, so that news never travels more than a single day between any settlement in the vale and the Council seat.

## The Travellers' Shelter and the Watchmen's Quarters

Behind the toll-house stand several small buildings of stone, erected in the same season by the same vintner-houses. They provide shelter for travellers caught upon the road in time of storm. Any traveller who reaches the toll-house after nightfall or in foul weather may claim a space on the bench in the common room, a ration of bread and ale, and clean straw for a bed. No payment is demanded; many travellers leave a small offering in the chest that stands by the door before they depart at dawn, but this is custom rather than obligation.

The quarters also house the watchmen who serve at the toll-house. Four watchmen are assigned at any one time: two on duty, two at rest, their watches rotating every four hours through the day and night. Their tour lasts thirty days, after which they return to the towns and are relieved by four others. The vintner-houses deliver supplies at the beginning of each season. The Council's steward reckons these deliveries and records them in a separate ledger, audited alongside the toll accounts. Each watchman also brings his own knife, bowl, and blanket.

## The Enduring Instrument of the Council's Authority

The Vintner's Bridge Toll stands as a modest but enduring instrument of the Council's authority. The bridge, toll-house, watchpost, quarters, and toll itself together constitute a compact inheritance that the present age has received from the founders of the Long Peace. Merchants and travellers commemorate the founding of the toll-house with an annual ceremony on the first day of autumn, when the master of the Council and the sworn members of the vintner-houses gather at the northern end of the bridge. The master speaks a few words affirming the Council's commitment to maintenance of the road, a cask of the oldest wine in the vale is broached and shared among those present, and the watchmen light a bonfire that burns through the night.
