## A Lighthouse at the Western Entrance to the Strait

At the western entrance to the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), where the strait opens into the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), the council of [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) maintains a small lighthouse and watchpost known as Greyring Light. The lighthouse was erected in the early generations of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace) and has been maintained by the [Northern Roads Watch](/content/government/local/northern-roads-watch) ever since. A small garrison of the Watch keeps the light and oversees navigation of the strait.

The lighthouse is one of the more important outposts of the cold north, held in particular esteem by the pilots and merchants who rely upon the light for safe navigation. The light has been the foundation of safe navigation through the strait for several centuries. Without its guiding beam, the western approach to the Velden Strait would be far more dangerous, for the waters at the mouth of the channel are studded with submerged rocks and the currents shift unpredictably where the ocean meets the confined waters of the strait. The pilots of [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) consider Greyring Light the first and most essential of the navigation aids along the passage, and the merchants who trade through the strait acknowledge that the prosperity of their commerce depends upon the safe arrival of vessels that the light has guided through the most treacherous section of the approach.

## The Stone Tower and the Great Oil Lamp

The lighthouse is a stone tower of four stories with a stout door and roof, built upon a rocky islet that lies just within the western entrance to the strait. The tower is constructed of the same dark grey stone that forms the islet itself, giving it the appearance of having grown from the rock. The lower chamber contains a small hearth for warmth and cooking, a store of provisions sufficient for the garrison's tour, and racks for the arms of the watch. The middle floors house the sleeping quarters and the workroom where the keeper prepares the oil and trims the wicks. The lantern room occupies the upper story, fitted with the great oil lamp that the lighthouse keepers have maintained since the founding of the tower. The lamp is a work of careful craftsmanship, its polished metal mirrors directing the flame into a beam visible for many leagues in clear weather. The keeper trims the wicks at dusk and signals the state of the tide by the colour of the light shown, a practice that the pilots of the strait have learned to read with the same ease as they read the charts.

## The Sergeant, the Four Men, and the Register

The garrison consists of a sergeant and four men who serve a tour of two months before being relieved by a fresh company from the mainland. The sergeant is responsible for maintaining the lamp, inspecting the beacons that mark the outer reefs, and keeping the register of vessels that pass through the strait. The register is a careful record kept in duplicate, one copy remaining at the lighthouse and the other sent to the magistrates of Velden at the end of each month. It notes the name of every vessel, its port of origin, its declared cargo, the direction of its passage, and the state of the weather at the time of its sighting. The magistrates rely upon this register when they judge the state of navigation and the volume of trade passing through the strait, and a sergeant who maintains a slovenly register is remembered with little favour by his successors. The garrison also maintains small boats that ferry supplies and relief crews to the lighthouse from the mainland, for the islet is accessible only by water and the boats are the principal means of communication between the lighthouse and the town.

## The Black-and-White Pillars and the Signal Chain

The beacons of the strait were erected by the council of [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) at the same time as the lighthouse and have been maintained by the Standing Watch ever since. The beacons stand upon the rocks and shoals that line the western approach, their stout stone pillars painted with bands of white and black that are visible from a considerable distance in daylight. By night, small lanterns are hung from arms projecting from the pillars, giving the pilots a chain of lights to follow as they pick their way through the dangerous waters. The beacons serve as the marks that pilots use to set their course, and a beacon that has been extinguished by storm or neglect is a serious matter, for a vessel that loses its bearing in the darkness may find itself upon the rocks before the crew can correct its course.

The beacons are connected by a chain of signals that the garrison exchanges with neighbouring towers on the mainland. When a vessel is sighted approaching from the ocean, the garrison lights a signal fire whose smoke is visible to the watchtowers on the cliffs above. These towers pass the message along the coast by shuttered lamps and flags, so that the harbormaster of Velden may know of an approaching vessel hours before it reaches the harbour mouth. The same chain of signals carries news of storms, of unusual tides, and of any hazard that may have appeared in the channel, and the system is maintained at considerable expense by the council of Velden, who regard it as essential to the safety of the strait trade.

## The Currents, Charts, and the Pilots' Guild

The waters of the strait are treacherous, and their navigation has been the subject of careful study by the pilots of the Distance-Craft since the founding of the tower. The pilots maintain the charts of the strait, updating them whenever a storm shifts a shoal or a new rock is discovered. These charts are kept in the archives of the pilots' guild at Velden, and copies are carried aboard every vessel that makes regular use of the passage. The pilots also oversee the larger vessels that pass through the strait, boarding them at the entrance and directing them through the dangerous waters with a care that has made the Velden pilotage the standard by which all other pilotage is judged. A captain who refuses the services of a pilot does so at considerable risk, for the currents of the strait can seize a vessel and carry it onto the rocks before the most experienced mariner can react.

## The Monthly Register and the Barometer Readings

The records of the lighthouse are maintained by the sergeant and sent to the harbormaster of [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) at the end of every month. They include the register of vessels, readings of the barometer and wind, and any unusual events observed during the tour. These records are the most complete of the navigation records of the strait and are consulted by every pilot who must judge a question of coastal navigation. A pilot preparing to guide a vessel through the western approach will study the recent records to learn of any changes in the channel, any wreckage that may have been sighted, or any unusual behaviour of the currents. The records are preserved in the archives of the magistrates of Velden, who have maintained them with care since the founding of the lighthouse, and the series is among the longest continuous records of navigation in the known world.

## The Foundation of Navigation Through the Strait

Greyring Light is one of the more important outposts of the cold north and the foundation of navigation through the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden). The lighthouse, beacons, garrison, charts, and pilots together form the system that has made the strait safe for the commerce of three continents. The lighthouse is held in particular memory by the pilots and merchants who rely upon it, and they mark the anniversaries of the founding with ceremonies attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every town of the coast. The light that burns on the islet at the western entrance to the strait is a symbol of the order and the security that the Long Peace has brought to the northern seas, and the keepers who maintain it are honoured for their service to the navigation of the known world.
