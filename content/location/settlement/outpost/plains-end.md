## Overview

At the eastern limit of the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains), where the grasslands break into the stony rises that presage the foothills of the distant mountains, the magistrates of [Serren](/content/location/settlement/city/serren) maintain a small watchpost and a beacon that the chroniclers of the academies have named Plains' End. The garrison raised the first walls during the early generations of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace), and their successors have held the post without interruption ever since. A mixed community of soldiers and herdsmen dwells within the shadow of the beacon, governed jointly by the sergeant of the garrison and the elders of the pastoral clans who drive their flocks across the eastern pastures.

Few outposts of the inland valleys lie farther from the walls of a town. The merchants who travel the eastern road and the pastoralists who camp upon the open grass regard Plains' End with an esteem that borders on reverence, for the beacon and the garrison together have preserved the peace of these plains across several centuries. The magistrates of Serren reckon the prosperity of their eastern trade in no small part upon the security that this post affords.

## The Watchpost

A square stone structure of two storeys commands a low rise that overlooks the meeting of grass and scree. Its door is banded in iron; its roof is shingled in slate carried from the quarries west of Serren at no small expense. From the upper windows a sentry may survey the plains as far as the haze permits and mark any approach along the eastern track long before a rider draws near enough to hail the gate. Within, a single hearth heats the common room, and a narrow stair climbs to the sleeping loft and the door that opens onto the beacon tower. The walls are fitted with racks for arms, shelves for provisions, and a locked chest that holds the garrison register.

The watchpost is held by a sergeant and four soldiers, each rotation lasting two months. The sergeant bears responsibility for the beacon, the inspection of the eastern approaches, and the registration of every pastoralist and merchant who passes. The register, kept in a firm hand upon parchment supplied by the magistrates, records the names, the direction of travel, and the number of beasts in each company; upon these pages the magistrates rely when they judge the state of the plains and the safety of the road. A careless entry can obscure a pattern of banditry or a dwindling herd, and sergeants who keep a slovenly register are remembered with little kindness by their successors.

## The Beacon

The tower that rises above the watchpost roof was built in the same season as the post itself, and its fire has been lit every night since, save for a handful of storm-scoured evenings when no flame could be kindled. In clear weather the light carries for many leagues across the grass, a fixed star that guides the traveller home. The keeper ascends at dusk to trim the wicks and feed the iron basket with seasoned wood; during the day he builds smokes of damp straw and green leaves to signal the approach of armed companies, whether friend or foe. Merchants caught upon the plains at nightfall confess that the first glimpse of the beacon, small and steady upon its rise, settles the fear that gathers in the dark.

Among the public works of the inland valleys, the beacons of the eastern plains are accounted the most vital. Each depends upon the others: when Plains' End lights its tower, the next post to the west answers within the hour, and so the news travels to Serren faster than any rider. The pastoralists speak of the beacon as a living thing, one that must be fed and tended and that repays the care with protection.

## The Pastoralists

The clans who move their flocks across the Great Grass Plains are the watchpost's most frequent visitors. They arrive at the gate with wool and pressed cheese, seeking grain, tools, and news from the towns. The exchange that has grown up around this traffic benefits both parties: the pastoralists receive goods they cannot produce upon the open grass, and the towns receive raw materials that feed their looms and their tables. Several families have dealt with Plains' End across four generations, and the elders of those clans know the sergeants by name and the sergeants in turn know the lineage of every flock.

Governance among the pastoralists rests with the clan elders, who adjudicate disputes over pasture and water, determine the routes of the seasonal drift, and keep the peace among the young men who might otherwise settle a grievance with knives. The elders meet with the garrison sergeant at the first gathering of autumn to coordinate the movement of herds and to announce the anniversaries of the founding, which are marked with a ceremony that includes the lighting of a secondary fire upon the rise and the recitation of the names of those who have held the post.

## The Quarters

Alongside the watchpost stand three stone shelters built to the same pattern as the main building, though lower and without towers. These quarters house the garrison during their tour and offer refuge to travellers overtaken by storm or darkness. Each shelter contains a hearth, bunks for six, and a small larder; the roofs are reinforced against the heavy winds that sweep across the plains in the deep winter. The magistrates of Serren dispatch a supply wagon at the beginning of each season — flour, salt, dried meat, lamp oil, fodder for the garrison's horses, and fresh parchment for the register. The arrival of the wagon is an event that draws the pastoralists from miles around, for it carries also the letters and the small luxuries that remind the garrison of the world beyond the grass.

## Significance

Plains' End occupies a place in the memory of the eastern settlements that outweighs its modest dimensions. The watchpost, the beacon, the quarters, and the commerce they sustain together form the inheritance that the early generations of the Long Peace left to the present age. The anniversaries of the founding draw magistrates and principal citizens from every town of the plains, who gather at the rise to observe the ceremonies and to reaffirm the compact between the towns and the garrison. The pastoralists light their secondary fire, the elders recite the names, and the beacon burns brighter than on any other night of the year, visible from every corner of the Great Grass Plains as a promise that the eastern limit remains guarded.
