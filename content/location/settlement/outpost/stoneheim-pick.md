## Overview

[Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) Pick is a small inhabited road station in the highlands of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), situated at the junction where the [Stoneheim Highland Road](/content/location/route/trade/stoneheim-highland-road) divides toward the central halls, the eastern mines, and the lower coast. The settlement stands beneath a dark ridge whose profile resembles the head of a miner's pick, giving the station its name. Its purpose is to serve the wagons and pack trains that cross the mountain routes, but it also acts as a depot for road materials, a place of exchange for mining families, and a refuge when snow or rockfall closes the higher road. Stoneheim Pick is governed by the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim), though its daily affairs are handled by a warden and a council of resident households.

The station is too small for a city wall and too exposed for a loose village. Its buildings form a compact crescent around a paved yard, with the storehouses on the uphill side and the stable galleries below. A broad gate opens toward the main road, while a narrower passage leads to the old mine track that gives the settlement access to stone and ore. The permanent population includes wardens, smiths, stable keepers, cooks, masons, and the families who supply them. During the open season the number of inhabitants may double, as merchants, miners, and hired guards wait for the roads to clear or gather into a convoy.

## The Ridge and the Junction

The ridge above the station is made of hard black stone veined with pale mineral seams. It rises sharply on one side and falls in broken shelves on the other, creating a natural shelter from the strongest valley winds. The road uses the saddle behind the ridge rather than the lower ravine, where spring floods can carry away loose ground. From the saddle, travellers can see the upper line of the Stoneheim road and the smoke from the forges of distant [Ironhollow](/content/location/settlement/town/ironhollow) when the air is clear. The view gives the station's wardens early knowledge of arriving traffic and of clouds gathering over the higher passes.

The three roads meet in the central yard. The main route runs toward Stoneheim, carrying finished metalwork and returning with grain and timber. The eastern road descends toward the iron workings and the lesser holds, while the western track joins the routes that lead toward the [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) coast and the [Frostwall Pass](/content/location/route/trade/frostwall-pass). None of the branches is equal in width or condition. The Stoneheim road is paved for much of its upper length, the mine road is rough and heavy with ore traffic, and the western road requires frequent repair after storms. The station's warden assigns crews according to the volume and danger of each branch.

The old mine track remains in use even though the nearest working has been exhausted. Its galleries now provide stone for repairs, and the lower tunnels hold dry storage rooms that remain cool throughout the summer. The miners who first occupied the ridge left marks on the rock showing distances to water and safe stopping places. Later road builders copied these marks into the official wayfinding system, and the symbols are still visible on the walls near the station gate. Stoneheim Pick thus retains a connection to the mining work that made the surrounding road necessary in the first place.

## Buildings and Services

The largest building is the Road Hall, a long stone structure with a hearth at either end and sleeping platforms along the walls. Travellers who arrive without a private tent can rent a place by the night, while members of an organised convoy receive space according to the warden's list. The hall also serves as a meeting place for merchants and road crews. Cargo disputes, requests for escorts, and announcements about closures are made there in public, because the station's position requires that no single house control all information about the junction.

The stable galleries are cut partly into the ridge. Their roofs are low, their doors broad, and their floors sloped toward drains that carry waste away from the cistern. The keepers care for mountain ponies, mules, and the heavy draft animals used on ore wagons. Animals unable to continue are not abandoned. The station may purchase them at a reduced price, keep them through the winter, or arrange for their return when a caravan comes back down the road. The practice prevents panic during a journey and ensures that a weakened animal does not collapse on a dangerous slope.

The smithy occupies a separate building because sparks and stored fodder do not mix. Its forge repairs wheel rims, chain, harness buckles, picks, shovels, and the iron fittings of the road gates. The smiths also maintain the station's supply of pitons, bridge clamps, and snow shovels. A merchant may consider these tools secondary to a cargo of metalwork, but a damaged wagon cannot be moved without them. The smiths therefore have authority to delay a convoy when its equipment is unsafe, a power that is accepted grudgingly by inexperienced travellers and with gratitude by those who have crossed the high road before.

## The People and Governance

Stoneheim Pick is chiefly a dwarven settlement, though human merchants and road workers are present during the trading season. The permanent households are tied to the road rather than to one mine or one forge. Some families keep horses, some maintain the cistern, and others have held the warden's office or the station records for generations. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the neighbouring holds regard these occupations as public service, and station families receive certain rights to pasture and quarry in exchange for keeping the road open.

The warden is appointed by officials of Stoneheim and confirmed by the households of the station. The warden controls the gates, assigns sleeping places, orders emergency repairs, and reports the condition of the branches to the hold council. A small household council advises on stores, prices, and the distribution of labour. Its meetings are practical and usually brief, though disputes over priority at the smithy or access to the stable can become serious when a convoy is waiting. The station's laws require the warden to explain any decision that affects a whole caravan, a custom intended to keep authority from becoming arbitrary in a place where delays can cost a merchant a season.

The human visitors contribute their own knowledge. Coastal merchants know the price of iron and steel beyond the mountains, while the road crews from the lower valleys understand the changing condition of bridges and slopes. The station keeps a guest ledger in which these visitors may record information about markets, weather, and road damage. The entries are read aloud during the evening meal, making the Road Hall a source of news for communities that otherwise hear from the wider world only a few times each year.

## Trade and Stores

The station handles cargo rather than producing much of its own. Wagons from Stoneheim carry tools, weapons, fittings, and worked stone, while returning trains bring grain, salt, cloth, timber, and the coastal goods required by the highland holds. The ore road adds iron, copper, and lesser mineral loads. Merchants may break a large consignment at Stoneheim Pick, selling part to a passing buyer or storing it until the road ahead reopens. The station's scale makes it useful for such exchanges. A city warehouse can hold more, but it cannot respond as quickly to a blocked pass or a sudden shortage in a remote settlement.

The storehouses are built with thick walls and raised floors. Grain is kept in sealed bins, cloth in dry rooms, and iron under covered sheds where meltwater cannot rust the surfaces. The station maintains a reserve belonging to the holds rather than to any private merchant. It is used during closures and replaced when the route reopens. The reserve includes flour, dried beans, salt, lamp oil, fodder, and timber for emergency bridge work. The warden may refuse a sale when stores fall below the level set by Stoneheim, even if a merchant offers a high price.

The settlement's market is held on the first clear day after a major convoy arrives. Miners from nearby workings bring stone, ore samples, and rough tools, while travellers sell small goods acquired on the coast. The market is less formal than the exchanges in Stoneheim, but its distance from the great halls gives local producers a chance to deal directly with buyers. The station benefits from the activity through stall fees and service charges, yet its prosperity depends more on steady traffic than on one exceptional sale.

## Weather and Safety

Snow can close the upper road before it reaches the station, leaving Stoneheim Pick as the last open refuge for travellers descending from the ridge. The inhabitants store fuel for the Road Hall and keep the stable galleries ready for animals brought in from the storm. Wind drives snow against the gate, and crews must clear the central yard repeatedly before a convoy can move. During thaw, the danger changes to falling stone and swollen streams. The station maintains signal poles on the ridge and sends runners to inspect the branches after heavy rain.

The road is generally secure under the protection of the holds, but isolated theft and raiding occur on the lesser tracks. Stoneheim Pick does not maintain a large garrison. Instead, it can call upon passing guards, nearby mine crews, and the patrols assigned to the main route. The warden keeps a list of available fighters and pack animals, and the station's bell signals when assistance is required. Travellers are expected to defend the yard if danger reaches the gate, though the buildings are designed to shelter rather than to withstand a prolonged siege.

## History and Significance

Stoneheim Pick began as a quarry camp beside the junction of old mining paths. The first permanent household formed around the workers who repaired the route, and the settlement grew when the holds decided that a central highland depot was necessary. The paved yard and the Road Hall were built during a period of increased metal exports, when ore trains became too numerous for the scattered shelters of the earlier road. Later improvements added the stable galleries, the cistern, and the signal poles that connect the station to the neighbouring passes.

The settlement remains a small but essential part of the Stoneheim road system. It gathers the scattered work of wardens, smiths, miners, and merchants into one dependable point, allowing the highland trade to continue despite distance and weather. Stoneheim Pick has no great hall and no celebrated masterwork, yet its services allow the products of the great halls to reach the world beyond the mountains. The ridge above it is a landmark, but the station itself is the true mark of the road: a place where every load is checked, every traveller can find shelter, and the highland routes are joined into a road worthy of their name.
