## Overview

The Kharesh Badlands are a broken and arid country at the northern margin of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), the great desert interior of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), where the foothills of the coastal highlands give way to the desert proper. The region takes its name from the deep gullies and the strange rock formations that the seasonal rains have carved into the soft stone, producing a landscape of ravines, pillars, and flat-topped mesas that is impassable to the unwary and yet crossed by ancient caravan routes. The badlands are not a kingdom or a single settlement but a corridor of difficult country that lies between the fertile northern coast and the deep desert, and the communities that inhabit the region have adapted their lives to the demands of the terrain.

The Kharesh Badlands are distinct from both the true desert and the coastal strip, and their character is shaped by the meeting of the two. The foothills provide occasional shelter and seasonal water, while the deep ravines that cut through the soft stone conceal springs and pasturage that are invisible to those who do not know the land. The caravans that travel between the coast and the interior must pass through the badlands, and the routes they follow have been established over centuries of travel and adjusted to the shifting conditions of the desert margin.

## Geography and Climate

The landscape of the badlands is a maze of ravines, gullies, and broken ridges, carved by the brief but violent rains that fall during the winter months. The underlying stone is soft and easily eroded, and the water has cut into it over the centuries to produce a terrain of steep-sided valleys and isolated mesas that is one of the most distinctive in the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). The gullies can be deep enough to swallow a caravan, and the unwary traveller who attempts to cross the badlands without a guide is at risk of becoming lost in the labyrinth. The seasoned caravanners know the routes by the position of distant peaks and the colour of the stone, and they read the land with a precision that no outsider can match.

The climate of the badlands is hot and dry, though less extreme than the deep desert beyond. The summer sun beats down upon the exposed rock with a ferocity that makes travel during the day difficult, and the temperature of the stone itself can burn unprotected skin. The nights are cool, for the rock radiates its heat rapidly into the clear sky, and the caravan companies that travel the badlands prefer to move by night during the summer months, resting in shaded gullies during the worst of the heat. The winter months are milder, and the rains that fall during the season fill the dry channels and bring a brief flush of green to the more sheltered valleys.

The water sources of the badlands are few and scattered, and their positions are known only to the experienced. Some are springs that emerge at the base of the rock formations, sustained by the deep aquifers that lie beneath the desert. Others are cisterns that have been cut into the stone by the inhabitants of past centuries and are maintained by the present communities. The most reliable of these sources are the seasonal pools that form in the deeper ravines after the winter rains, and the caravans that travel the badlands plan their journeys to take advantage of these temporary waters.

## Settlements and Peoples

The permanent settlements of the badlands are few, and they are concentrated at the points where the water sources are most reliable and the routes converge. The most substantial of these is [Kheled](/content/location/settlement/village/kheled), the upland settlement in one of the rocky massifs that rise from the desert floor, whose springs and mines sustain a community that is among the most isolated in the known world. Smaller encampments and way stations are scattered across the badlands, and they are occupied seasonally by the herders and the miners who work the country. The caravans that pass through the badlands stop at these stations to rest and to take on water, and the station keepers are a familiar sight to every caravan master who travels the routes.

The population of the badlands is predominantly [human](/content/species/sapient/humans), and the people are among the most experienced in the desert environment. The herders move their flocks between the sheltered valleys in patterns that have been established over generations, taking advantage of the seasonal pastures that emerge after the rains. The miners work the deposits of copper, salt, and the precious stones that are found in the rocky uplands, and the mineral output of the badlands is a significant contribution to the trade of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact). The caravan masters who guide the caravans through the badlands are the most experienced of all, and their knowledge of the routes and the water sources is a valued inheritance.

The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) are present in the badlands as well, and their jackal-headed form and their keen senses are well suited to the demands of the terrain. The salawa of the badlands are fewer in number than their kindred in the deep desert, but they are respected for their tracking and their endurance, and they are frequent visitors to the settlements and the caravan stations. The [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), the confederation of nomadic peoples of the Kharesh, include the salawa and the human nomads who traverse the badlands, and their governance of the routes and the water sources is the foundation of the regional order.

## Economy and the Caravan Trade

The economy of the badlands is built upon the caravan trade that passes through the region and the mineral wealth that is extracted from the rocky uplands. The caravans that travel between the northern coast and the inland cities must cross the badlands, and the services that the region provides to the caravans, including water, provisions, and guides, are the foundation of the local economy. The tolls and the fees that are collected at the way stations are a significant source of income, and the station keepers are bound by the covenants of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) to maintain the water sources and to provide for the caravans at a fair rate.

The mineral deposits of the badlands include copper, salt, and the precious stones that are among the most valued products of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The copper is smelted at small works near the mines and carried by the caravans to the markets of the coast. The salt is extracted from the deposits that lie beneath the rocky uplands and transported in blocks that are wrapped in cloth for the journey. The precious stones, including the carnelians, agates, and the occasional sardonyx, are mined from the deepest workings and traded to the caravans at prices that reflect the difficulty of the extraction and the rarity of the stones.

The herders of the badlands keep flocks of goats and sheep that are suited to the sparse pasturage of the sheltered valleys, and the wool, the leather, and the dried meat that are produced from these animals are traded to the caravans and to the settlements of the desert margin. The gardens that are cultivated in the most sheltered valleys produce dates, figs, and the hardy grains that can tolerate the arid conditions, and the surplus is exchanged for the goods that the region cannot produce for itself.

## Routes and Hazards

The routes that cross the badlands are among the most challenging in the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), and the caravans that travel them require experienced guides and careful preparation. The most direct route follows the line of the seasonal watercourses, but the channels can flood with little warning during the winter rains, and the caravans must be ready to take refuge on the high ground at any moment. The routes that follow the higher ground are safer from the floods but pass through more exposed country, where the heat and the thirst are the principal dangers. The guides choose among the routes according to the season, the weather, and the condition of the water sources, and their judgement is the difference between a successful journey and a disaster.

The hazards of the badlands are not limited to the weather and the terrain. The deep ravines conceal the lairs of snakes and scorpions, and the unwary traveller who reaches into a crevice without looking is at risk of a dangerous bite. The flash floods that follow the rains can sweep a caravan away in minutes, and the dust storms that rise from the desert can reduce visibility to nothing and choke the unwary. The bandits who prey upon the caravans have long used the badlands as a refuge, and the patrols of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) are a necessary escort for the more vulnerable companies.

The cairns that mark the routes are a distinctive feature of the badlands landscape, and the maintenance of the cairns is one of the responsibilities of the communities that live along the caravan roads. The cairns are built of the dark stone that is found in the region, and they stand above the scrub as silent guides to the traveller who has lost the way. A cairn may be the work of a single season or the accumulation of many generations, and the inscriptions and the offerings that are placed at the larger cairns are a record of the travellers who have passed and the events that have marked the routes.

## Governance and Custom

The governance of the badlands is informal and practical, resting with the caravan masters, the station keepers, and the elders of the herding and mining communities. The covenants of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) provide the framework within which these local authorities operate, and the pact's regulations on the maintenance of the water sources and the treatment of the caravans are enforced by the station keepers and the guides who work the routes. The council of the pact meets at the inland cities and hears the disputes that arise in the badlands, but the day-to-day governance is in the hands of the people who know the country and who depend upon the caravans for their livelihood.

The customs of the badlands are shaped by the demands of the caravan trade and the harshness of the environment. Hospitality is a sacred obligation, and the station keepers are bound by tradition to provide water and shelter to any traveller who arrives at their doors, regardless of the traveler's ability to pay. The water sources are shared according to long-established rules, and a station keeper who denies water to a traveller in need is subject to the censure of the pact and the disgrace of the community. The covenants that govern the routes and the water sources are recorded in the oral traditions of the badlands, and they are transmitted from one generation to the next by the elders and the guides.

## Significance

The Kharesh Badlands are the corridor through which the trade of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) passes on its way between the coast and the interior, and the services that the region provides to the caravans are a vital component of the economy of the continent. The badlands are not a wealthy region, but they are a strategic one, and the communities that inhabit the difficult country have built their lives upon the steady flow of the caravans and the mineral wealth that lies beneath their feet. The covenants that govern the routes and the water sources are a model of the cooperation that sustains the desert trade, and the badlands stand as a reminder that the prosperity of the cities rests upon the labour and the knowledge of the people who dwell in the most demanding country between the coast and the deep desert.
