## Overview

The [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) Wastes are the deep and largely unmapped interior of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), the great belt of desert and scrub that lies beyond the known caravan routes and beyond the settled territories of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact). The wastes take their name from the continent itself, and the word is used by the chroniclers of the coast and the inland cities to describe the country that lies at the far edge of the caravan maps, where the routes grow uncertain and the names of the landmarks are known only to the nomads who traverse them. The Sahrani Wastes are not a single region but the sum of the unmapped places, the deep desert, the salt flats, and the rocky uplands that extend toward the inland seas and the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) in the far south.

The wastes are among the most demanding environments in the known world, and the peoples who inhabit them are few and scattered. The [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) of the deep desert and the [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) nomads who travel with them are the primary inhabitants, and their knowledge of the routes and the water sources is unmatched by any outsider. The communities that dwell in the wastes have no written records and no formal governance, and their way of life is shaped by the demands of the desert and the ancient traditions of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), the confederation to which most of the deep-desert peoples belong.

## Geography and Climate

The Sahrani Wastes are a desert of many characters, and their appearance changes dramatically from one part to another. In some places the land is a true erg, a vast field of shifting dunes whose crests rise hundreds of feet above the interdune corridors and whose flanks are smooth and difficult to climb. In other places the ground is flat and stony, a reg of pebbles and gravel that extends to the horizon without relief, broken only by the occasional outcrop of dark stone. The salt flats that lie in the deeper depressions of the wastes are perhaps the most desolate of all, and the surface of the salt can crack into sharp polygons that cut the feet of the unwary and the hooves of the animals that stray onto them.

The rocky uplands that rise from the sands are the most habitable parts of the wastes, and the massifs that stand above the desert floor are islands of life in the sea of sand. The springs that emerge at the base of these uplands are fed by the deep aquifers that lie beneath the desert, and the flow of the springs is constant and reliable, unaffected by the seasonal variations in rainfall. The valleys that lie between the uplands are sheltered from the wind and the sand, and the gardens that are cultivated in the most sheltered of these valleys produce dates and figs that are valued by the caravans that pass through.

The climate of the wastes is one of extremes, even by the standards of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). The summer sun beats down upon the open sand with a ferocity that can kill the unprepared within hours, and the surface temperature of the dunes is hot enough to burn unprotected skin. The winter nights are bitterly cold, for the sand radiates its heat rapidly into the clear sky, and the temperature can drop to near freezing even during the summer months. Rainfall is rare and unpredictable, arriving in brief and violent storms that may flood the interdune corridors before draining away into the sand.

## The Deep Desert and Its Peoples

The deep desert is the domain of the [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa), the jackal-headed people whose keen senses and their endurance are ideally suited to the demands of the wastes. The salawa travel in family groups, following routes that have been established over generations of oral tradition and that are adjusted according to the condition of the water sources and the movement of the game. The salawa are formidable hunters and patient trackers, and their knowledge of the desert and its ways is the foundation of their survival. The [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the deep desert are fewer in number, and they coexist with the salawa in a relationship of mutual respect and cooperation, each pursuing their own routes and their own prey but sharing the water sources and the grazing grounds according to the conventions of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes).

The confederation of the Kharesh Tribes extends its authority over the deep desert through the council of elders that convenes at the [Sands' Assembly](/content/culture/sands-assembly), a gathering that is held at a designated oasis at a time determined by the movement of the tribes and the condition of the water sources. The decisions of the Assembly are binding on all the members of the confederation, and the regulations that govern the use of the water sources, the passage of the caravans, and the conduct of the hunting are enforced by the elders of the individual kindreds. The confederation has no permanent force and no fixed capital, and its governance rests entirely upon the consent of the peoples who belong to it and the accumulated wisdom of the elders who lead them.

The [orcs](/content/species/sapient/orcs) of the arid interior are present in the wilder and more remote reaches of the wastes, where their nomadic existence is similar in form to that of the human nomads but distinct in tradition. The orcs are competitors for the same scarce resources, and the salawa and the human nomads regard them with a wariness that is born of long acquaintance. Encounters between the peoples are rare and generally avoided, for the desert is wide enough that kindreds can pass their lives without meeting, and the water sources are sufficiently scattered that disputes over them are uncommon.

## Water and the Caravan Routes

The water sources of the wastes are the foundation of life in the desert, and the deep aquifers that feed them are the most precious resource of the region. The springs that emerge at the base of the rocky uplands are the most reliable, and the flow of these springs is constant through the seasons. The wells that have been sunk into the desert floor in the deeper reaches of the wastes are less reliable, for the water table lies far below the surface and the work of drawing water is exhausting. The seasonal pools that form in the depressions after the rains are temporary and cannot be relied upon, though the nomads who know the desert will travel far to take advantage of them when they appear.

The caravan routes that pass through the wastes are few and difficult, and they are followed only by the most experienced of the caravan masters. The routes connect the inland cities of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) with the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach), the federation of settled communities that inhabit the shores of the inland seas in the far south. The caravans that travel these routes carry the spices and the dyes of the deep south to the markets of the north, and they return with the grain, the cloth, and the manufactured goods that the southern communities cannot produce for themselves. The journey takes many weeks, and the margin between sufficiency and disaster is narrow, for a delayed arrival at the next water source can be fatal for the entire company.

The caravans are guided by the salawa and the human nomads who know the desert, and the fees that are paid for their services are a significant source of income for the kindreds of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes). The guides choose the routes according to the season and the condition of the water sources, and their judgement is the difference between a successful journey and the loss of the caravan. The guides travel on foot, for the horses and the camels of the caravans are unsuited to the demands of the deep desert, and the salawa guides in particular are capable of sustained travel that no other people can match.

## Hazards and Survival

The hazards of the Sahrani Wastes are many, and the margin between life and death is narrow. The summer heat is the most immediate danger, and the traveller who is caught in the open desert without water can be dead within hours. The mirages that rise from the heated sand are a constant danger to the inexperienced, for they can lead a traveller to walk for hours toward what appears to be a pool of water only to find nothing when the vision fades. The sandstorms that rise from the desert floor can strip the flesh from an unprotected face in minutes, and the caravans that are caught in such a storm must halt and cover themselves until the wind passes.

The wildlife of the wastes is adapted to the harshness of the environment, and the snakes, the scorpions, and the larger predators are a constant hazard to the traveller who sleeps on the open ground. The salawa are familiar with the habits of the desert fauna, and they teach the caravan masters the rules that must be observed at every camp: shake out the bedding before lying down, check the boots before putting them on, and never reach into a crevice without looking first. The herbs and the medicines that the salawa carry are the most effective treatment for the bites and the stings that the careless traveller may suffer, and the salawa guides are valued as much for their medicine as for their knowledge of the routes.

## Significance

The Sahrani Wastes are the final frontier of the known world, the deep interior that lies beyond the reach of the settled communities and the formal governance of the caravan cities. The wastes are not a productive region in the usual sense, for the desert yields little that the markets of the coast and the inland cities value, but the routes that cross the wastes are essential to the trade between the north and the south, and the peoples who dwell in the wastes are the guardians of those routes. The significance of the Sahrani Wastes lies in the mastery of the desert that the salawa and the human nomads have achieved, a mastery that no outsider has been able to replicate, and in the covenants of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) that keep the routes open and the water sources available to those who travel under the protection of the confederation.

The wastes are a reminder that the prosperity of the settled communities rests upon the labour and the knowledge of the peoples who inhabit the most demanding country in the known world. The salawa and the human nomads of the deep desert do not seek the wealth of the cities, but their guardianship of the caravan routes and the water sources is a service that no settled community could provide, and the trade that flows through the wastes is the foundation of the fortunes that are made in the markets of the coast and the inland cities. The Sahrani Wastes are thus both a barrier and a bridge, a region whose harshness is matched only by the resilience of the peoples who call it home.
