## Overview

Nordheim is the northernmost of the great settled landmasses described in the western charts, a continent of broad tundra, low forest, and mountain walls that hold the interior against the sea. Its southern coast faces a cold ocean of long swells and shifting fog, while its northern shore reaches into waters that freeze along the bays during the darkest months. The continent is not entirely barren. A narrow southern belt supports dense coniferous woodland, and the river valleys that descend from the interior provide summer pasture and short-season fields. Beyond those valleys lies the Frostwall Tundra, whose pale grasses, stunted shrubs, and frozen lakes cover much of northern Nordheim.

Nordheim is often confused in southern records with the coastal names [Nordhaven](/content/location/settlement/city/nordhaven) and Nordheim used by several old seafaring families. The continental name is older than those local usages. Its first meaning is uncertain, though most northern sources translate it as the northern home. The name describes climate rather than political unity, for Nordheim contains many independent settlements and travelling peoples whose common customs arise from the need to preserve food, shelter, and trustworthy routes through a country where winter can make a short journey into an isolation of many weeks.

## Mountains and Lowlands

The continent is divided by the [Whitewall Range](/content/location/natural/feature/whitewall-range), which runs east to west across its centre. The mountains are lower in the west but broader in the east, where glaciers descend through valleys and terminate in lakes of blue water. The northern face is steep and wind-scoured. The southern face has deeper forests, mineral springs, and a series of valleys that remain open to agriculture during the warm season. Several passes cross the range, but only three are used by laden caravans, and each is marked by refuge halls stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber.

South of the Whitewall Range, the land becomes rolling and wooded. Fir, black pine, silver birch, and red cedar dominate the lowlands, with open clearings formed by fire, windfall, and the grazing of elk. Rivers flow south from the range and broaden as they cross the forest, creating chains of wet meadows and shallow lakes. These waterways freeze at different times according to their depth and current, and experienced travellers know which channels remain open when the surface of the smaller lakes has already hardened.

The northern country is flatter. Ancient ice has scraped away much of the soil, leaving gravel ridges, shallow basins, and exposed shelves of dark rock. The Frostwall Tundra occupies this region, though its southern margin merges gradually into lichen woodland. In summer the ground softens only a short distance below the surface, and thousands of pools appear among the ridges. In winter the pools vanish beneath snow and ice, and the land becomes a continuous field of white broken by black stone and the tracks of travelling herds.

## Climate and Light

Nordheim has long winters and a brief summer in which every living thing grows, feeds, and reproduces with urgency. The first snow may arrive before the autumn harvest is complete in the north, while the southern valleys may remain clear for another month. Winter winds move from the interior toward the coast, carrying dry powder that fills hollows and leaves the ridges bare. Along the shore, storms are frequent but less severe than those that cross the open water, because the sea ice absorbs part of the wave energy during the coldest months.

The sun remains low for much of the winter and stays above the horizon through much of the northern summer. The long summer light supports an abundance of insects, flowering plants, and nesting birds, while the dim winter favours creatures that store fat, grow heavy coats, or live beneath the snow. The northern people use the changing light as a practical calendar. The first continuous twilight marks the time to bring boats above the tide line, and the return of a certain green moss to the south-facing stones signals that the river ice will soon weaken.

Rainfall is moderate in the south and scarce in the tundra. Snow supplies most of the northern water, and the spring thaw can produce floods that reshape river crossings. Settlements are built on terraces above the ordinary flood level, with plank walks and stone foundations that allow water to pass beneath rather than collect around the walls. The people of the lowlands keep detailed records of thaw dates, lake levels, and the movement of ice, because a reliable account of one spring may save a distant settlement from misjudging the next.

## Tundra and Forest Life

The tundra is covered by sedges, blue grass, dwarf willow, stone heather, and several mats of moss that remain green beneath the snow. The plants grow close to the ground, where they are protected from the wind and can use the heat held by dark soil and stone. During the brief summer, the hollows fill with white flowers, yellow poppies, and purple bell plants. Their blooms attract clouds of insects, which in turn support fish, birds, and small mammals. The tundra appears empty from a distant ridge, but its shallow ponds and grass mats contain a dense seasonal life.

The principal grazers are the broad-horned tundra deer and the shaggy white ox, both of which move between the northern plains and the southern forest edge. Their migration follows the retreat of snow and the emergence of edible plants. Wolves, pale foxes, and the great northern bear follow the herds, while the smaller snow hare and burrowing vole remain active beneath the snow. The coast supports seals, walruses, and cold-water whales, and [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) settlements are found near the channels that remain open through winter.

The southern forest has its own hierarchy of life. Elk browse upon young trees, beavers dam the slower streams, and a dark lynx hunts through the undergrowth. The [ursines](/content/species/sapient/ursines) of the forest margins gather nuts, fish, and roots, and their seasonal movements often coincide with the human villages that trade for hides and medicines. Birds of prey use the open river valleys, while owls and pine martens occupy the darker woodland. The health of the forest is judged by the number of beaver ponds and the return of the great cranes, which nest only where the wetlands remain undisturbed.

## Peoples and Settlement

Human settlements are concentrated along the southern rivers and the sheltered coast. Villages use turf, stone, and timber according to what the nearby land provides, and nearly every house has a smoke room or winter chamber below the main floor. The southern ports trade furs, fish, timber, salt, amber, and iron ore for grain and cloth from warmer lands. Their ships are broad and low, designed for coastal waters where ice and fog are more dangerous than open waves.

The tundra is travelled chiefly by [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines), humans, and ursines. The travelling clans maintain routes between summer grazing grounds, winter shelter, and freshwater springs that remain open beneath snow. Their camps are built from hide, woven grass, and portable frames, and their knowledge is preserved through route songs that name hills, water holes, and dangerous ice. These peoples do not consider the tundra empty or unclaimed. They understand it as a country of shared obligations, where a traveller who conceals a spring risks the survival of every group that follows.

[Dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) inhabit the eastern mountains, where veins of iron, copper, and dark crystal are exposed in the glacial valleys. Their halls are smaller than the great underground cities of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), but their surface roads and winter stores are extensive. Dwarven smiths trade tools and fittings to the human ports, receiving grain, wool, and whale oil in return. They also maintain the stone markers that guide travellers through the highest passes, and these markers are treated as neutral property by every people that uses the roads.

## The Coast and the Cape

The southern sea is cold, shallow near the coast, and rich in fish. Herring, cod, and silver-backed salmon gather where river water meets the ocean, while kelp forests shelter crabs, shellfish, and young seals. Coastal villages set aside breeding bays during the spring, and the prohibition on netting in those waters is enforced by local councils rather than distant rulers. Winter fishing is dangerous, but the summer catch is large enough to sustain inland communities through the longest months.

At the western edge of Nordheim stands the [Storm Cape](/content/location/natural/feature/cape-of-storms), a dark headland that reaches into the meeting of two currents. Its cliffs are nesting grounds for seabirds, and its lighthouse fires are visible from distant routes when the air is clear. The cape creates difficult weather, but it also marks a safe approach to the southern harbours. Pilots measure their course by the colour of the water and the sound of waves striking the outer rocks.

## Significance

Nordheim is significant for its stores of timber, fish, iron, amber, and hardy animals, but its greater importance lies in the knowledge required to live there. The continent demonstrates that cold does not mean lifelessness and that mobility can be as deliberate as settlement. Its roads, camps, refuge halls, and river records form a network adapted to seasonal change rather than opposed to it.

The peoples of Nordheim remain diverse and politically independent, yet they share rules concerning shelter, rescue, and the use of migration routes. Those rules are the foundation of northern commerce and survival. In the records of warmer lands, Nordheim is sometimes reduced to snow and darkness. Its own records describe something more exact: a bright summer, a patient winter, and a country whose abundance appears only to those who understand its timing.
