## Sun-Baked Grassland East of the High Stone

East of the [Stoneheim Highlands](/content/location/settlement/region/stoneheim-highlands) and beyond the reach of the rivers that flow west into the [Vintner's Vale](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/vintners-vale), the land opens into a broad, sun-baked grassland that the dwellers of the inland valleys call the Sunfire Steppe. The steppe takes its name from the harsh, direct light of the summer sun, which is the most immediate impression that a traveller from the wooded valleys receives upon crossing the mountains. The steppe stretches from the eastern foothills of the [Stonespire](/content/location/natural/feature/stonespire) to the distant horizon, broken only by the shallow valleys of the rivers that descend from the high interior and by the salt flats that mark the lowest depressions.

The steppe is the home of a sparse population of pastoralists who follow their flocks across the grasslands in a seasonal pattern that has changed little since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era). The pastoralists live in portable tents of felt and hide and move with the seasons between the higher ground of the foothills in summer and the lower ground of the rivers and salt flats in winter. This pattern of movement ensures that the grazing pressure on any one area is never enough to damage the fragile grassland ecosystem.

## Continental Extremes and the Spring Rains

The steppe has a continental climate far harsher than that of the coastal regions. Summers are long and hot, unbroken by the cooling influence of the sea. Winters are long and bitterly cold, unbroken by the warming influence of the ocean. Rainfall is sparse and falls mostly in spring and early summer, sufficient for the grasses that cover the surface of the steppe but not for the trees that coastal regions support. The temperature range between the hottest summer day and the coldest winter night can exceed fifty degrees, and the wind that sweeps across the open country adds to the severity of both seasons.

Occasional drought occurs when the spring rains fail, causing loss of flocks and requiring migration of households. These droughts have shaped the careful management of flocks that the elders of the pastoralists maintain, including the practice of maintaining reserve herds that are not slaughtered except in times of extreme need. The memory of past droughts is preserved in the route songs that guide pastoralists to the most reliable water sources.

## Tough Grasses and the Burrowing Rodents

The grasslands of the steppe are the foundation of the pastoral economy, supporting the flocks of sheep and goats that the pastoralists tend. The principal grasses are tough species that the spring rains bring up rapidly and that the summer heat dries into a golden brown that persists through winter. The grasses regenerate quickly after grazing, sustaining the seasonal pattern that the pastoralists follow. The root systems of these grasses extend deep into the soil, allowing them to draw moisture from depths that shallow-rooted plants cannot reach.

The grasslands also support a population of smaller creatures that form the ecology of the steppe. Burrowing rodents provide prey for birds of prey catalogued by scholars of the Bestiaries and Herbals. Wild horses that the pastoralists catch and train for riding provide the mobility that their way of life requires. The horses are essential for managing the flocks over the vast distances of the steppe, and a household without horses cannot maintain its place in the seasonal migrations.

## Unreliable Watercourses and the Salt Trade

The rivers that descend from the high interior cross the steppe in shallow valleys that the pastoralists use as their winter grounds. The rivers are small and unreliable, freezing in winter and dwindling in summer. They support a narrow strip of trees and shrubs that pastoralists use for fuel and building, the only source of timber upon the steppe. The rivers are the reason that the pastoralists concentrate their flocks along the valleys in the colder months, seeking shelter from the wind and access to water.

The rivers are also the source of salt that the pastoralists trade with the towns of the inland valleys. Salt is collected from shallow pools that form in the lowest depressions of the steppe and carried by packhorse to the markets of the towns. The salt trade has been the foundation of contact between the pastoralists and the towns, benefiting both parties. The salt from the steppe is distinct in flavour from sea salt and is prized by the inland communities for preserving meat and fish.

## Clan Elders and the Annual Trading Fairs

The pastoralists of the steppe are organised into clans that follow the seasonal pattern of the grasslands, governed by elders of the households in consultation with headmen of the clans. The elders maintain the flocks, pastures, routes, and public order of the clans. They are elected by the households for a term of five years and hold office at the pleasure of the households.

The pastoralists trade with the towns of the inland valleys, exchanging wool, cheese, and salt from the steppe for grain, tools, and cloth that the towns produce. This trade has been conducted in peace for several centuries, forming the foundation of prosperity for both parties. The annual trading fairs at the edge of the steppe are major events in the calendar of the region.

## Glittering Pans and the Wading Birds

The salt flats mark the lowest depressions of the surface and are the source of the salt that the pastoralists trade with the towns. Salt is collected from shallow pools that form in the flats after the spring rains, dried in the sun, and packed into sacks for transport. The salt is of high quality, and the prosperity of the pastoralists who control the principal flats is built upon it. The flats are visible from a great distance, their pale surface glittering in the sun.

The flats are bounded by the surrounding steppe and crossed by the routes that pastoralists follow in their seasonal migrations. They are home to a sparse population of creatures adapted to the salt, including wading birds catalogued by scholars of the Bestiaries and Herbals.

## Least Settled Landscape of the Three Continents

The Sunfire Steppe is one of the least settled landscapes of the three continents, forming the foundation of the pastoral economy that the towns of the inland valleys rely upon for wool, cheese, and salt. The grasslands, rivers, salt flats, pastoralists, and trade routes are the inheritance of the present age. The ceremonies marking the founding are among the most important public observances of the steppe, attended by the elders and principal pastoralists of every clan of the grasslands.
