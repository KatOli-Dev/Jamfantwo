## Pale Sands and Polished Light

Along the southern shore of the principal continent, between the [Sahrani Coastal Strip](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sahrani-coastal-strip) to the east and the mouth of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) to the west, the coast takes on a distinctive character that has been named the Glasslight Coast. The name comes from the long, unbroken beaches of fine sand deposited by offshore currents, which reflect the light of the sun in a manner comparable to polished glass. The coast is bounded on the east by the rocky headland that marks the limit of the Sahrani Strip, on the west by the cliffs that rise toward the strait, on the north by the low hills that mark the limit of the coastal plain, and on the south by the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean).

The coast is sparsely populated, with the population concentrated in a string of small fishing villages along the beaches and a smaller number of farms upon the coastal plain. The villages and farms are connected by a network of unpaved tracks maintained by the magistrates of the coastal towns, suitable for small carts but not for larger vehicles of the trade routes. The settlements are modest in scale, their buildings constructed from the local stone and roofed with tiles that weather to the same pale colour as the sand.

## Warm Maritime Breezes and Gentle Winters

The coast enjoys a warm maritime climate moderated by the steady influence of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean). Summers are hot but rarely oppressive, tempered by the sea breeze that rises in the afternoon. Winters are mild and dry, the season when fishermen can work the beaches in comfort. Rainfall is sparse and falls mostly in winter, sufficient for crops cultivated upon the coastal plain but not for the more demanding crops of inland valleys. The consistent climate allows for a long growing season, and the coastal plain produces reliable harvests year after year.

Occasional storms drive the surf high upon the beaches, damaging boats and gear and requiring extensive labour to repair. These storms have shaped the careful management of boats and gear that the [Inner Sea Watch](/content/government/local/inner-sea-watch) maintains along the coast. The storms are most common in late autumn, and their approach is heralded by a change in the colour of the water and the behaviour of the seabirds.

## Fine Sand, Dunes, and the Sardine Fishery

The beaches extend for several days of travel from east to west without interruption. The sand is fine and pale, the product of slow erosion of offshore rocks by ocean currents carried ashore by the prevailing swell. The sand supports a sparse vegetation of tough grasses and shrubs that farmers have used to stabilise the dunes behind the beaches, providing habitat for smaller creatures catalogued by scholars of the Bestiaries and Herbals. The dunes rise in a series of low ridges behind the high-water mark, and the hollows between them shelter a distinctive community of plants and animals adapted to the dry and shifting conditions.

The beaches are the principal source of fish for the villages. Fishermen work from small boats launched from the beach, using nets to catch small sardine-like species prized in the markets of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar). The fish are dried and salted for transport to inland towns. The fishery has been the foundation of the prosperity of the villages, and the rhythms of the fishing season govern the calendar of the coastal communities.

## Kilns, Ash, and the Pale Glass

The fine sand of the beaches is the raw material of the glass that workshops of the coast have produced since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era). The sand is mixed with ash of coastal shrubs and heated in small kilns that glass-makers tend with care. The resulting glass is pale and clear, establishing the reputation of the coast as a source of fine glassware. The glass-makers are among the most skilled craftsmen of the warm coast, held in high esteem by the magistrates and merchants of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar). Their products range from simple drinking vessels to intricate decorative pieces that are exported to markets across the known world.

The glass-makers are organised into workshops governed by master glass-makers in consultation with the magistrates of the nearest town. The masters maintain the kilns, workshops, standards of glass, and public order of the workshops. They are elected by the journeymen for a term of five years and hold office at the pleasure of the journeymen. Apprentices serve for seven years before being admitted to the journeymen, during which they learn the techniques of mixing, blowing, and annealing that distinguish the glass of the coast from that produced elsewhere. The colours of the glass can be varied by adding minerals from the inland hills, and the workshops experiment constantly with new formulations.

## Grain Fields and the Irrigation Channels

The farms of the coastal plain produce grain and vegetables for the coastal towns, cultivated since the founding of the villages. The principal crops are small grains that the dry climate supports, forming the staple of the diet of villages and towns. The farms are worked by farming households that have cultivated the plain for several generations. The fields are arranged in long strips running perpendicular to the coast, allowing each household a share of both the better-drained higher ground and the moister lower ground near the dunes.

The farms are governed by heads of households in consultation with the magistrates of the nearest town. The heads maintain the fields, irrigation works, tracks, and public order of the farms. They are elected by the households for a term of three years and hold office at the pleasure of the households. The irrigation system draws water from shallow wells sunk into the coastal plain, and the water is distributed through channels lined with stone to prevent seepage. The system requires constant maintenance, and each farming household contributes labour to the cleaning and repair of the channels in proportion to the size of its holding.

## Fishermen, Glass-Makers, and Farmers

The villages of the Glasslight Coast are small communities of fishermen, glass-makers, and farmers, each occupying a distinct quarter arranged around a central market square. The houses are built from the same pale stone that lines the irrigation channels, with thick walls that keep the interiors cool during the heat of the day. Each village has a communal kiln for the glass-makers, a smokehouse for the fishermen, and a well that provides water for the whole community. The villages are governed by councils of elders drawn from the three trades, who meet weekly to settle disputes and coordinate the maintenance of the tracks and the beach.

## Fishing, Glass, and the Heritage of the Coast

The Glasslight Coast is one of the most distinctive landscapes of the warm coast, forming the foundation of the fishing and glass-making economies of the southern shore. Its beaches, workshops, farms, villages, and tracks are the inheritance of the present age. The annual ceremonies marking the founding are among the most important public observances of the warm coast, attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every coastal town.
