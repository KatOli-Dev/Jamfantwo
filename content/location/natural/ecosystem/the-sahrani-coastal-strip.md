## Overview

The Sahrani Coastal Strip is the narrow band of fertile land that lies between the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the great desert of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) in northern [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), a region of warm climate, abundant water, and rich soils that has supported human civilisation since the earliest records were kept. The strip extends along the entire northern coast of the continent, varying in width from as little as a few miles in the steeper sections of the shoreline to as much as fifty miles where the river valleys cut deep into the interior, and it is the most densely populated region of Sahrana by a considerable margin. The coastal cities that line this strip, including [Qassir](/content/location/settlement/city/qassir), [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar), and [Taretha](/content/location/settlement/city/taretha), are among the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the known world, and their wealth and their culture are the products of the extraordinary fertility of the coastal lands and the trade that flows through their harbours.

## Climate

The climate of the Sahrani Coastal Strip is warm and humid throughout the year, moderated by the waters of the Auric Ocean that wash the northern coast with a steady current that flows from the east. The temperature varies little between the seasons, remaining warm enough for cultivation in every month, and the difference between the coolest and the warmest times of the year is a matter of a few degrees rather than the dramatic shifts that characterise the climates of the northern continents. The humidity is high, and the air carries the scent of the sea and of the lush vegetation that covers the land from the shoreline to the edge of the desert.

The rainfall of the coastal strip is abundant and reliable, falling in two distinct seasons that the inhabitants call the great rains and the little rains. The great rains arrive in the winter months, when the monsoon winds from the Auric Ocean bring sheets of rain that may continue for days at a time, filling the rivers and flooding the low-lying fields with water that is essential for the cultivation of rice and other moisture-loving crops. The little rains arrive in the summer, a briefer and less intense period of precipitation that sustains the second growing season and prevents the land from drying out under the summer sun. Between the two rainy seasons, the weather is warm and clear, and the sun shines from a sky of deep and unbroken blue.

## Flora of the Coastal Strip

The natural vegetation of the Sahrani Coastal Strip is a dense and layered woodland that has largely been cleared for agriculture over the centuries of human settlement but still persists in the areas that are too steep or too wet for cultivation. The coastal forest is composed of trees that are adapted to the warm and humid conditions, including the broad-leaved sailwood, whose leaves are large and waxy and whose trunk rises straight and tall, and the iron-red mahogany, whose dense and durable timber is among the most valued in the known world. The canopy of the coastal forest is high and continuous, shading the forest floor so thoroughly that the undergrowth is thin and composed chiefly of shade-tolerant ferns and saplings waiting for the fall of a canopy tree to release them into the light.

The river valleys that cut through the coastal strip are lined with gallery forests of a different character, dominated by the water-loving silkwood and the graceful hanging willow, whose branches droop to the water and provide shade for the fish and the amphibians that inhabit the streams. The floodplains of the rivers are carpeted with a dense growth of grasses and sedges during the dry season, and they are transformed into shallow lakes during the rains, when the waters rise and cover the low ground for weeks at a time. The transition between the river valley and the higher ground is marked by a belt of fan-palm woodland, the fronds of the palm visible from a great distance as they rise above the surrounding vegetation, and the fruit of the fan-palm is a staple food for the peoples of the coastal strip.

The cultivated plants of the Sahrani Coastal Strip are among the most diverse of any region in the known world, for the warm and reliable climate permits the cultivation of crops from every continent. Rice is the staple grain, grown in flooded terraces on the lower slopes and the valley floors, and its harvest provides the foundation of the diet for the majority of the population. Cotton is grown for the production of the fine cloth that is one of the principal exports of the coastal cities, and the cotton fields are a familiar sight across the coastal plain, their white bolls gleaming in the sun at the harvest time. Spices are grown in the gardens and the smallholdings that surround every settlement, and the saffron, the cardamom, and the black pepper of Sahrana are prized in the markets of every continent.

## Fauna of the Coastal Strip

The animal life of the Sahrani Coastal Strip is as rich and as varied as its plant life, reflecting the diversity of habitats that exist within the narrow band between the sea and the desert. The coastal forest is home to a population of primates, including the golden langur, whose long tail and golden-brown coat make it one of the most conspicuous animals of the canopy, and the small but vocal tree monkey, whose calls echo through the forest at dawn and dusk. The larger mammals of the forest include the spotted forest cat, a solitary predator that hunts in the trees and on the ground, and the Sahrani bear, a small and timid omnivore that is seldom seen but whose tracks are a common discovery in the deeper parts of the woodland.

The river systems of the coastal strip support a population of aquatic and semi-aquatic animals that are central to the ecology of the region. The river crocodile, a large and patient predator that reaches lengths of more than twenty feet, is the dominant aquatic predator of the slow-moving rivers, and its presence is a constant consideration for the peoples who live and work upon the waterways. The crocodile shares the rivers with the smooth-skinned river dolphin, a graceful and intelligent animal that travels in small pods and is regarded by the coastal peoples as a sign of good fortune, and with a host of fish species that include the silver barb, the spiny eel, and the great river catfish, the last of which can grow to a size that strains the credulity of visitors from the northern continents.

The birds of the coastal strip are abundant and conspicuous, and the most notable among them is the sun crane, a tall wading bird with plumage of pure white and a crest of golden feathers that rises from the crown of its head. The sun crane is sacred to the peoples of the coastal cities, and its image appears in the art and the architecture of every settlement from Qassir to Taretha. The kingfishers of the riverbanks are a flash of colour against the green of the vegetation, and the weaver birds that hang their intricate nests from the branches of the fan-palm are a constant presence in the gardens and the farmsteads of the coastal strip.

## The City-States and Their Ecology

The human population of the Sahrani Coastal Strip is concentrated in the city-states that line the coast, each of which has developed in response to the particular conditions of its location and its history. The cities are built of the local stone, a pale limestone that reflects the heat of the sun and keeps the interiors of the buildings cool, and their streets are narrow and shaded, designed to channel the breezes from the sea through the urban fabric. The gardens of the coastal cities are among the most celebrated features of Sahrani civilisation, and the irrigation systems that sustain them are works of engineering that have been refined over centuries of practice.

The relationship between the cities and the surrounding countryside is one of intimate dependence, for the cities produce little of their own food and rely upon the agricultural production of the coastal strip for their survival. The farms and the plantations that surround the cities are worked by a population of [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) farmers and labourers, and the goods that they produce are brought to the urban markets through a network of canals and roads that connects every part of the coastal strip. The [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) is the most powerful of the coastal polities, its fleet of trading vessels and its network of commercial alliances ensuring its dominance over the trade of the northern coast.

The ecology of the coastal strip has been shaped by human activity over a period of many centuries, and the landscape that exists today is the product of a long and deliberate process of modification. The forests have been cleared and replanted, the rivers have been channelled and diverted, and the soils have been enriched and maintained through the application of compost and the rotation of crops. The result is a landscape that is at once natural and artificial, a cultivated garden of extraordinary productivity that has supported a dense human population for longer than any comparable region in the known world.

## Transition to the Desert

The southern boundary of the Sahrani Coastal Strip is not a sharp line but a gradual transition from the fertile coastal lands to the arid interior of the Kharesh, and the ecology of this transitional zone is distinct from that of either the coast or the desert proper. The vegetation becomes sparser as the rainfall diminishes, and the dense coastal forest gives way to a savanna of scattered trees and drought-resistant shrubs, dotted with the thorns of the acacia and the fleshy leaves of the succulents that store water against the long dry season. The soil becomes sandier and more alkaline, and the cultivation of rice and cotton gives way to the hardier crops of the dryland farmer, including millet, sorghum, and the drought-tolerant date palm.

The settlements of the transitional zone are smaller and more scattered than those of the coast, and they serve as the interface between the coastal economy and the caravan trade of the desert interior. The towns of the transition, including [Dustford](/content/location/settlement/town/dustford) and the villages that lie along the caravan routes, are the places where the goods of the coast are exchanged for the spices, the minerals, and the precious stones of the Kharesh, and their markets are a meeting point of two worlds. The peoples of the transition include the settled farmers of the coast and the nomadic [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) of the desert, and their interactions are governed by agreements of mutual access and shared use of the water sources that sustain both ways of life.

## Significance

The Sahrani Coastal Strip is the most densely populated and the most productive region of the entire continent, and its cities and its farms have sustained a civilisation that is among the oldest and the most cultured in the known world. The trade that flows through the coastal ports connects Sahrana to the markets of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), and the spices, the cloth, and the crafted goods of the coastal cities are valued throughout the three continents. The coastal strip is also a zone of ecological transition, where the fertile and the arid meet and where the peoples of the two have learned to coexist, and its importance to the world is matched only by the precariousness of its position between the sea and the desert.
