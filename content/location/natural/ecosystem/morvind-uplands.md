## Overview

The [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) Uplands form the mountainous interior of the continent of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), a region of high peaks, deep valleys, and extensive plateaus that occupies the greater part of the landmass and defines its character. The uplands rise from the narrow coastal lowlands in a series of increasingly steep steps, culminating in the central massif whose highest peaks are covered in permanent snow and ice. The region is marked by the presence of the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves), whose underground halls and mountain fortresses have been carved into the living rock over centuries of patient labour, and by the hardy communities of [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) and [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) who inhabit the more sheltered valleys and the lower slopes. The ecology of the Morvind Uplands is shaped by altitude, exposure, and the long winter that dominates the rhythm of life at every elevation.

## Geology and Landforms

The mountains of the Morvind Uplands are among the oldest in the known world, their rocks having been folded and faulted by forces that no living chronicler can date with certainty. The peaks are rounded and massive rather than sharp and jagged, for the action of ice and frost over geological time has worn away the sharper edges and left a landscape of smooth summits and broad ridges. The valleys between the ridges are U-shaped in cross-section, the product of glacial carving that scooped out the rock and left the characteristic forms that are visible throughout the region. The floors of the main valleys are broad and flat, filled with the sediment that has accumulated since the retreat of the glaciers, and they provide the most extensive areas of level ground in the entire upland region.

The rock of the Morvind Uplands is rich in minerals, a fact that has shaped the history and the economy of the entire continent. The veins of iron, copper, tin, and silver that run through the mountains have been worked since the earliest times, and the deeper levels of the dwarven mines have yielded ores of a purity that is seldom found elsewhere. The mountains also contain deposits of coal, of marble, and of the precious stones that are the foundation of the decorative arts of the dwarven people, and the wealth that flows from the earth has made the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) the most prosperous of the dwarven polities.

## Climate of the Uplands

The climate of the Morvind Uplands is cold and continental, with long winters and short summers that impose a strict rhythm upon the life of the region. The winter lasts for the better part of eight months, from the first snows of late autumn to the final thaw of late spring, and the temperatures during this period are among the lowest recorded anywhere in the known world. The cold is dry and still, and the snow that falls accumulates to a depth of many feet in the valleys while the higher slopes are scoured bare by the winds that sweep across the peaks.

The summer in the Morvind Uplands is brief but intense, a period of rapid growth and feverish activity that lasts for no more than three months at the lower elevations and as little as six weeks on the higher slopes. The snow melts with remarkable speed, and the valleys are transformed into a landscape of rushing streams and blooming meadows within a matter of days. The temperatures during the summer are mild, rarely rising above what would be considered comfortable in the lowlands of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the nights remain cold even at the height of the season.

The precipitation of the uplands is moderate, falling as snow in the winter and as rain during the brief summer. The western slopes of the mountains receive more moisture than the eastern, for the prevailing winds from the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) drop their moisture as they rise over the coastal ranges, and the eastern valleys and plateaus are correspondingly drier. The snowpack that accumulates in the high valleys is the source of the rivers that flow down to the coast, and the timing and the volume of the melt are matters of close attention for the communities that depend upon the water.

## Alpine Flora

The plant life of the Morvind Uplands is arranged in distinct altitudinal zones, each with its own characteristic species and adaptations to the conditions of that elevation. The lower slopes, up to the line where the forest gives way to the open mountain, are covered in a dense coniferous forest composed of the Morvind fir, the black spruce, and the mountain pine, the last of which is capable of surviving at higher elevations than any other tree in the known world. The forest is dark and quiet, the canopy dense enough to block much of the light, and the floor is carpeted with moss and needles that muffle the sound of footsteps.

Above the treeline, the vegetation changes abruptly to the open tundra of the alpine zone, where the plants are low-growing and adapted to the extreme conditions. The dwarf willow and the alpine birch spread across the ground in mats that hug the surface, their stems buried in the moss and their leaves exposed to the sun for the brief growing season. The flowering plants of the alpine zone are among the most remarkable in the known world, for they must complete their entire life cycle within the span of a few weeks. The mountain saxifrage and the alpine buttercup appear as soon as the snow retreats, their bright colours a sharp contrast to the grey of the rock and the white of the remaining snow, and they set their seed and die back before the return of the winter.

The highest elevations, above the limit of continuous vegetation, support only the hardiest of life forms. Lichens encrust the exposed rock surfaces, their slow growth measuring the passage of years in fractions of an inch, and a few species of moss manage to survive in the sheltered crevices where moisture accumulates. The permanent snow and ice of the highest peaks support no plant life at all, and these barren summits are the domain of the wind and the cold alone.

## Fauna of the Highlands

The animal life of the Morvind Uplands is adapted to the harsh conditions and the seasonal extremes, and the species that inhabit the region are among the hardiest in the known world. The mountain goat is the most conspicuous of the large herbivores, its thick coat and sure-footedness allowing it to navigate the steepest slopes with ease. The goat moves between the elevations with the seasons, grazing the alpine meadows in the summer and descending to the forest edge in the winter, and its population is the primary prey of the large predators of the uplands.

The snow cat, a large feline with a coat of pure white that provides camouflage against the winter snow, is the apex predator of the alpine zone. The snow cat hunts the mountain goat and the smaller mammals of the high country, and its range extends across the full extent of the Morvind Uplands. The cat is solitary and elusive, seldom seen by the human and dwarven inhabitants of the region, and its presence is known chiefly through the tracks that it leaves in the snow and the occasional loss of livestock from the valley farms.

The smaller mammals of the uplands include the alpine hare, whose coat turns white in the winter and brown in the summer, and the mountain marmot, a large rodent that hibernates through the winter in burrows that it excavates in the talus slopes. The marmot whistle is one of the characteristic sounds of the alpine summer, and its presence is an indicator of the health of the high-altitude ecosystem. The birds of the uplands include the golden eagle, whose nests are found on the ledges of the highest cliffs, and the ptarmigan, a grouse that changes its plumage with the seasons and provides a source of fresh meat for the inhabitants of the valleys.

## The Dwarven Holds and the Underground Ecosystem

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the Morvind Uplands have created an ecosystem of their own within the mountains, for their underground halls are not merely dwellings but complex environments that support a distinctive community of life. The halls are heated by the geothermal warmth of the earth, and the air that circulates through them is maintained at a temperature that is comfortable for dwarven life regardless of the conditions on the surface. The fungi that grow in the dark passages are cultivated for food and for medicine, and the blind fish of the underground streams provide a source of protein that supplements the diet of grains and preserved meat imported from the lowlands.

The dwarven settlements of the Morvind Uplands are concentrated in the central massif, where the mineral deposits are richest and the geothermal warmth is most accessible. The [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) form the largest of these confederations, their tunnels extending for miles beneath the mountain and their halls capable of housing a population of many thousands. [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) itself is the greatest of the holds and the seat of the federation, while the dependent mining towns such as [Khazgar](/content/location/settlement/city/khazgar) and [Ironhollow](/content/location/settlement/town/ironhollow) sustain the forges and the export of metalwork to the coast.

The relationship between the dwarves and the surface ecology of the uplands is one of careful management, for the dwarves depend upon the surface for timber, for grazing, and for the food that cannot be produced underground. The forests of the lower slopes are managed for timber production, and the alpine meadows are used for the summer grazing of the herds of mountain sheep and goats that provide wool and meat. The dwarven communities of the uplands are thus integrated into the surface ecology in ways that are not always apparent to visitors who see only the stone and the darkness of their halls.

## Human and Elven Communities

The [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) settlements of the Morvind Uplands are located in the sheltered valleys and on the plateaus, where the climate is less severe than in the high mountains and the soil is deep enough for cultivation. The [Valdmark](/content/government/national/valdmark) is the primary agricultural region of the interior, a broad valley that runs along the eastern side of the central massif and benefits from the rain shadow of the western ranges. The farms of the Valdmark produce the grain and the wool that sustain the population of the region, and the market towns that line the valley are the hubs of trade between the agricultural lowlands and the dwarven highlands.

The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the Morvind Uplands are found in the wooded valleys of the lower slopes, where the coniferous forest provides the shelter and the resources that their way of life requires. The elven communities of Morvind are smaller and more isolated than those of Aethelar, and they have adapted to the colder conditions by developing a stockier build and a greater tolerance for low temperatures. The elves of the uplands maintain the same traditions of forest stewardship that characterise their kin elsewhere, but their management of the mountain forest is necessarily less intensive than the cultivation of the temperate woodlands of the south.

## Significance

The Morvind Uplands are the heart of the continent, the source of its mineral wealth and the foundation of its economy and its culture. The mountains are the home of the dwarven people in their greatest concentration, and the halls of the Morvind uplands are the most extensive and the most ancient of all dwarven settlements. The region is also a refuge for hardy species of plants and animals that are found nowhere else in the known world, and its ecology represents one of the most complete examples of a cold-altitude ecosystem in the three continents. The balance between the underground and the surface, between the dwarven and the human and the elven, and between the demands of extraction and the need for preservation, is the central challenge of life in the Morvind Uplands.
