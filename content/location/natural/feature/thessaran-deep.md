## The Trench off the Southern Shelf

Beneath the southern shelf of [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara), where the warm sea drops away from the coastal shallows, lies the Thessaran Deep, a long and narrow trench whose depth alters the currents of the warm water and shapes the life of the southern coast. The trench runs parallel to the southern coast for several days of sailing, and its depth is great enough that the surface waters of the inner shelf are measurably cooler than the waters that the currents carry from the open ocean. The chronicler of the maritime pilots has set down that the Deep is the principal reason for the productivity of the southern fisheries, and that the navigators of the coast have marked the edge of the trench with floating stone buoys since the first generation of the harbour pilots who watched the colour of the water before setting out.

The name of the trench is older than the present coastal kingdoms, and the pilots of the southern ports use a word that the ancient tablets of the highland passes preserve. The word is not a precise translation, for the pilots of the present age apply it to the entire length of the trench, while the older tablets apply it to a particular reach that the ancient pilots considered the most dangerous. The chronicler notes that the uncertainty has not reduced the authority of the name, and the navigators of every southern port employ the word when they speak of the deep water that the trench opens beneath their keels.

## The Currents and the Cold Upwelling

The Thessaran Deep alters the currents of the warm sea in a manner that the chronicler of the maritime cartographers has described as the most useful of the natural engines of the southern coast. The trench lies close enough to the southern shelf that the prevailing currents, which carry warm water northward along the coast, are deflected by the edge of the deep and turned upward toward the surface. The deflection brings cold water from the lower reaches of the trench to the upper layers of the sea, and the cold water meets the warm currents at the edge of the shelf in a band of turbulence that the pilots of the coast have learned to recognise by the colour of the water and the behaviour of the seabirds.

The cold water that rises from the trench carries with it the mineral nutrients that the deeper layers of the sea have gathered from the sinking of organic matter over many seasons. The nutrients feed the small creatures of the sea, and the small creatures in turn support the fish that the southern fisheries pursue. The chronicler of the southern fisheries has set down that the productivity of the southern coast is several times greater than that of the central coast, where the trench lies further from the shelf and the cold water does not rise in sufficient quantity to enrich the upper waters. The pilots and the fishers of the southern ports are agreed that the deep is the foundation of their prosperity, and the deep is treated with the care that the most important of the natural features of the coast deserves.

## The Migrating Fish and the Shellfish Beds

The fish that the southern coast pursues are the migratory species that follow the band of cold water northward in the warm season and retreat southward when the coastal waters cool in the autumn. The principal fish are the great shoals of sardine and anchovy that move along the coast in a seasonal procession, the tuna that follow the shoals, and the larger predatory fish that the offshore boats pursue in the deeper water beyond the reach of the coastal nets. The chronicler of the southern fisheries has recorded that the fish are most numerous in the months when the cold upwelling is at its strongest, and that the fishers of the southern ports set their calendars by the appearance of the first shoals at the edge of the shelf.

The shellfish beds of the southern coast are no less productive than the fisheries of the open water. The cold water of the upwelling supports the growth of the mussels, the oysters, and the smaller shellfish that the coastal communities gather from the rocky shallows and the edges of the channels. The beds are extensive, and the gathering of the shellfish is a communal activity that the customary law of the coastal villages regulates with care. The chronicler of the southern records has set down that the shellfish beds are among the most reliable of the food sources of the southern coast, and that the harvest of the shellfish has sustained the coastal communities through seasons when the open-sea fish have been scarce.

## The Sudden Weather and the Pilots

The cold upwelling that the Thessaran Deep produces is also the cause of the sudden weather changes that the pilots of the southern coast have learned to expect. When the cold water rises to the surface and meets the warm air above, the contrast in temperature produces a band of fog and squall that can descend upon a vessel within minutes, even on a day that began with a clear sky. The pilots of the southern coast have developed a body of knowledge concerning the conditions that produce the sudden weather, and the knowledge is preserved in the harbour agreements of the principal ports and in the customary teaching of the pilot families.

The chronicler of the maritime records has set down that the sudden weather of the southern coast is the most dangerous feature of the warm sea, and that more vessels have been lost to the sudden fog and the sudden squall than to the great storms of the open water. The pilots watch the colour of the water, the behaviour of the seabirds, and the appearance of the haze upon the horizon, and the pilots signal the state of the weather to the vessels in the roadstead by means of the flags and the lanterns that the harbour authorities maintain. The chronicler notes that the pilots who have mastered the reading of the weather are regarded with a respect that is matched by no other accomplishment of the maritime life, and that the families of the pilots are among the most respected of the southern communities.

## The Floating Stone Buoys

The edge of the Thessaran Deep is marked by a line of floating stone buoys that the navigators of the southern coast have maintained since the first generations of the harbour pilots. The buoys are not the wooden casks or the iron-bound barrels of the open-sea routes; they are blocks of the dark volcanic stone of the southern hills, fitted with rings of rope and chained to the seabed by lengths of cable that the smiths of the coastal towns have forged with care. The buoys float because the stone is of a kind that holds a bubble of air within its pores, and the bubble gives the block a buoyancy that the chronicler of the maritime engineers has recorded as a curiosity of the southern quarries.

The buoys are placed at intervals along the edge of the trench, and the buoys mark the boundary between the safe water of the inner shelf and the deeper water of the trench. The pilots navigate by the buoys when the fog descends, and the pilots use the buoys to fix the position of the vessel in the absence of other marks. The chronicler of the maritime records has set down that the maintenance of the buoys is a communal obligation that the coastal towns share, and that the buoys are inspected and replaced at the turning of every season. The chronicler notes that the buoys are among the most distinctive of the navigation aids of the warm sea, and that the system of buoys has served the southern coast for several generations without significant interruption.

## The Merfolk and the Trench

The [merfolk](/content/species/sapient/merfolk) of the southern coast know the Thessaran Deep more intimately than any surface sailor, and the merfolk communities have their own understanding of the currents and the cold upwelling that the trench produces. The merfolk inhabit the waters of the trench as well as the shallows of the inner shelf, and the merfolk navigate the deep water by means of senses that the surface pilots do not possess. The chronicler of the maritime records has recorded that the merfolk have warned the surface vessels of the approach of the sudden weather upon several occasions, and the warnings have been credited with the saving of lives in conditions that the pilots themselves could not have foreseen.

The relationship between the merfolk and the human fishers of the southern coast is governed by a body of customary law that the harbour agreements incorporate. The merfolk have their own breeding grounds within the trench, and the human fishers refrain from setting their nets in the waters above the breeding grounds during the season of the breeding. The merfolk in turn refrain from disturbing the shellfish beds that the human communities depend upon, and the two communities share the knowledge of the movements of the fish and the behaviour of the seabirds. The chronicler notes that this arrangement is among the most successful of the joint management systems of the warm sea, and that the merfolk and the human fishers have maintained a quiet cooperation through several generations.

## The Harbours and the Agreements

The principal harbours of the southern coast stand at the mouths of the rivers that descend from the central valleys of [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara), and the harbours are the gathering points for the fishers and the merchants of the coast. The harbour agreements of the southern ports incorporate the rules of the navigation, the seasons of the fishing, the management of the shellfish beds, and the conduct of the merfolk and the human communities in their shared waters. The chronicler of the maritime records has set down that the harbour agreements are among the oldest of the public instruments of the southern coast, and that the agreements have been revised from time to time but never replaced.

The harbours maintain the buoys that mark the edge of the trench, and the harbours maintain the pilots who navigate the coastal waters. The pilots are licensed by the harbour authorities, and the pilots are required to demonstrate their knowledge of the currents, the weather, and the buoys before they are entrusted with the conduct of a vessel. The chronicler notes that the pilots are held in the highest esteem by the merchants and the fishers of the southern coast, and that the pilots are among the most respected of the public servants of the maritime communities.

## Significance

The Thessaran Deep is one of the most distinctive of the natural features of the warm sea, and the trench is the foundation of the fisheries, the navigation, and the customary law of the southern coast of [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara). The currents, the cold upwelling, the migrating fish, the shellfish beds, the sudden weather, the floating stone buoys, and the merfolk communities are the inheritance that the deep has bequeathed to the present age. The chronicler notes that the deep is held in particular esteem by the pilots and the fishers who make their living upon the warm sea, and the pilots and the fishers mark the turning of the seasons with appropriate ceremonies. The Thessaran Deep endures, in the cold water that rises from the trench and in the colour of the water that the pilots read before they set out, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the southern coast of the long continent of the eastern waters.
