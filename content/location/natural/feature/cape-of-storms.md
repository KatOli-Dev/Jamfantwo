## Overview

At the western edge of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), where the long curve of the southern coast gives way to the open waters of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), the Cape of Storms reaches into the meeting of two currents and gives the western seas their most familiar name. The dark headland is the westernmost reach of the continent, and its cliffs are the first landfall of the weather that descends from the polar seas and rolls in from the warmer waters to the south. Foreign pilots, sailing north from the established routes, have applied the cape's name to the whole land that lies beyond it, and the continent of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) is, in the reckoning of southern navigators, the country that the Cape of Storms announces. The chronicler of the cold northern waters sets down that the name belongs first to the headland and only by extension to the continent, though the two are now so closely linked in the records of the maritime academies that the distinction is often forgotten.

## The Headland and the Cliffs

The Cape of Storms is a dark and angular headland of old grey stone, rising several hundred feet above the sea, and its flanks are scored by the weather of centuries. The cliffs that fall to the water are sheer and broken, and the ledges of the lower rocks are the nesting grounds of the seabirds that breed upon the cape in the spring. The chronicler of the [Cataloguing of Flora and Fauna](/content/belief/science/physical/cataloguing-of-flora-and-fauna) has recorded the gulls, the cormorants, the guillemots, and the small grey auk that make their homes upon the ledges, and the colonies are among the largest on the southern coast of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The birds are the most constant inhabitants of the cape, and their cries, when the wind is from the west, are audible at a considerable distance out to sea.

The headland is bounded on three sides by water, and the two currents that meet at the cape are the source of both the difficult weather and the navigational value of the landfall. From the north comes a cold current that follows the outer shelf of the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean), and from the south a warmer current that carries the influence of the temperate seas along the continental coast. The two streams meet at the cape and produce eddies, tide-rips, and a distinctive colouration of the water that the pilots have learned to read as the mark of the land. The lighthouse that stands upon the highest point of the headland is visible from distant routes when the air is clear, and its fires are the first indication of land for vessels approaching from the west.

## Weather and the Western Approach

The cape creates difficult weather, and the chronicler of the cold northern records sets down that the storms that descend upon the western approach are the most sudden and the most violent of any on the southern coast of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The cold current from the north and the warmer water from the south combine to produce fogs that may persist for days, and the wind that funnels between the two streams can shift without warning from a gentle breeze to a gale. The pilots who navigate the western approach treat the cape with a respect that is not given to any other headland on the coast, and the records of the [Surveying of Heights and Distances](/content/belief/science/physical/surveying-of-heights-and-distances) contain careful measurements of the wind, the current, and the visibility at the cape through every season of the year.

The difficult weather is balanced by the navigational value of the landfall, and the cape is the most reliable mark for vessels approaching the southern harbours of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) from the west. The lighthouse fires are visible at a distance of many miles in clear weather, and the colour of the water, the sound of the waves upon the outer rocks, and the flight of the seabirds are all signs that the pilots read as they set their course for the coast. The chronicler notes that a vessel which has sighted the cape is never more than a day's sail from a sheltered harbour, and that the headland is the foundation of the safety of the navigation of the western approach.

## The Pilots and the Routes

The pilots of the western coast are a small and a careful body of mariners, drawn from the families that have navigated the approaches to [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) for generations, and the cape is the foundation of their craft. The pilots read the colour of the water, which shifts from the deep blue of the open ocean to a greenish hue over the shallows and a pale, milky colour where the two currents mingle at the cape. They read the sound of the waves upon the outer rocks, for the swell that breaks upon the ledges of the cape produces a deep and regular booming that can be heard at a considerable distance when the wind is right. They read the flight of the seabirds, for the gulls and the auks that nest upon the cliffs are seldom found far from land, and a pilot who sees a flock ahead may reckon that the cape is near.

The pilots also maintain the records of the cape, and the measurements that they make are the foundation of the charts of the western approach. The barometer readings, the wind directions, the visibility, and the times of the tides are entered in the logs that the pilots carry, and the logs are the most reliable source of information about the cape that the academies possess. The chronicler records that the pilots of the cape are held in particular esteem by the merchants of the coast, and that no vessel of value approaches the western harbours without a pilot who knows the headland and its ways.

## The Name and Its Wider Use

The name Stormfall was first given to the cape by the foreign pilots who approached [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) from the south, and the oldest coastal traditions of the southern seas say that storms fall from the mountains of the distant land as though poured from a bowl, descending through the valleys and striking the sea with little warning. The foreign pilots used the name Stormfall at first for the cape alone, and the word was applied to the headland before it was applied to the continent. As the voyages of the foreign mariners extended along the coast, the name came to be used for the whole of the land that the cape announced, and the continent of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) is, in the reckoning of the foreign pilots, the country of which the Cape of Storms is the most familiar mark.

The continental name has been carried back to the southern seas, and the pilots of the warm coast and the academies of the southern harbours now use the name Stormfall for the entire continent. The chronicler of the cold northern records observes that the Stormfall peoples themselves distinguish the seasons and the regions of their land with greater precision, and that the inland chronicles refer to particular valleys, particular ranges, and particular coasts rather than to any single name for the whole. The Cape of Storms remains, in the reckoning of all the pilots who approach it, the most distinctive of the headlands of the western seas, and the name that the headland bestowed upon the continent is, in the judgment of the chronicler, a tribute to the power of the weather that gathers at the meeting of the two currents.

## Significance

The Cape of Storms is the westernmost reach of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) and the source of the continental name that the foreign pilots have applied to the whole of the land beyond. The headland, the lighthouse, the cliffs, and the seabirds are the inheritance that the cape has bequeathed to the present age, and the pilots and the merchants of the western coast hold the cape in particular memory. The chronicler records that the cape is the foundation of the safety of the navigation of the western approach, and that the difficult weather and the navigational value of the headland are, in the reckoning of those who know the sea, the two faces of a single truth about the meeting of the two currents at the western edge of the continent.
