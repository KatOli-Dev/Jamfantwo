## Overview

The Whitewall Range is the long mountain chain that runs east to west across the centre of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), dividing the continent's northern tundra from its southern forests and serving as the watershed from which the rivers of both slopes descend to the lowlands. The range is lower in the west and broader in the east, where the glaciers descend through the longer valleys and terminate in lakes of blue water that mark the slow retreat of the ice. The northern face is steep and wind-scoured, while the southern face is more sheltered, with deeper forests, mineral springs, and a series of valleys that remain open to agriculture during the brief warm season. The chronicler of the northern cartographers has set down that the Whitewall is to [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) what the [Crown Range](/content/location/natural/feature/crown-range) is to [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara) - a spine and a watershed, a barrier and a corridor - though the Whitewall is colder, narrower, and more subject to the long winters that govern every activity of the continent.

The range takes its name from the pale stone of its upper slopes, which the chronicler of the mountain patrols has described as white as the belly of a winter fish when the sun falls upon it at the right hour. The name is used by every people of the continent, from the tundra travellers to the southern foresters, and the mountain councils of the interior have accepted it as the formal designation of the range. The range must not be confused with the [Frostwall](/content/location/settlement/region/frostwall-marches) of the deep interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), a separate and quite different escarpment of the far continent, for the two ranges are distinguished by climate, by geology, and by the peoples who inhabit their slopes.

## The Western Reaches

The western reaches of the Whitewall Range are the lowest section of the range, and the mountains rise in a series of low hills and broken ridges rather than the high crests that mark the centre and the east. The passes in the west are open for a longer season than those of the higher country, and the roads that descend to the western coast are among the most reliable in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The chronicler of the coastal pilots has set down that the western reaches of the range are the first to be cleared of snow in the spring and the last to be closed in the autumn, and that the trade between the southern forests and the western ports is conducted for a longer season through the western passes than through any other crossing of the range.

The western reaches are also the most forested, for the lower elevation and the milder air allow the trees to grow more densely than they do in the higher country. Fir, black pine, and silver birch dominate the lower slopes, and the valleys that descend to the western coast carry open clearings that are the domain of the herders of the western communities. The chronicler notes that the western reaches are the home of no permanent mining community, for the ore-bearing rock of the range lies at greater depth in the west than in the east, and the small communities that dwell among the western hills rely upon the herding, the forest, and the trade with the coastal ports for their livelihood.

The rivers that descend from the western reaches are short and swift, for the distance from the ridge to the coast is measured in a few miles, and the rivers reach the sea before they have gathered any great volume of water. The rivers are not navigable, but they are dependable, and the small communities of the western valleys draw upon them for the water that their cattle and their household gardens require. The chronicler records that the western rivers freeze early in the autumn and thaw late in the spring, and that the timing of the freeze and the thaw is one of the principal determinants of the rhythm of the western year.

## The Central Reaches

The central reaches of the Whitewall Range are higher than the western, and the passes that cross the central section are open for a shorter season than those of the western hills. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has recorded that the central section of the range is the most difficult to traverse in winter, for the snow lies deep in the passes and the winds that descend from the high crests can reach a force that no laden animal can withstand. The high valleys of the central section are the home of the small herding communities that move their flocks between the summer pasture on the open grass and the winter shelter of the sheltered valleys, and the chronicler notes that the customary law of the highland has refined the timing of these movements into a discipline that the foreign visitor seldom masters.

The central section is also the most heavily travelled in the warm season, for the central passes are the shortest route between the southern forests and the northern tundra, and the caravans that carry the goods of the lowlands to the interior communities use the central passes in preference to the longer routes through the western hills or the eastern glaciers. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has set down that the central passes are marked by refuge halls stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber, and that the halls are maintained by the highland wardens under the oversight of the customary councils. The central passes are the busiest of the crossings, and the chronicler records that the maintenance of the halls is a communal obligation that is shared by the highland and the lowland communities alike.

The forests of the central section are thinner than those of the western reaches, and the higher slopes are covered by open grass and low scrub. The trees that grow upon the central section are the hardiest of the continent, capable of withstanding the long winters and the brief summers that characterise the highland, and the chronicler notes that the timber of the central forests is the most valued in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) for its density and its straight grain. The mineral springs of the central section are also among the most valued in the known world, for the water that rises from the deep rock is rich in iron and in the salts that the healers of the southern communities use in their remedies.

## The Eastern Reaches and the Glaciers

The eastern reaches of the Whitewall Range are broader and higher than the central or the western, and the glaciers that descend from the eastern crests are the most extensive in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The glaciers move slowly down the longer valleys, and they terminate in lakes of blue water that mark the point at which the ice melts as quickly as it advances. The chronicler of the mountain cartographers has recorded that the eastern lakes are the deepest in the continent, and that the water that they contain is the purest that the naturalists of the southern academies have been able to examine. The lakes are the source of the eastern rivers, which descend to the southern forests and provide the water that the southern farmlands require.

The eastern reaches are the most heavily mineralised section of the range, and the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the eastern mountains have worked the veins of iron, copper, and dark crystal that are exposed in the glacial valleys. The dwarven halls of the eastern reaches are smaller than the great underground cities of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind), but the surface roads and the winter stores are extensive, and the dwarven smiths trade tools and fittings to the human ports in return for the grain, the wool, and the whale oil that the high country cannot produce. The chronicler notes that the dwarves also maintain the stone markers that guide travellers through the highest passes, and that the markers are treated as neutral property by every people that uses the roads.

The eastern reaches are the most exposed to the winter weather, and the passes of the eastern section are open for the shortest season of any in the range. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has set down that the eastern passes may be closed for more than half the year, and that the communities of the eastern valleys maintain substantial winter stores against the long isolation. The cold of the eastern reaches is intense, and the winds that descend from the glaciers are the most bitter in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), but the mineral wealth and the timber of the eastern valleys have sustained a settled population that the chronicler has described as the most self-reliant of any in the continent.

## The Southern Slope

The southern face of the Whitewall Range is more sheltered than the northern, and the forests that clothe the lower slopes are denser and more varied than those of any other section. Fir, black pine, silver birch, and red cedar dominate the lowlands, and the clearings formed by fire, windfall, and the grazing of elk support a wealth of berry shrubs, mushrooms, and the hardy vegetables that the southern communities cultivate in their kitchen gardens. The mineral springs that rise from the southern face are among the most valued in the continent, and the valleys that open to the south are the home of the agricultural settlements that produce the grain and the vegetables that the highland cannot grow.

The southern valleys are the most densely settled lands of the range, and the villages of the southern slope are arranged along the rivers that descend from the central section. The architecture of the southern villages reflects the climate, with houses of timber and stone, steep roofs weighted with stone to resist the gales, and broad chimneys that carry the smoke of the peat fires through the long winters. The chronicler of the southern agronomists has recorded that the harvest of the southern valleys is the most reliable in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), and that the abundance has supported a settled population of considerable size since the earliest records of the southern communities.

The southern forests are the home of the wildlife of the continent, and the chronicler of the southern naturalists has set down that the health of the forest is judged by the number of beaver ponds and the return of the great cranes, which nest only where the wetlands remain undisturbed. Elk browse upon the young trees, beavers dam the slower streams, and a dark lynx hunts through the undergrowth. The [ursines](/content/species/sapient/ursines) of the forest margins gather nuts, fish, and roots, and their seasonal movements often coincide with the human villages that trade for hides and medicines. The chronicler notes that the southern forests are a refuge for the traveller and a source of timber for the builders, and that the customary law that governs the forest is among the most carefully observed of any in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim).

## The Northern Face

The northern face of the Whitewall Range is steep and wind-scoured, and the forests that clothe the southern slopes are absent from the higher and colder northern face. The bare rock and the snowfields of the northern crests descend in cliffs and broken scree to the edge of the Frostwall Tundra, and the chronicler of the northern cartographers has set down that the northern face is the most exposed and the most difficult of any in [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim). The winds that sweep across the tundra strike the northern face with a force that no shelter can resist, and the winter cold is so intense that the rock of the higher crests splits with a sound that the tundra travellers have likened to the report of a great bell.

The northern face is crossed by no laden caravans, and the only travellers who venture upon the higher slopes are the tundra peoples who follow the seasonal routes between the summer grazing grounds and the winter shelter. The [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines) of the tundra range across the plains that lie beyond the range, and the chronicler notes that the lupine knowledge of the northern face is the most complete in the continent, for the lupine communities have crossed the range in every season and in every kind of weather. The lupine routes are marked by cairns and by the customary signs that the tundra peoples maintain, and the markers are respected by every people that uses the range.

The rivers that descend from the northern face are short and steep, and they reach the tundra before they have gathered any great volume of water. The rivers freeze early in the autumn, and the ice remains until late in the spring, and the tundra communities draw upon the rivers for water through the long winter by cutting holes in the ice and by the patient labour of breaking the frozen surface. The chronicler records that the water of the northern rivers is the purest in the continent, and that the tundra communities have a deep respect for the rivers that sustain them through the long cold.

## The Passes and the Refuge Halls

The passes of the Whitewall Range are the openings that allow the road across the continent to follow its course, and the chronicler of the mountain patrols has set down that only three of the passes are used by laden caravans. The three caravan passes are spaced at intervals along the range, each marked by a refuge hall stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber for the use of travellers who are caught by the weather. The halls are maintained by the highland wardens under the oversight of the customary councils, and the maintenance is a communal obligation that is shared by the highland and the lowland communities alike. The chronicler records that the loss of a refuge hall is a disaster for the communities on both sides of the range, and that the maintenance of the halls is a duty that is not neglected in any season.

The central pass is the most heavily travelled, and its refuge hall is the largest in the range. The hall is built of stone and timber, with a fire pit at its centre and sleeping platforms along the walls, and the stores that it contains are renewed each autumn by the highland wardens before the first lasting snow. The eastern pass is the most isolated, for it lies in the shadow of the glaciers, and the wardens who maintain it must travel for many days from the nearest settled valley. The western pass is the most accessible, and its hall is the most frequently visited, for the trade between the southern forests and the western ports is conducted through the western crossing for a longer season than through any other.

The [Frostwall Clans](/content/government/national/frostwall-clans) of the deep interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) maintain a similar system of passes and refuge halls in the escarpment that divides the central uplands from the remote valleys of the far interior, and the chronicler notes that the two systems have much in common, though the Whitewall passes are used by the trade of a settled continent while the Frostwall passes are used chiefly by the patrols of the dwarven clans. The two systems are governed by different customary laws, but the principle of the refuge hall is the same in both ranges, and the chronicler records that the practice of the refuge hall is one of the oldest and most honoured institutions of the mountain peoples of the known world.

## Significance

The Whitewall Range is the spine and the watershed of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim), and its pale crests, its glacier-fed lakes, its mineral springs, and its long winters are the inheritance that the continent has received from the highland. The range is at once a barrier and a corridor, a watershed and a source of water, a treasury of iron and a foundation of the customary law that governs the use of the passes, the refuge halls, and the grazing grounds. The chronicler of the northern cartographers sets down that the prosperity of the continent is bound up with the health of the range, and that the maintenance of the passes, the forests, the rivers, and the refuge halls is a duty that is shared by every people whose lands depend upon the water and the stone that the highland provides. The Whitewall endures as the pale backbone of [Nordheim](/content/location/natural/continent/nordheim) and as the inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the cold mountains of the northern continent.
