## Overview

The Thunderback Range is the long mountain chain that traverses the interior of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) from the northeastern coast to the southwest, a young and restless range whose central peaks retain their snow through the warm season and whose southern valleys still tremble with the earthquakes that mark the continuing growth of the highland. The range is the watershed of the continent, for it divides the rivers that descend to the eastern bays from the rivers that flow toward the western interior, and the chronicler of the western cartographers has set down that the Thunderback is to [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) what the [Crown Range](/content/location/natural/feature/crown-range) is to [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara), with the important difference that the Thunderback is younger and far more active. The mountains of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) to the north have been worn by countless ages into rounded summits and gentle slopes, but the Thunderback still rises, still shakes, and still alters the courses of its rivers with every strong season.

The range takes its name from the storms that fall from its central peaks, for the chronicler of the coastal pilots has recorded that the storms of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) descend through the valleys as though poured from a bowl, and the thunder of the falling weather is the most constant sound of the highland summer. The name is used by the highland communities and by the foreign pilots alike, and the mountain councils of the interior have accepted it as the formal designation of the range in their records. The chronicler notes that the name Thunderback is sometimes confused in foreign accounts with the older ranges of the colder continents, but the range is wholly distinct in its youth and in its restless character.

## Ridges and Valleys

The central peaks of the Thunderback Range rise high enough to retain snow through the warm season, and the snowfields that lie upon them feed the rivers that descend to both coasts. The summits are bare and wind-scoured, and the rock is the dark stone of a young range, sharp and untamed. The lower ridges are covered in forest and open grass, and the valleys between the ridges are deep and narrow, cut by the rivers and the glaciers that descend from the upper snowfields. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has recorded that the valleys of the central section are among the most difficult in the known world to traverse, for the slopes are steep, the rivers are swift, and the only level ground is found in the occasional basin where the water has gathered long enough to lay down a thin soil.

The southern valleys of the range are the most active in their geology, for the earthquakes that mark the continuing growth of the highland are most frequent along the southern flanks. The chronicler of the southern patrols has recorded that new cliffs appear after the strongest shocks, that hot springs and sulphurous pools emerge from the broken ground, and that the courses of the rivers are altered by landslides that close one valley and open another. The valleys of the southern section are also the most fertile, for the ash-rich soil that the eruptions have laid down over the centuries produces heavy crops of grain and fruit, and the communities that dwell in the sheltered basins enjoy a prosperity that is unusual for so high and so troubled a country.

The contrast between the Thunderback and the worn ranges of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind) is one of the most striking features of the geography of the northern continents. The Morvind ranges have been rounded by countless ages of frost and weather, and their slopes are gentle and their valleys are broad. The Thunderback, by contrast, is still sharp and still growing, and its slopes are steep and its valleys are narrow. The chronicler of the inland cartographers has set down that the difference is the difference between an elder and a youth, and that the two ranges together present a measure of the span of a mountain's life that the student of the world can read at a glance.

## The Eastern Slope

The eastern side of the Thunderback Range is steep and wet, for the prevailing weather of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) drives the rains against the eastern face of the range, and the rivers that descend from the highland snows are full through most of the year. The rivers cut through the dark stone in narrow gorges, and the gorges open at the coast into broad bays and estuaries that are sheltered by the headlands. The eastern slope is the most densely settled of the range, for the floodwater that descends from the mountains each year leaves a layer of dark silt upon the fields, and the soil is among the most fertile in the known world. The chronicler of the eastern agronomists has recorded that the harvest of the eastern valleys is consistently heavier than the harvest of any other region of the continent, and that the abundance has supported a settled population of considerable size since the earliest records.

The largest agricultural settlements of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) are found upon the eastern slope of the Thunderback, where the alluvial fans that lie at the mouths of the highland valleys provide the deep soil and the gentle gradient that the large-scale cultivation of grain requires. The villages of the eastern slope are arranged around communal wells and along the roads that follow the terraces, and the architecture of the slope reflects the climate, with low buildings of stone, broad eaves, and shaded courtyards that catch the breeze in the heat of the summer. The chronicler notes that the terraces require constant maintenance, for the rivers that built them are capable of taking them away, and the terrace walls are rebuilt and strengthened after every flood that the summer storms bring down from the highland valleys.

The forests of the eastern slope are dense and varied, and the lower valleys are the home of the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) who inhabit the older woodlands and the high valleys where the streams remain cool throughout the summer. The elven settlements are small and widely spaced, and their forest management favours mixed stands rather than a single useful tree, for the elven principle is to take without diminishing the woodland. The chronicler records that the elven presence upon the eastern slope is among the most ancient in [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall), and that the elves of the range are held in particular respect by the highland communities for their knowledge of the weather and the water.

## The Western Slope

The western side of the Thunderback Range is drier than the eastern, for the rains have already fallen upon the eastern face, and the air that descends to the west is comparatively warm and dry. The western rivers are shorter and less reliable than the eastern, and they flow through interior basins that are covered by grass, thorn scrub, and seasonal streams. The chronicler of the western agronomists has recorded that the harvest of the western valleys is more variable than the harvest of the eastern, and that the communities of the western slope have developed a body of customary knowledge that governs the use of the limited water and the protection of the grazing grounds. The western slope is less densely settled than the eastern, but the communities that dwell there are self-reliant and resilient, and the chronicler notes that the western slope has produced some of the most accomplished horse-breeders and herders in the known world.

The interior basins of the western slope are the home of the [orcs](/content/species/sapient/orcs) who range across the uplands and the drier interior of the continent. The orc communities follow seasonal routes between pasture and mineral springs, and their presence in the western basins is one of the defining features of the region. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has recorded that the relationship between the orc communities and the human communities of the eastern slope is generally one of cautious trade, and that the highland councils have established agreements that govern the use of the springs and the passes. The agreements are renewed at the customary gatherings of the mountain councils, and the chronicler notes that the renewals have been conducted without serious interruption for many generations.

The high valleys of the western slope are the domain of the [goliaths](/content/species/sapient/goliaths), whose presence in the highest reaches of the range is the most distinctive feature of the highland. The goliaths inhabit the upper valleys and the ridges above the snowline, where the air is thin and the cold is intense, and their knowledge of snow bridges, cliff paths, and mountain weather makes them indispensable guides for any traveller who must cross the high country. The chronicler of the mountain patrols has set down that the goliaths are the most reliable of the highland guides, and that no other people of the range can match their knowledge of the snow and the storm.

## The Weather of the Range

The weather of the Thunderback Range is the most distinctive feature of the highland, and the chronicler of the coastal pilots has set down that the mountains create their own weather in a manner that no other range in the known world quite matches. Cold winds descend from the northern ocean, warm air arrives from the southern seas, and the mountain valleys funnel both toward the eastern coast. Their meeting produces sudden storms that may begin beneath clear skies, and the chronicler has recorded that the storms of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) descend through the valleys as though poured from a bowl, striking the sea and the lowland with little warning. Cloud gathers along the ridge, thunder rolls through the passes, and rain reaches the low country before distant villages have seen the first lightning.

The storms are most frequent in the late summer and the autumn, when the southern sea is warmest and the contrast between the cold winds and the warm air is at its sharpest. The chronicler notes that the people of the highland treat the weather observation as a common duty, and the harbour towers and the signal stations of the range maintain a continuous record of the wind direction, the cloud height, and the time between the distant thunder and the rain. The accuracy of the records has made [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) pilots valued in distant ports, where captains seek their advice before crossing unfamiliar water, and the chronicler records that the highland weather stations are among the most densely maintained in the known world.

The refuge halls of the highland are the most visible sign of the care that the communities take against the weather, and the chronicler of the mountain patrols has set down that the halls are maintained at the highest points of the principal passes, stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber for the use of travellers who are caught by the weather. The halls are administered by the highland wardens, who keep the roads clear of snow during the weeks when the crossings are open and who maintain the warning fires that signal the approach of the worst storms. The chronicler records that the loss of a refuge hall is a disaster for the communities on both sides of the range, and that the maintenance of the halls is a duty that is not neglected in any season.

## Significance

The Thunderback Range is the spine and the watershed of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall), and its restless ridges, its fertile valleys, its stormy weather, and its young mountains are the inheritance that the continent has received from the highland. The range is at once a barrier and a corridor, a watershed and a source of water, a treasury of metal and a foundation of the customary law that governs the use of the springs, the passes, and the grazing grounds. The chronicler of the inland cartographers sets down that the prosperity of the continent is bound up with the health of the range, and that the maintenance of the terraces, the forests, the rivers, and the refuge halls is a duty that is shared by every people whose lands depend upon the water and the stone that the highland provides. The Thunderback endures as the restless backbone of [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall) and as the inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the young mountains of the western continent.
