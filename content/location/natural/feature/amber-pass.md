## The Crossing of the Two Highlands

The Amber Pass is the most reliable of the highland crossings that link the eastern river country of [Ashara](/content/location/natural/continent/ashara) to the western valleys of [Thessara](/content/location/natural/continent/thessara) by way of the great ranges that shoulder against one another at the head of the inland sea. The pass traverses the northern shoulder of the [Sunken Crown](/content/location/natural/feature/sunken-crown) of Ashara, descends into the central basin, and rises again through the [Crown Range](/content/location/natural/feature/crown-range) of Thessara to the eastern terrace lands. The chronicler of the highland roads has set down that the Amber Pass is not the highest of the crossings between the two continents, but it is the most dependable, and the merchants of both regions have long preferred it to the steeper routes of the south. The road has served the inland trade since the first generations of settled life upon the two ranges, and the wear of the wheels and the hooves is visible in the surface of the stone in places where the paving has not been relaid for several generations.

The pass is named for the amber resin that gathers in the soil of the highland forests of the Sunken Crown, and the name is older than the present kingdoms. The amber is gathered by the forest communities in the late dry season, when the ground is at its hardest, and the pieces are carried down to the markets at the foot of the pass for trade with the lowland cities. The chronicler of the highland customs records that the resin was the first of the highland products to find a regular market in the lowlands, and that the road that carried the amber came to be known by the name of the resin itself.

## The Stone Floor and the Springs

The Amber Pass owes its dependability to the geology of the shoulder it crosses and to the quality of the water that the highland yields through the longest dry seasons. The floor of the pass is a bed of dark volcanic stone that the ancient eruptions of the Sunken Crown laid down in a single broad sheet, and the stone is so closely jointed that the rain that falls upon the upper road drains through the cracks before it can gather into channels. The drainage is the foundation of the road's reliability in the wet season, for a crossing of softer stone would become a stream bed after the first heavy storm, and the pack animals would lose their footing upon the mud. The chronicler of the highland road-builders has set down that the stone floor has been the model for the paving of the principal roads of the two continents, and that the engineers of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league) and the river councils of Ashara have studied the paving of the Amber Pass with care.

The springs of the Amber Pass are no less important than the floor, for the highland receives its water from the cloud forest that clings to the upper slopes of the Sunken Crown. The springs rise in several places along the pass, and the water is cool and clear through the driest months of the year. The merchants and the drovers who use the road have long relied upon the springs for the watering of their animals, and the highland communities have built stone troughs and shelters at the principal springs to ensure that the water is not fouled by the traffic. The chronicler records that the springs are the subject of customary agreements between the forest communities and the merchants, and that the maintenance of the troughs is a communal obligation that is observed without serious interruption in any season.

## The Upper Road and the Pack Animals

The upper road, which is the section that traverses the northern shoulder of the Sunken Crown, is a winding path that the highland communities have maintained for many generations. The path is too steep in places for wheeled vehicles, and the merchants and the drovers who use it rely upon pack animals rather than carts. The principal animals are the small, sure-footed donkeys of the Asharan highlands, the hardy mules of the central basin, and the patient oxen of the Thessaran eastern terraces. The chronicler of the highland customs has set down that the drivers of the pack trains are a distinct community of craftsmen, and that the knowledge of the road and the animals is passed from parent to child in a manner similar to the transmission of the highland songs.

The upper road is closed by snow for a portion of the winter, though the closure is generally shorter than that of the higher passes of the two ranges. The highland wardens keep watch upon the upper road from the refuge halls that the customary law requires the highland communities to maintain, and the wardens signal the state of the road to the merchants and the drovers by means of the fire signs that the highland code has refined over many generations. The chronicler records that the closure of the upper road is announced to the merchants of the lowland cities by means of riders who carry the news to the markets at the foot of the pass, and that the merchants mark the period of closure by adjusting the timing of their purchases in the lowland markets.

## The Descent to the Basin

Below the springs and the high meadows, the Amber Pass descends into a broad central basin that lies between the two ranges. The basin is a chain of linked plains, each separated from the next by a low ridge of harder stone, and the road that follows the basin is one of the oldest in the known world. The chronicler of the inland cartographers has recorded that the basin has been a highway for so long that the wear of the wheels and the hooves is visible in the surface of the limestone, and that the road is the principal route by which the highland minerals of the Sunken Crown reach the rivers and the ports of Ashara and the eastern terraces of Thessara.

The towns of the basin are staging points for the caravans, and the merchants of the basin maintain the inns, the warehouses, and the workshops that the road requires. The [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) of the lower passes have established workshops in the basin towns, producing the survey instruments and the small water devices that the farmers of the terraces require. The chronicler notes that the basin is the most cosmopolitan section of the road, for the merchants of the highland and the lowland meet in the basin markets, and the goods of the two continents are exchanged in quantities that no other crossing can match.

## The River Country and the Barges

When the merchants and the drovers reach the eastern edge of the central basin, the road gives way to the rivers that descend from the eastern slope of the Crown Range. The pack animals are offloaded at the principal river towns, and the cargo is transferred to barges that carry it down the rivers to the eastern coast of Thessara and to the lowland markets of Ashara. The chronicler of the river commerce has set down that the transfer is the foundation of the efficiency of the Amber Pass, for the rivers are navigable for the larger part of their length and the barges carry far heavier loads than the pack animals of the upper road.

The barges of the river country are flat-bottomed vessels of considerable size, manned by crews who have grown up upon the rivers and who know the channels, the shallows, and the seasonal floods with a precision that the chronicler of the highland roads has recorded as the most reliable seamanship of the inland waters. The bargemen maintain the booms and the sails that the river requires, and the loading of the cargoes is supervised by the river clerks who record the weight and the destination of every bale. The chronicler notes that the bargemen are held in particular esteem by the merchants of both continents, for the transfer of the cargo from the pack animals to the barges is the moment at which the value of the highland goods is realised in the markets of the lowland.

## The Highland Communities and the Maintenance

The highland communities that inhabit the approaches to the Amber Pass are the principal maintainers of the road, and the maintenance is a communal obligation that the customary law of the highland enforces. The communities of the upper slopes rebuild the stone walls that support the road after the heavy rains, clear the ditches that carry the water away from the paving, and inspect the springs and the troughs that the pack animals require. The chronicler of the highland road-builders has set down that the maintenance is the most thorough of any road in the two continents, and that the road of the Amber Pass has endured for several generations without significant interruption.

The highland wardens of the Amber Pass are drawn from the principal communities of the upper slopes, and the wardens are responsible for the safety of the road and for the assistance of travellers who are caught by the weather. The wardens maintain the refuge halls that stand at intervals along the upper road, and the halls are stocked with dried meat, peat, and spare timber for the use of travellers in time of need. The chronicler records that the wardens are the most trusted of the highland officials, and that the merchants of both continents mark the anniversaries of the founding of the wardenship with appropriate ceremonies.

## Significance

The Amber Pass is one of the most useful of the natural features of the two continents, and the pass is the foundation of the highland trade between Ashara and Thessara. The road, the springs, the upper road of the pack animals, the basin of the central plain, and the barges of the river country are the inheritance that the pass has bequeathed to the present age. The chronicler notes that the pass is held in particular esteem by the merchants and the highland communities of both ranges, and the merchants and the communities mark the anniversaries of the founding of the road with appropriate ceremonies. The Amber Pass endures, in the stone of its floor and the water of its springs, as the living inheritance of those whose work and whose life are bound up with the highland trade between the warm continent and the long continent of the eastern waters.
