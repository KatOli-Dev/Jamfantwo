## Rocky Chain Off the Western Cliffs

Off the western coast of the principal continent, beyond the cliffs that mark the limit of the [Ashen Heath](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/ashen-heath), a chain of small, rocky islands runs northward into the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean). The islands take their name from the steep, broken coastlines that the waves have cut into them, and they are called the Drowned Isles for the way the spray of the ocean seems to overwhelm them in foul weather. They are bounded on the east by the cliffs of the mainland, on the west by the open ocean, on the south by the waters that wash the cliffs of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), and on the north by the colder waters leading to the [Stormfall Coast](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/stormfall-coast).

The islands have been sparsely populated since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era), with the population concentrated in a string of small fishing villages along the sheltered bays of the eastern side of the chain. The villages are connected by small boats that ferry passengers and goods between the islands, serving as the principal means of communication between the villages and the towns of the mainland coast. The islands are one of the most distinctive landscapes of the cold north and are held in particular esteem by the fishermen who make their living upon them.

## Thin Soil, Tough Grasses, and the Farm Exchange

The islands of the chain vary in size from the larger islands supporting the principal villages to smaller rocks rising only a few feet above the water that serve as the home of seabirds breeding upon them. The islands are composed of the same ancient stone that forms the cliffs of the mainland, exposed in the cliffs that drop to the water on every side. They are covered with a thin soil supporting a tough vegetation of grasses and low shrubs, forming the foundation of the limited agriculture on the larger islands.

The islands are home to a small population of farmers who cultivate the higher ground and a larger population of fishermen who work the surrounding waters. The farmers exchange their grain and wool for the fish that the fishermen take, and this exchange is the foundation of the island economy. The islands are the source of much of the fish that the towns of the mainland coast require and are held in high esteem by the magistrates and merchants of those towns.

## Sheltered Anchorages and the Pilot's Beacons

The sheltered bays of the eastern side of the chain are the principal anchorages of the islands, and they have been the home of the fishing villages since the founding of the settlements. The bays are small and the water is deep, protected from the prevailing winds by the cliffs rising to the west. They are the principal landing places for the small boats serving the islands, carrying the supplies that the villages require.

The bays have been the subject of careful survey by the pilots of the [Inner Sea Watch](/content/government/local/inner-sea-watch), who maintain beacons marking the entrances. The bays are the foundation of the safety of navigation around the islands, and the navigation is the source of much of the contact between the islands and the mainland.

## Seabird Colonies and the Island Ecology

The islands support a rich variety of wildlife forming the foundation of the ecology of the chain. The cliffs support seabirds that breed upon the ledges in the spring, including gulls, cormorants, and smaller auks that the Bestiaries and Herbals have catalogued. The interior of the larger islands supports smaller mammals that birds of prey feed upon, and the surrounding waters support the fish that the fishermen pursue.

The wildlife is the source of much of the food of the villages and the foundation of the careful management of the fishery that the elders of the villages maintain. It has been the subject of careful study by the scholars of the academies, who have recorded the patterns of birds and fish in their catalogues.

## Cod, Haddock, and the Village Regulations

The waters around the islands support a rich fishery that has been the foundation of the prosperity of the villages since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era). The principal fish are cod and haddock taken from deeper waters, and herring taken from shallower waters in the summer. The fishery is regulated by the elders of the villages in consultation with the pilots of the [Inner Sea Watch](/content/government/local/inner-sea-watch), with regulations setting the seasons of the catch, the methods fishermen must use, and the prices they may charge.

The fishery has been the subject of long debate between the villages of the islands and the towns of the mainland coast, settled by the careful management that the Watch maintains. The regulations set the proportion of the catch that each party may take, and this proportion has been the foundation of the careful management of the fishery.

## Fires of the Inner Sea Watch

The lighthouses of the islands were erected by the council of [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) in the early generations of the [Long Peace](/content/history/long-peace) and have been maintained by the [Inner Sea Watch](/content/government/local/inner-sea-watch) ever since. They stand upon the highest points of the larger islands, and their lights are visible far out to sea in clear weather. The keepers are senior seamen of the Watch relieved of duty afloat, who maintain the fires, trim the wicks at dusk, and signal the state of the tide by the colour of the lights they show.

The lighthouses have been the foundation of the safety of navigation around the islands and the refuge of many a pilot caught upon the coast in time of storm. They are among the most important public works of the cold north and are held in high esteem by the pilots and merchants of every city of the coast.

## Foundation of the Western Fishery

The Drowned Isles are one of the most distinctive landscapes of the cold north, and they are the foundation of the fishery and the navigation of the western coast. The islands, the bays, the villages, and the lighthouses are the inheritance that the isles have bequeathed to the present age. The isles are held in particular memory by the fishermen and pilots who make their living upon them, and they mark the anniversaries of the founding of the lighthouses with appropriate ceremonies. These ceremonies are among the most important public observances of the cold north, attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every town of the coast.
