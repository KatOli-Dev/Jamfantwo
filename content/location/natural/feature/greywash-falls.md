## Cascades, Mills, and the Flour of the Lower River

Where the Greywash descends from the [Vintner's Vale](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/vintners-vale) to the lower reaches of the river, the channel narrows and the water drops in a series of cascades called the Greywash Falls. The falls lie within a day's ride of [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara) and are one of the most distinctive natural features of the cold north. They are bounded on the east by the lower slopes of the vale, on the west by the upper reaches of the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash), on the north by the cliffs marking the descent, and on the south by the calmer water below.

The falls have been a landmark of the river since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era) and have been the subject of careful study by the scholars of the academies. They are the home of a small population of millers and fishermen, and the mills that the falls have powered are the source of much of the flour that the towns of the lower river require. The falls are held in particular esteem by the merchants and the millers who make their living upon them.

## Three Drops of the Greywash

The cascades descend in three principal drops over a distance of several hundred yards, separated by stretches of calmer water where the river runs over the bedrock of the plateau. The upper cascade is the highest, dropping in a single sheet of water that the academies compare to a curtain of glass. The central cascade is broken into several channels by rocks fallen from the cliffs above and is the most picturesque of the three. The lower cascade is the smallest and is the principal site of the mills that the river supports.

The cascades are the source of the power that the mills require, and the mills have been a feature of the falls since the founding of the towns of the lower river. The mills are the source of much of the flour that the towns require and the foundation of the careful management of the water that the [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) established.

## Rock Faces and the Small Streams

The cliffs flanking the falls are composed of the same ancient stone that forms the plateau of the upper river, exposed in the faces dropping to the water below. They are the home of seabirds that breed upon the ledges in the spring, including the gulls and cormorants that the Bestiaries and Herbals have catalogued. The cliffs are covered with a tough vegetation of shrubs and small trees that the salt spray has shaped into low, wind-bent forms, forming the foundation of the ecology of the falls.

Small streams run off the slopes to the river below, providing the only source of fresh water upon the cliffs. The streams are small and unreliable, sufficient for the small millers' village above the falls but not for any larger settlement.

## Water-Wheels and the Millers' Village

The mills of the falls have been a feature of the river since the [Founding Era](/content/history/founding-era) and are the source of much of the flour that the towns of the lower river require. They are built upon the lower cascade, where the water is most reliable and the head is greatest, and are turned by water-wheels constructed with care. The mills are the most productive of the mills of the cold north and are held in high esteem by the magistrates and merchants of [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara).

The mills are maintained by the millers in consultation with the river warden that the [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) established, and the river warden supervises the distribution of the water among the mills. This distribution has been the foundation of the careful management of the mills and is the source of much of the prosperity of the towns of the lower river.

## Salmon, Trout, and the Greywash Compact

The waters below the falls support a rich fishery that has been the foundation of the prosperity of the villages along the river below the falls. The principal fish are the salmon running up the river in the spring and autumn, the trout taken from the deeper pools below the falls, and the smaller fish taken from the shallows in summer. The fishery is regulated by the river warden in consultation with the elders of the villages, with regulations setting the seasons of the catch, the methods fishermen must use, and the prices they may charge.

The fishery has been the subject of long debate between the towns of the upper river and the towns of the lower river, settled by the careful management that the [Greywash Compact](/content/history/greywash-compact) established. The treaty sets the proportion of the catch that each town may take, and this proportion has been the foundation of the careful management of the fishery.

## Birds, Mammals, and the River Ecology

The falls support a rich variety of wildlife forming the foundation of the ecology of the falls. The cliffs support seabirds that breed upon the ledges in the spring, including the gulls and cormorants that the Bestiaries and Herbals have catalogued. The slopes support smaller mammals that the birds of prey feed upon, and the waters below the falls support the fish that the fishermen pursue.

The wildlife is the source of much of the food of the villages along the river and the foundation of the careful management of the chase that the river warden maintains. The wildlife has been the subject of careful study by the scholars of the academies, who have recorded the patterns of the birds and the fish in their catalogues.

## Milling and the Fishery of the Lower Greywash

The Greywash Falls are one of the most distinctive natural features of the cold north and the foundation of the milling and fishery of the lower river. The cascades, the cliffs, the mills, the villages, and the streams are the inheritance that the falls have bequeathed to the present age. The falls are held in particular memory by the millers and fishermen who make their living upon them, and they mark the anniversaries of the founding with appropriate ceremonies. These ceremonies are among the most important public observances of the cold north, attended by the magistrates and principal citizens of every town of the lower river.
