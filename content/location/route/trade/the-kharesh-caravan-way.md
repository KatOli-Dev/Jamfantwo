## Overview

The Kharesh Caravan Way is the network of overland routes that cross the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), connecting the fertile coastal cities of the north to the scattered oases, the upland settlements, and the distant reaches of the interior. The way is not a single road but a web of routes, each chosen for its access to water, its favourable terrain, and its avoidance of the worst hazards that the desert can offer. The caravans that travel these routes are the lifeline of the interior, carrying the goods of the coast inland and returning with the spices, the minerals, the precious stones, and the other products of the deep desert that have made the fortune of the Sahrani trade. The Kharesh Caravan Way is among the oldest and the most demanding trade routes in the known world, a passage that has been travelled by the same peoples and the same beasts for centuries beyond reckoning.

## The Geography of the Routes

The Caravan Way begins at the chain of coastal cities that line the northern shore of Sahrana, with the primary termini at [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar), [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra), and [Nessara](/content/location/settlement/city/nessara), each serving as the point of departure for the caravans that strike southward into the desert. The routes from these cities converge within a few days of travel, joining into a smaller number of main arteries that carry the bulk of the traffic into the interior. The principal route runs south-south-west from the coast, following the line of the seasonal watercourses that provide the most reliable access to water in the marginal zone that lies between the fertile coast and the deep desert. This route passes through [Dunesend](/content/location/settlement/outpost/dunesend), which serves as the final staging point for the caravans before they enter the true desert, and from the outpost the route strikes southward into the great erg, the vast field of shifting dunes that is the heart of the Kharesh.

Beyond the erg, the route reaches the rocky uplands that rise from the sands like islands in a sea of stone, and it is here that the most important oases and the most substantial settlements of the interior are found. The uplands of the central Kharesh are a chain of massifs that run from the north-west to the south-east, and their sheltered valleys and their perennial springs provide the water and the shade that sustain the settled communities of the interior and the caravans that visit them. The most significant of these upland settlements are the oasis towns of [Kheled](/content/location/settlement/village/kheled) and [Shurra](/content/location/settlement/village/shurra), each situated at the base of a massif where the groundwater emerges to support a thriving community of farmers, herders, and traders. Beyond the central uplands, the route continues southward toward the [Southern Reach](/content/government/national/southern-reach) and the inland seas that lie beyond the Kharesh, though this southern extension of the route is less travelled and more dangerous than the central passages.

## The Goods of the Caravan Trade

The cargoes that move along the Kharesh Caravan Way are a study in contrasts, for the caravans carry the products of two very different worlds and the exchange between them is the foundation of the interior economy. The southward caravans carry the manufactured goods, the cloth, the tools, and the foodstuffs that the interior communities cannot produce for themselves, including the grain of the coastal strip, the iron and the metal goods imported from [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the fine cloth that is woven in the coastal cities. The northward caravans carry the products that have made Sahrana the most sought-after source of luxury goods in the known world, including the spices that are gathered from the desert plants and the rocky uplands, the dyes that are extracted from the insects and the minerals of the interior, and the precious stones that are mined from the veins that run through the upland massifs.

The most valuable cargo that travels the Kharesh Caravan Way is the spice known as sunroot, a rare and aromatic substance that is found only in the deepest and the most inaccessible parts of the desert, where it grows in the shelter of the rocky outcrops that rise from the erg. The sunroot is harvested by the nomads of the deep desert, who trade it to the caravans in exchange for the goods of the coast, and its value in the markets of the northern continents is such that a single pouch of the dried spice can command a price that would support a family for a year. The trade in sunroot is the most lucrative and the most secretive of the caravan commerce, and the locations of the sunroot groves are closely guarded by the nomads who harvest them, passed down through the generations as a trust that is never committed to writing.

## The Peoples of the Way

The Kharesh Caravan Way is travelled and sustained by a diverse population of peoples whose lives are shaped by the demands of the desert and the rhythms of the trade. The merchants who organise the caravans are based chiefly in the coastal cities, where they finance the expeditions and arrange for the sale of the cargoes that return from the interior. These merchants are members of the [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast) and the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the two great commercial alliances that dominate the trade of Sahrana, and their competition for the most valuable cargoes and the most favourable terms is a constant feature of the coastal economy. The guides who lead the caravans across the desert are drawn from the [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa), the nomadic people whose knowledge of the Kharesh is the most comprehensive and the most reliable of any sapient inhabitants of the desert. The salawa have travelled the routes of the Caravan Way for generations, and their understanding of the shifting dunes, the hidden water sources, and the seasonal patterns of the desert weather is the foundation upon which the entire caravan trade depends. The guides are the most respected members of any caravan, for their decisions can mean the difference between the safe arrival at the next oasis and the slow death that awaits the lost in the deep desert.

The nomads who inhabit the deep desert, including the salawa and the [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) tribes that range across the erg, are the primary suppliers of the goods that the caravans carry northward. These nomads move across the desert in patterns that are governed by the availability of water and the seasonal growth of the desert plants, and their encounters with the caravans are the most significant events in their annual cycle. The exchanges between the nomads and the caravans are conducted according to traditions that are older than any written law, and the terms of the trade are established through negotiation and the mutual recognition of the value that each party brings to the exchange. The nomads are not a single people but a collection of kindreds and tribes, each with its own territory, its own traditions, and its own relationships with the caravans that cross its lands, and the successful navigation of these relationships is one of the most demanding skills that a caravan leader must acquire.

## The Hazards of the Crossing

The crossing of the Kharesh is among the most dangerous journeys that any traveller can undertake, and the hazards that the caravans face are as varied as they are unforgiving. The most immediate and the most relentless hazard is the lack of water, for the distances between the reliable water sources in the deep desert are measured in days and weeks of travel, and the margin between the sufficient provision and the fatal shortage is narrow in the extreme. The caravans carry water in the skins of goats and in the sealed clay vessels that are made for the purpose, and the schedule of the journey is dictated by the locations of the wells and the springs that punctuate the route. The wells of the Kharesh are the most precious resource of the desert, and they are protected by the traditions and the agreements that have been established among the peoples who use them. A well that is polluted or a spring that has dried up can be a catastrophe for a caravan that has no alternative source of water within reach, and the knowledge of the condition of the wells is the most closely guarded information that the guides possess.

The heat of the Kharesh is the second great hazard, and the combination of the intense sun by day and the freezing cold by night is a physical demand that tests the endurance of every traveller who crosses the desert. The caravans travel by night during the hottest months, when the sun would kill a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) within hours of exposure, and the pauses for rest and for prayer are scheduled around the rhythm of the sun and the moon. The seasonal winds are another hazard, for the sandstorms that they can generate are capable of burying a caravan in a matter of hours, and the dunes that shift with each major storm can obliterate the landmarks that the guides rely upon for navigation. The sandstorms are most common in the spring and the autumn, and the caravans that travel during these periods must be prepared to sit out the storms in the shelter of the tents and the pack animals until the wind abates and the sky clears.

The bandits and the raiders of the desert are the third great hazard, and the history of the Caravan Way is marked by the recurring challenge of the outlaws who prey on the trade that passes through their territories. The raiders are drawn from the marginal communities that inhabit the fringes of the desert, and they strike at the slower and the less well-defended caravans, making off with the goods and the animals before melting back into the dunes. The caravans are defended by armed guards who are recruited from the coastal cities and the desert tribes, and the size and the quality of the guard force is a significant factor in the cost of the expedition. The larger caravans are less vulnerable to attack, for the raiders prefer to avoid the forces that can offer a determined resistance, and the caravans that travel the main routes in the peak season are generally safe from anything but the most audacious assault.

## The Economic and Cultural Significance

The Kharesh Caravan Way is the economic backbone of the Sahrani interior, and its significance extends far beyond the value of the goods that travel along its routes. The trade that the Caravan Way sustains is the foundation of the prosperity of the coastal cities, for the spices, the minerals, and the precious stones of the interior are the primary exports that flow through the harbours of the [League of the Northern Coast](/content/government/national/league-of-the-northern-coast) to the markets of Aethelar and beyond. The coastal cities depend upon the caravans for the supply of the goods that make their trade profitable, and the interruption of the caravan routes is the greatest threat to the prosperity of the coast that any political or natural event can pose. The Caravan Way is also a channel for the exchange of culture and of ideas between the coast and the interior, and the movement of the peoples along its routes has shaped the languages, the customs, and the beliefs of both the settled and the nomadic communities that share the desert. The stories that are told around the campfires of the Caravan Way are a blend of the traditions of the coast and the interior, and the songs that are sung by the salawa guides and the merchants from Khalisar are a testament to the mingling of the two worlds that the trade has made possible.

## The Future of the Caravan Way

The Kharesh Caravan Way faces challenges that are as significant as the hazards of the desert itself, and the future of the route will depend upon the ability of the peoples who use it to adapt to the changing conditions of the world. The growth of the maritime trade has shifted some of the traffic from the overland routes to the sea, and the cost of the caravan transport has made the interior goods more expensive than the competing products from other regions. The caravans that travel the Kharesh are fewer and smaller than they were in earlier centuries, and the communities that depend upon the trade have learned to adapt their economies to the reduced volume of the traffic. The knowledge of the desert that is the foundation of the Caravan Way is a resource that cannot be replaced, however, and the salawa and the other nomadic peoples who hold that knowledge will continue to sustain the trade for as long as there is value in the goods that the interior provides. The route that has connected the coast to the interior for so many centuries will endure, though its shape and its significance may change with the times that lie ahead.
