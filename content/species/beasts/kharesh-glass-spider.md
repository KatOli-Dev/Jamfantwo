## Overview

The Kharesh glass spider is the most feared of the arthropod predators of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), a great arachnid of crystalline carapace whose range extends across the rocky uplands and the edges of the deep erg of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana). The spider takes its name from the translucent quality of its exoskeleton, which catches the sunlight in a manner that the desert peoples have likened to blown glass, and the creature is reputed among the most venomous of the known world. The chronicler notes that the glass spider is the subject of an extensive body of lore among the caravan peoples, who regard it with a mixture of fear and a grudging respect, and the bite of the spider is reckoned among the most serious of the hazards of the desert crossing.

## Form and Appearance

The glass spider is a large arachnid, with a leg span that may reach the span of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) hand from the tip of the foremost leg to the tip of the hindmost, though the body is comparatively modest in size and the great leg span gives an impression of size that is greater than the actual weight of the creature. The body is divided into the two characteristic regions of the spiders, the cephalothorax and the abdomen, and the junction between them is narrowed to a slender waist that allows the spider to twist and to turn within the confined spaces of its lair. The cephalothorax bears the eight eyes of the arachnid, arranged in two rows of four, and the chelicerae, the paired fangs that deliver the venom, are of considerable size and are capable of piercing the thickest of the desert boots.

The carapace of the glass spider is its most distinctive feature, and it is the source of the creature's common name. The exoskeleton is a pale and translucent material, almost glass-like in its clarity, and the tissues that lie beneath are visible through the shell as pale and shifting shapes. The colour of the carapace varies with the diet of the individual, ranging from the pale amber of a spider that has fed upon the sand lizards to the deeper red of one that has taken the blood of the larger mammals, and the desert trackers are said to be able to judge the recent prey of a spider by the colour of its shell. The legs are long and tapering, each tipped with a claw that is suited to the grip upon the loose sand and the rough stone, and the legs are covered in fine hairs that are sensitive to the vibrations of the sand and that serve the spider as its principal organ of perception.

## Habitat and Lair

The glass spider inhabits the rocky uplands and the margins of the deep desert, the regions where the sand gives way to the outcrops of weathered stone and the sparse vegetation that the desert supports. The spider is not found in the deep erg, where the soft sand is unsuited to its method of hunting, nor in the true oases, where the dense vegetation and the presence of the larger predators keep the spider at bay. The favoured ground is the broken country of the wadis and the spurs, where the spider can find shelter beneath the stones and where the prey is plentiful upon the trails that thread between the rocks.

The lair of the glass spider is a burrow that the spider excavates beneath a stone or in the lee of a cliff, and the entrance is closed by a sheet of the spider's silk that is laid across the opening and that is dusted with the sand of the surroundings to render the door invisible. The silk of the glass spider is remarkable for its strength, far exceeding the silk of the spiders of the temperate lands, and a single thread is capable of bearing many times the weight of the spider that spins it. The silk is used not only in the construction of the lair but also in the hunting, for the spider lays a fan of fine threads across the sand in front of the lair, and the vibration of a creature that has touched the threads is the signal that draws the spider from its burrow.

## Hunting and Venom

The glass spider is an ambush predator that hunts by the vibrations that its silk transmits from the ground about the lair. The spider lies within the burrow, with its legs resting upon the fan of threads that radiate from the entrance, and the contact of a passing creature upon the threads is detected through the fine hairs that cover the spider's legs. The spider determines the size and the direction of the prey from the pattern of the vibrations, and it emerges from the burrow with a speed that is startling for a creature of its size. The prey is taken by a sudden rush from the lair, the spider leaping upon the quarry and seizing it with the great chelicerae, which deliver the venom with a precision that the chroniclers of the desert have likened to the strike of a serpent.

The venom of the glass spider is a complex mixture of compounds that serves both to subdue the prey and to begin the digestion of the tissues. The venom of the smaller spiders is not ordinarily fatal to [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), though it produces a severe local reaction and a period of incapacitation that may last for several days, but the venom of the larger females is among the most dangerous of the known world, and an untreated bite is fatal in a significant proportion of cases. The symptoms of a serious bite include a violent pain at the site of the wound, a swelling that spreads rapidly up the affected limb, a profuse sweating, and a progressive paralysis of the muscles that, if unchecked, leads to a failure of the respiration. The desert peoples treat the bite with a number of traditional remedies, including the application of the crushed leaves of certain desert plants and the binding of the limb above the wound, and the more accomplished of the healers are said to be able to draw the venom from the wound by the application of a heated stone.

## Diet and Prey

The diet of the glass spider is varied, and the spider takes whatever creature of appropriate size blunders into the range of its silk. The smaller spiders feed upon the insects of the desert, the sand lizards, and the small rodents that inhabit the rocky margins, and the larger females are capable of taking prey as large as a young goat or a fox. The spider does not ordinarily attack the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) or the [salawa](/content/species/sapient/salawa) of the caravans, though it will do so if disturbed or if the lair is threatened, and the caravan masters take care to pitch the camps at a distance from any ground that shows the characteristic fan of the spider's silk. The spider is capable of going for long periods without food, and the desert trackers speak of lairs that have been found with the remains of many prey animals within them, suggesting that the spider accumulates a larder against the lean times of the deep desert.

## Reproduction and Life Cycle

The life cycle of the glass spider is closely tied to the seasons of the desert, with the mating occurring in the late winter when the desert is at its coolest and the young hatching in the spring when the first flush of vegetation brings the prey within reach of the lair. The male is much smaller than the female, and he approaches the female's lair with great caution, for the female is known to take the male as readily as any other prey if the mating advances are not conducted with the proper care. The courtship is a long and elaborate affair, the male plucking upon the threads of the female's fan in a pattern that the female recognises as the approach of a suitor, and the mating itself is conducted within the female's lair over a period of several hours.

The eggs are laid within a silken sac that the female constructs within the deepest chamber of the lair, and the sac may contain several hundred eggs. The female guards the sac until the young hatch, and the young spiders remain with the mother for several weeks before they disperse to find their own lairs. The mortality of the young is high, for the eggs and the hatchlings are preyed upon by a number of the desert creatures, and only a small fraction of the brood survives to maturity. The female dies after the dispersal of the young, the exhaustion of the breeding taking its toll upon a creature that does not feed during the period of the guarding of the sac.

## Significance

The Kharesh glass spider is among the most distinctive of the predators of the desert, and its presence in the rocky uplands is a measure of the health of the margin between the deep erg and the habitable country. The spider is the object of an extensive lore among the caravan peoples, and the tales of the great spiders that haunt the lonely wadis of the deep desert are a familiar feature of the storytelling of the campfire. The chronicler notes that the spider is not wantonly destructive, for it takes only what it requires to sustain itself, and the loss of a goat or a sheep to a spider is an unusual event rather than a regular occurrence. The spider is, however, a creature that the caravan masters respect, and the care that the travellers take to avoid the lairs of the glass spider is one of the small disciplines that make the desert crossing possible.
