## Overview

The phoenix is a great bird of the high mountains and the volcanic country, a creature of singular beauty and of singular reputation whose range extends across the highest ranges of the three continents. The phoenix takes its name from the plumage of flame-coloured feathers that the creature displays in its aspect, and the bird is held by the naturalists of the academies of [Amurra](/content/government/national/amurra-republic) to be one of the most singular of the avifauna of the world. The chronicler notes that the phoenix is not a creature of the open country or the dense forest, for the bird requires the high cliffs of the volcanic peaks and the shelter of the craters and the warm caves that are found there, and the phoenix is found in greatest numbers in the highest ranges of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and in the volcanic peaks of the eastern continent.

## Form and Appearance

The phoenix is a great bird, with a body that is set upon two powerful legs and a pair of wings that are the bird's most celebrated feature. The plumage of the phoenix is the creature's most singular attribute, and the colour of the feathers is a brilliant gold that shades to a deep crimson at the tips of the wings and the tail, and the plumage is said by the chroniclers of the high country to be of a luminosity that is visible at a considerable distance. The feathers of the phoenix are not the feathers of the ordinary birds, for the feathers of the phoenix are possessed of a peculiar quality that the naturalists of the academies have not been able to explain, and the feathers that have been preserved in the cabinets of the curious are said to retain a measure of warmth long after the bird that bore them has departed.

The head of the phoenix is set with a long and graceful neck and a hooked beak of considerable size, and the eyes are large and of a deep and lustrous black that the chroniclers of the high country have likened to the colour of the obsidian of the volcanic country. The tail of the phoenix is long and is composed of feathers that are longer than the body of the bird, and the tail is spread in the manner of the display of the great birds of the warm countries, the phoenix carrying the tail in a train that trails behind the creature when it is in flight. The size of the phoenix is considerable, and the wingspan of a mature bird is greater than the wingspan of the [stormwing roc](/content/species/beasts/stormwing-roc) of the mountain ranges, though the body of the phoenix is lighter in proportion to the wings than the body of the roc.

## Habitat and Range

The phoenix inhabits the high mountains and the volcanic country of the three continents, with the largest populations in the highest ranges of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and in the volcanic peaks of the eastern continent, and a smaller population in the southern highlands of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) that lies above the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). The phoenix requires the high cliffs of the volcanic peaks and the shelter of the craters and the warm caves that are found there, and the bird is not found in the open country or in the dense forest. The nest of the phoenix is typically situated upon a ledge of a cliff that is warmed by the vapours of the volcanic country, and the nest is a substantial structure of sticks and of the shed feathers of the parent birds that is built up over many seasons of use.

The phoenix is a solitary bird outside the brief season of the breeding, and the mature birds hold ranges that they mark by the song that the male utters at the dawn and the dusk of the day, a song of singular beauty that the chroniclers of the high country have likened to the sound of a bell of fine silver. The range of a mature phoenix may extend for many miles through the high country, and the bird ranges widely in search of the prey that sustains it. The phoenix is most active in the hours of daylight, and the bird is a familiar sight in the high country, the creature circling upon the updraughts above the volcanic peaks in a manner that the mountain peoples regard as one of the great spectacles of the high country.

## Diet and Behaviour

The phoenix is a bird of considerable appetite, and the creature feeds upon a wide range of prey according to the opportunity. The principal prey of the phoenix is the smaller creatures of the high country, including the mountain hares and the marmots of the alpine meadows, and a phoenix will take several such creatures in the course of a day. The phoenix is also a predator of the birds of the high country, and the creature will take the chicks of the cliff-nesting birds when the nests are unguarded. The phoenix does not ordinarily attack [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), but the accounts of the high country include a number of incidents in which a phoenix has defended a nest with a ferocity that the mountain communities have learned to respect.

The hunting technique of the phoenix is to quarter the high country at a height that is sufficient to allow the keen eyes to detect the movement of the prey, and the phoenix descends upon the prey with a swift and powerful stoop. The prey is seized in the talons of the great feet and borne to a sheltered spot upon the cliff to be consumed, and the prey is torn by the hooked beak. The phoenix is capable of a long and sustained flight, and the bird will range for many miles from the nest in the course of a day's hunting. The phoenix is also an active forager upon the ground, and the bird will walk the alpine meadows in search of the insects and the small creatures that are found among the rocks.

## The Phoenix in Legend and Tradition

The phoenix occupies a singular place in the traditions of the high country, and the bird is the subject of a body of lore that is among the most celebrated of the legendary traditions of the three continents. The chroniclers of every nation have preserved accounts of the phoenix, and the bird is the symbol of the renewal and the perseverance of the bright things of the world. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the high country hold that the phoenix is the eldest of the birds and that the creature was old when the first of the elven kind set foot upon the world, and the elves regard the phoenix with a reverence that is not accorded to any other bird. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) do not hunt the phoenix, for the dwarven traditions hold that the phoenix is a creature of the upper world and that the dwarves are the lords of the lower, and the dwarves take care to seal the upper passages of their halls against the intrusion of the creature.

The phoenix is the subject of a celebrated tradition that is found in the lore of every people of the world, the tradition of the renewal of the bird from the ashes of its own pyre. The accounts of the high country describe how a phoenix that has come to the end of its long life will build a nest of the sweet-scented woods of the high country and will set the nest alight with the heat of its own body, the bird being consumed in the flames and a young phoenix rising from the ashes of the pyre. The naturalists of the academies have debated the reality of the tradition for centuries, and the question has not been settled to the satisfaction of all parties, but the chroniclers of the high country are unanimous that the bird is possessed of a longevity that is not matched by any other creature of the world.

## Significance

The phoenix is the foremost of the great birds of the high mountains, and the presence of the creature in the volcanic country of the three continents is a measure of the wildness that endures in the country of the peaks. The phoenix is a predator that contributes to the balance of the smaller creatures of the high country, and the loss of the phoenix from any district would be followed by a deterioration of the highland pasture that the chroniclers have noted in the records of the mountain communities. The chronicler notes that the phoenix endures, in the high country of the three continents and in the legendary lore of every people that shares the world with the bird, as a symbol of the wildness that persists in the country of the peaks, and the song of the phoenix at the dawn is a reminder that the upper country is not empty of life nor subject to the order of the valleys below.
