## Overview

The iron-backed bear is the largest terrestrial predator of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/morvind-uplands), a great bear of dark pelage and remarkable strength whose range extends across the coniferous forests of the lower slopes and the valleys of the mountain interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). It takes its name from the grey patch upon the shoulders that foresters have likened to a plate of iron; the creature is reputed among the most dangerous predators of the northern continent. The iron-backed bear is held in wary respect by the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain holds, who regard the creature as a hazard of the surface world and seal the lower entrances of their halls against its intrusion.

## Form and Appearance

The iron-backed bear is a heavily built animal, with a body that is broad and muscular set on short powerful legs capable of supporting the great weight of the mature animal. The head is large and rounded, with a broad muzzle and small eyes set above a powerful jaw armed with heavy molars and great canines that are the bear's principal weapons. The claws of the forepaw are long and curved, capable of delivering a blow that has been known to strip the bark from a mature fir with a single stroke.

The coat is the most distinctive feature. The pelage is a dark brown, almost black in mature animals, with a broad patch of grey or silver-tipped hair across the shoulders and upper back that gives the bear its common name. The patch is most pronounced in old males; foresters of the mountain valleys judge the age of a bear by the extent and brightness of the patch. The winter coat is dense and long; the underwool is among the finest of the mountain furs. Pelts are valued by trappers of the highland valleys and merchants of the lowland towns.

Size varies with season and abundance of food; a well-fed male in the autumn may attain a weight several times that of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) of average height. The bears are at their leanest in early spring, when they emerge from the dens in which they have passed the winter, foraging hungrily upon new growth of the meadows and carrion left by the winter upon the snow. Bears gain weight rapidly through summer and autumn as they feed upon ripening berries and fish of the streams; the autumn feeding is the period when the bear lays down the fat that carries it through the winter sleep.

## Habitat and Range

The iron-backed bear inhabits the coniferous forests of the Morvind Uplands, the dense woodlands that clothe the lower slopes of the mountains and extend down to the sheltered valleys of the interior. It prefers the broken country of the forest margin and clearings, where berries and new growth provide the bulk of the food, and ranges widely through the woodlands in the course of the seasons. The bear is solitary outside the brief mating season; mature animals hold ranges marked by scratches of great claws upon the trunks of firs and by scent left upon prominent rocks.

The dens are excavated beneath the roots of great firs or in natural caves of the broken country; the bear retires to the den in late autumn when the first snow lies upon the high country. The winter sleep is not a true hibernation; the bear may emerge during warmer winter days to forage briefly upon the snow, but it remains within the den for the greater part of the winter months, drawing upon the fat laid down during the autumn feeding. The cubs are born within the den in late winter and remain with the mother through the spring and summer of their first year before dispersing to find their own ranges.

## Diet and Behaviour

The iron-backed bear is an omnivore; the diet varies with the season and availability of food. In spring the bear feeds upon new growth of the meadows, carrion left exposed by winter, and digs for roots and tubers of the forest floor with the long claws of the forepaw. In summer it takes berries that ripen in clearings and insects found beneath the bark of fallen trees. In autumn the bear feeds voraciously upon ripening berries and nuts, and it also takes fish that ascend the streams to spawn, wading into the shallows to seize them with a stroke of the great paw.

The bear is not ordinarily aggressive toward [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans); a bear that is undisturbed generally avoids encountering a traveller or forester. But a bear with cubs or a bear surprised at close quarters is a creature of extreme danger; the mountain communities give the bears a wide berth during the season when the cubs are at heel. The bear is also a hazard to livestock of the valley farms; shepherds must keep their flocks within the protection of dogs and watchers, for a bear that has found a flock will take several sheep in a single night. The bear is a capable hunter of deer and elk of the mountain forests; mature males are known to take a yearling deer with a sudden rush from the cover of underbrush.

## The Bear and the Dwarven Holds

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) have a long acquaintance with the iron-backed bear; dwarven traditions hold that the bear is the most dangerous of the surface predators that venture near the dwarven halls. The dwarves do not hunt the bear except in the case of a creature that has taken to killing livestock or that has entered a hall; customary law forbids the taking of a bear not actively engaged in such depredation. The dwarves seal the lower entrances with iron grilles designed to exclude the bear and dispose of kitchen waste in a manner that does not attract the creature to the vicinity of the gates.

The hunting of the iron-backed bear is a recognised pursuit of the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the mountain valleys; trappers take a small number each year under licence from the councils. The hunt is conducted with the spear or the heavy bow; the hunter must have the skill and courage to bring the creature down before it can close the distance, for the charge is of terrifying speed and the blow of the great paw is sufficient to break the bones of a horse. The bear is accounted the most dangerous of mountain game; the hunter who has taken a bear is honoured with a title second only to that of the hunter who has taken the snow cat.

## Significance

The iron-backed bear is the foremost of the terrestrial predators of the Morvind Uplands; its presence in the mountain forests is a measure of the health of the woodland ecosystem. It is the predator that keeps the populations of deer and elk in balance with the carrying capacity of the forest, and its foraging upon berries and insects is a factor in the maintenance of the diversity of the forest floor. The dwarves of the mountain holds regard the bear with a wary respect; the precautions they take against its intrusion are among the most practical of the surface traditions of the dwarven communities. The bear endures in the forests of the lower slopes and in the lore of the mountain peoples as a symbol of the wildness that persists in the country of the dwarven halls; the creature is a reminder that the surface of the mountain is not wholly subject to the order of the holds that lie beneath.
