## Overview

The [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) reef serpent is the largest of the marine reptiles of the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), a long-bodied predator of the warm coastal waters that inhabit the reefs and the submerged ridges of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean). The serpent is held in wary respect by the maritime communities of the strait, who regard it as the foremost predator of the coastal waters and whose fishermen take care to read the signs of its presence before they set their nets upon the reefs. The chronicler notes that the serpent is not a creature of the open ocean, for it prefers the broken country of the reefs and the straits, and it is rarely encountered by the vessels that ply the deep waters of the Auric.

## Form and Appearance

The Velden reef serpent is a long and slender reptile, with a body that may exceed the length of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) span by several times, though the great majority of the serpents that are encountered by the fishermen are of more modest size. The body is sinuous and muscular, adapted to the sinuous motion that is the serpent's characteristic mode of swimming, and the tail is flattened from side to side to form a vertical fin that is the principal organ of propulsion. The skin is covered in small scales that are smooth to the touch and that shed water readily as the serpent moves through the waves, and the colour of the skin varies from the dark green of the adults to the lighter olive of the young.

The head of the reef serpent is narrow and elongated, with a jaw that is set with rows of sharp and backward-curving teeth that are suited to the seizing and the holding of slippery prey. The eyes are small and adapted to the moderate light of the coastal waters, and the serpent is reputed to be able to detect the movement of a fish at a considerable distance in the clear water of the strait. The nostrils are set high upon the snout and can be closed when the serpent dives, and the serpent is capable of remaining submerged for long periods, drawing upon the air that is held in the expanded lung. The serpent is not venomous, and the danger that it presents to the swimmer or the diver is from the great jaws and the muscular coils rather than from any poison.

## Habitat and Range

The reef serpent inhabits the coastal waters of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), where the warm currents support a rich fauna of fish and of the smaller marine creatures upon which the serpent feeds. The serpent is most numerous in the vicinity of the reefs and the submerged ridges, where the broken country provides a cover for the ambush and where the prey is concentrated by the currents. The serpent does not range into the colder waters of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), and it is seldom encountered outside the strait and the adjacent coast, though an occasional individual is reported from the harbours of the eastern Aethelaran coast.

The serpent is a creature of the warm season, and it is most active in the late spring and the summer when the waters of the strait are at their warmest and the prey is most abundant. In the winter the serpent retires to the deeper water beyond the reefs, where it lies in a torpor upon the bottom and draws upon the fat that it has laid down during the active months. The serpent does not migrate in the manner of the great fishes of the open ocean, and the populations of the strait are largely resident, the individuals holding the same range through the seasons and the years.

## Diet and Hunting

The principal prey of the reef serpent is the fish of the coastal waters, and the serpent is an ambush predator that lies in wait among the rocks of the reef and the submerged ridges. The serpent is coloured in a manner that blends with the weed and the shadows of the reef, and it remains motionless for long periods, waiting for the approach of a fish of suitable size. The strike is sudden and the prey is seized in the jaws and held by the backward-curving teeth, and the serpent then retires to a sheltered spot to consume the catch at its leisure. The serpent takes the larger fish of the reef, including the grouper and the snapper, and the smaller individuals are known to take the squid and the octopus that haunt the rocky bottom.

The serpent does not ordinarily attack [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), and the divers of the pearl beds of the strait are able to work the reefs in safety except in the rare circumstances when a serpent is provoked or when the water is too warm and the serpent is unusually active. The fishermen of the strait regard the serpent as a competitor for the fish of the reef, and they take every opportunity to drive a serpent from the vicinity of their nets, though they are careful not to engage a large adult at close quarters. The serpent has been known to take a dog that has been swimming from a fishing boat, and there are records of attacks upon bathers in the shallow water of the beaches, though such attacks are rare and are usually attributable to a serpent that has been provoked or that has mistaken the swimmer for a seal.

## Reproduction

The reproduction of the reef serpent is a matter that the naturalists of the strait have studied with care, for the serpent is a creature of interest to the fishermen and the gathering of the eggs is a customary practice of certain of the coastal communities. The serpent comes ashore to lay its eggs upon the sandy beaches of the strait, and the female selects a site above the high-water line where the sand is warm and the sun will provide the heat that is required for the incubation. The female excavates a hollow in the sand with her hind flippers, and she lays a clutch of several dozen eggs in the hollow, covering them with the sand before she returns to the water.

The eggs are left to incubate in the warmth of the sand, and the young serpents hatch after a period of several weeks, the timing varying with the temperature of the sand and the conditions of the season. The young serpents make their way to the water as soon as they hatch, and they are independent of the mother from the moment of the hatching. The mortality of the young is high, for the eggs and the hatchlings are preyed upon by a number of the coastal creatures, including the crabs and the gulls, and only a small fraction of the brood survives to maturity. The gathering of the eggs of the reef serpent is regulated by the customs of the coastal villages, and the taking of the eggs is permitted only during a brief season and only in the quantities that the community may require.

## The Coastal Communities and the Serpent

The communities of the strait have a long acquaintance with the reef serpent, and the lore of the fishermen includes a substantial body of knowledge of the creature's habits and its moods. The fishermen read the signs of the serpent's presence in the behaviour of the fish and the birds, and they know that a surface of unusually calm water upon the reef, or a sudden scattering of the gulls from a particular spot, may indicate a serpent lying in wait beneath. The fishing boats are careful to keep a distance from any serpent that is sighted, and the crews carry long poles with which to fend off a serpent that approaches too closely.

The pearl divers of the strait are the most exposed of the maritime communities to the danger of the serpent, and the traditions of the diving guilds include a number of practices that are designed to reduce the risk. The divers are careful to work in pairs, with one diver in the water while the other remains upon the boat, and the divers carry a sharp knife with which they may cut themselves free from the coils of a serpent that has seized them. The guilds maintain a careful record of the sightings of the serpents, and the captains of the diving boats consult the records before they set out upon the water. The serpent is not held in superstition by the maritime communities, for the strait peoples are a practical folk, and the creature is regarded as a hazard to be managed rather than a spirit to be propitiated.

## Significance

The Velden reef serpent is the foremost predator of the coastal waters of the strait, and its presence in the reef country is a measure of the health of the marine ecosystem. The serpent is the predator that keeps the populations of the reef fish in balance with the productivity of the waters, and the loss of the serpent from any district would be followed by an overpopulation of the prey species that would in time upset the order of the reef. The serpent is not held in particular reverence by the coastal communities, but it is respected as a creature of the waters, and the customary practices that have grown up around the management of the serpent are among the most practical of the maritime traditions of the strait. The chronicler notes that the serpent endures, as it has endured for centuries, in the warm waters of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the Auric, and the fishermen of the coastal villages continue to read the signs of its presence and to give it the wide berth that its size and its power demand.
