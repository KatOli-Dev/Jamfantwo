## Overview

The storm petrel is the most numerous of the seabirds of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), a small and dark bird whose presence upon the waters is a familiar sight to the mariners of the strait and the adjacent coasts. The petrel takes its name from the belief of the sailors that the bird appears upon the water in advance of the storms that move across the sea, a belief that has been the subject of considerable learned debate but that retains its hold upon the lore of the maritime communities. The chronicler notes that the storm petrel is one of the most extensively studied of the seabirds of the northern waters, and the naturalists of the strait towns have contributed substantially to the understanding of the bird's life and its habits.

## Form and Appearance

The storm petrel is a small bird, with a body that is roughly the size of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) hand and a wingspan that is several times the length of the body. The plumage is a dark sooty brown across the back, the wings, and the head, with a paler grey or white upon the underparts and a distinctive white patch upon the rump that is visible when the bird is in flight. The bill is short and hooked, suited to the taking of the small creatures that constitute the bird's prey, and the legs are short and slender, with webbed feet that are adapted to the swimming that is the bird's occasional mode of progression.

The flight of the storm petrel is the bird's most distinctive feature, and it is the source of the bird's reputation as a herald of the weather. The bird flies low over the water with a fluttering and erratic motion, the wings beating rapidly and the body seeming to dance upon the surface of the waves, and the flight is unlike that of any of the other seabirds of the Stormfall. The bird's feet are often seen to patter upon the surface of the water as it flies, a behaviour that the naturalists have interpreted as a means of detecting the small creatures that the bird takes from the surface, and the pattering is one of the characteristic sounds of the sea in the vicinity of a flock of petrels.

## Habitat and Range

The storm petrel is found upon the waters of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) and the adjacent reaches of the [Strait of Velden](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), with the greatest concentration of the birds in the vicinity of the rocky islands and the cliffs of the strait. The bird breeds upon the offshore islands and upon the inaccessible cliffs of the mainland, where the nests are placed in the burrows that the birds excavate in the peaty soil or in the crevices among the rocks, and the colonies that are found upon the larger islands may number many thousands of pairs. The birds are present upon the breeding grounds from the early spring until the late autumn, and they disperse to the open waters of the sea for the winter months.

The bird is most numerous in the vicinity of the fishing grounds and the upwellings that bring the small creatures of the sea to the surface, and the flocks that gather upon the water in the wake of a fishing vessel may number many hundreds of birds. The petrel is a bird of the open sea rather than of the coast, and the birds do not ordinarily approach the shore except at the breeding colonies, and the maritime communities of the strait regard the appearance of the petrel upon the water as a sign that the vessel is well out to sea.

## Diet and Feeding

The storm petrel feeds upon the small creatures of the surface waters, including the tiny crustaceans and the fish fry that are brought to the surface by the currents and the upwellings. The bird takes the prey from the surface of the water as it flies, the bill snapping upon the small creatures with a rapid and precise motion, and the bird is also capable of pattering upon the surface with the feet to detect the prey that lies just beneath. The bird is an opportunistic feeder, and the flocks will gather upon any spot where the prey is concentrated, including the slicks of oil and the floating weed that mark the convergence of the currents.

The bird is also a follower of ships, and the flocks that gather in the wake of a vessel may persist for as long as the vessel is underway, the birds feeding upon the small creatures that are disturbed by the passage of the hull and upon the refuse that is discharged from the galley. The presence of the petrels in the wake of a vessel is a familiar sight to the mariners of the strait, and the birds are accounted among the most agreeable of the companions of the long passage. The bird does not dive beneath the surface in pursuit of the prey, for its plumage is not adapted to the submersion, and the bird relies upon the surface-feeding for the entirety of its sustenance.

## The Lore of the Storms

The storm petrel has long been associated in the lore of the maritime communities with the weather that moves across the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), and the bird is popularly believed to appear upon the water in advance of the storms. The naturalists of the present age have offered several explanations of the association, the most plausible of which holds that the bird is driven to the surface by the disturbance of the water that precedes the storm, and that the bird's appearance upon the waves is therefore a consequence rather than a cause of the coming weather. The mariners of the strait are not disposed to abandon the older belief, and the sight of the petrels gathering upon the water is still accounted a reliable sign that the weather is about to change.

The petrel is also associated in the maritime lore with the souls of the sailors who have died upon the sea, and the bird is sometimes called the storm soul or the sailor's bird by the communities of the strait. The belief is not held with the force of a religious conviction, but it adds a note of melancholy to the appearance of the bird upon the water, and the mariners of the strait are accustomed to speak to the petrels that gather in the wake of the vessel, as though the birds were the spirits of those who have gone before. The chronicler notes that the belief is a comfort to the mariners in the long passages of the winter months, when the sea is empty of the other birds and the petrels are the only companions of the voyage.

## Reproduction

The storm petrel breeds upon the offshore islands and the inaccessible cliffs of the strait, and the nests are placed in the burrows that the birds excavate in the peaty soil or in the crevices among the rocks. The female lays a single egg, and the egg is incubated by both birds over a period of several weeks. The chick is cared for by both parents, and the chick remains in the nest for a further period of several weeks before it is capable of flight. The young petrels are independent of the parents from the moment of the fledging, and they disperse to the open waters of the sea without any further parental care.

The colonies of the storm petrel are vulnerable to the introduction of predators, and the rats and the cats that have been brought to some of the offshore islands by the fishing communities have had a serious effect upon the breeding populations. The naturalists of the strait have worked to remove the predators from the principal breeding islands, and the populations have recovered in the districts where the eradication has been successful. The petrels are long-lived birds for creatures of their size, and a petrel that survives the hazards of the early years may live for several decades, the oldest birds returning to the same burrow year after year to breed.

## Significance

The storm petrel is the most characteristic of the seabirds of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), and its presence upon the water is a familiar feature of the maritime landscape of the northern waters. The bird is a companion of the mariner and a symbol of the sea itself, and the lore that surrounds the petrel is a measure of the close attention that the maritime communities pay to the life of the waters that sustain them. The chronicler notes that the bird endures, in the waters of the sea and in the traditions of the strait, as a reminder that the sea is not empty of life, and that even the most familiar of the voyages carries the mariner into a country that is inhabited by creatures whose lives are ordered by the rhythms of the water and the weather.
