## Overview

The iron-backed bear is the largest of the terrestrial predators of the [Morvind Uplands](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/the-morvind-uplands), a great bear of dark pelage and remarkable strength whose range extends across the coniferous forests of the lower slopes and the valleys of the mountain interior of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). The bear takes its name from the grey patch upon the shoulders that the foresters have likened to a plate of iron, and the creature is reputed among the most dangerous of the predators of the northern continent. The chronicler notes that the iron-backed bear is held in a wary respect by the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain holds, who regard the creature as a hazard of the surface world and who take care to seal the lower entrances of their halls against the intrusion of the bear.

## Form and Appearance

The iron-backed bear is a heavily built animal, with a body that is broad and muscular and set on short and powerful legs that are capable of supporting the great weight of the mature animal. The head is large and rounded, with a broad muzzle and small eyes that are set above a powerful jaw that is armed with the heavy molars and the great canines that are the bear's principal weapons. The claws of the forepaw are long and curved, and they are capable of delivering a blow that has been known to strip the bark from a mature fir with a single stroke.

The coat of the iron-backed bear is its most distinctive feature. The pelage is a dark brown, almost black in the mature animals, with a broad patch of grey or silver-tipped hair across the shoulders and the upper back that gives the bear its common name. The patch is most pronounced in the old males, and the foresters of the mountain valleys are able to judge the age of a bear by the extent and the brightness of the patch. The winter coat is dense and long, and the underwool is among the finest of the mountain furs, and the pelts of the iron-backed bear are valued by the trappers of the highland valleys and the merchants of the lowland towns.

The size of the iron-backed bear varies with the season and the abundance of the food, and a well-fed male in the autumn may attain a weight that is several times that of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) of average height. The bears are at their leanest in the early spring, when they emerge from the dens in which they have passed the winter, and they forage hungrily upon the new growth of the meadows and the carrion that the winter has left upon the surface of the snow. The bears gain weight rapidly through the summer and the autumn as they feed upon the ripening berries and the fish of the streams, and the autumn feeding is the period when the bear lays down the fat that will carry it through the winter sleep.

## Habitat and Range

The iron-backed bear inhabits the coniferous forests of the Morvind Uplands, the dense woodlands that clothe the lower slopes of the mountains and that extend down to the sheltered valleys of the interior. The bear prefers the broken country of the forest margin and the clearings, where the berries and the new growth of the forest provide the bulk of the food, and he ranges widely through the woodlands in the course of the seasons. The bear is solitary outside the brief season of the mating, and the mature animals hold ranges that they mark by the scratches of the great claws upon the trunks of the firs and by the scent that they leave upon the prominent rocks.

The dens of the iron-backed bear are excavated beneath the roots of the great firs or in the natural caves of the broken country, and the bear retires to the den in the late autumn when the first snow lies upon the high country. The winter sleep is not a true hibernation, for the bear may emerge from the den during the warmer days of the winter to forage briefly upon the snow, but the bear remains within the den for the greater part of the winter months, drawing upon the fat that has been laid down during the autumn feeding. The cubs are born within the den in the late winter, and they remain with the mother through the spring and the summer of their first year before they disperse to find their own ranges.

## Diet and Behaviour

The iron-backed bear is an omnivore, and the diet varies with the season and the availability of the food. In the spring the bear feeds upon the new growth of the meadows and the carrion that the winter has left exposed, and the bear is known to dig for the roots and the tubers of the forest floor with the long claws of the forepaw. In the summer the bear takes the berries that ripen in the clearings and the insects that are found beneath the bark of the fallen trees. In the autumn the bear feeds voraciously upon the ripening berries and the nuts of the forest, and the bear also takes the fish that ascend the streams to spawn, wading into the shallows to seize the fish with a stroke of the great paw.

The bear is not ordinarily aggressive toward [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), and a bear that is undisturbed will generally avoid the encounter with a traveller or a forester, but a bear with cubs or a bear that has been surprised at close quarters is a creature of extreme danger, and the mountain communities take care to give the bears a wide berth during the season when the cubs are at heel. The bear is also a hazard to the livestock of the valley farms, and the shepherds of the highland valleys must keep their flocks within the protection of the dogs and the watchers, for a bear that has found a flock will take several sheep in the course of a single night. The bear is a capable hunter of the deer and the elk of the mountain forests, and the mature males are known to take a yearling deer with a sudden rush from the cover of the underbrush.

## The Bear and the Dwarven Holds

The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Holds of Stoneheim](/content/government/national/holds-of-stoneheim) and the [Deep Realm of Karhold](/content/government/national/deep-realm-of-karhold) have a long acquaintance with the iron-backed bear, and the dwarven traditions hold that the bear is the most dangerous of the surface predators that venture near the dwarven halls. The dwarves do not hunt the bear except in the case of a creature that has taken to killing livestock or that has entered a dwarven hall, and the customary law of the holds forbids the taking of a bear that is not actively engaged in such depredation. The dwarves take a number of precautions to keep the bears from the vicinity of the halls, and the lower entrances are sealed with iron grilles that are designed to exclude the bear, and the waste from the kitchens is disposed of in a manner that does not attract the creature to the immediate vicinity of the gates.

The hunting of the iron-backed bear is a recognised pursuit of the [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans) of the mountain valleys, and the trappers of the highland communities take a small number of bears each year under licence from the councils. The hunt is conducted with the spear or with the heavy bow, and the hunter who engages a bear must have the skill and the courage to bring the creature down before it can close the distance, for the charge of the bear is a thing of terrifying speed and the blow of the great paw is sufficient to break the bones of a horse. The bear is accounted the most dangerous of the mountain game, and the hunter who has taken a bear is honoured by his community with a title that is second only to that of the hunter who has taken the snow cat.

## Significance

The iron-backed bear is the foremost of the terrestrial predators of the Morvind Uplands, and its presence in the mountain forests is a measure of the health of the woodland ecosystem. The bear is the predator that keeps the populations of the deer and the elk in balance with the carrying capacity of the forest, and the bear's foraging upon the berries and the insects is a factor in the maintenance of the diversity of the forest floor. The dwarves of the mountain holds regard the bear with a wary respect, and the precautions that they take against the intrusion of the bear are among the most practical of the surface traditions of the dwarven communities. The chronicler notes that the bear endures, in the forests of the lower slopes and in the lore of the mountain peoples, as a symbol of the wildness that persists in the country of the dwarven halls, and the creature is a reminder that the surface of the mountain is not wholly subject to the order of the holds that lie beneath.
