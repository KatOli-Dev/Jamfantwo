## The Flame-Plumed Bird of the High Mountains

The phoenix is a large bird of the high mountains and volcanic country, found across the highest ranges of the three continents. Naturalists classify it in the order Avifauna, family Phoenicidae. It requires high cliffs of volcanic peaks and the shelter of craters and warm caves; it is found in greatest numbers in the highest ranges of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and in the volcanic peaks of the eastern continent.

## Gold and Crimson Feathers

The phoenix has a body set upon two powerful legs and large wings. The plumage is gold shading to deep crimson at the tips of the wings and tail; the feathers retain a measure of warmth long after being shed, a property naturalists have not been able to explain. The head is set upon a long neck with a hooked beak; the eyes are large and dark. The tail is composed of feathers longer than the body, carried in a train trailing behind in flight. The wingspan of a mature bird is greater than that of the [stormwing roc](/content/species/fauna/stormwing-roc), though the body is lighter in proportion to the wings.

## Volcanic Peaks and Warm Caves

The phoenix inhabits the high mountains and volcanic country of the three continents, with the largest populations in the highest ranges of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar) and the volcanic peaks of the eastern continent, and a smaller population in the southern highlands of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) above the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh). It requires high cliffs of volcanic peaks and the shelter of craters and warm caves; it is not found in open country or dense forest. The nest is typically situated upon a ledge warmed by the vapours of volcanic country; it is a substantial structure of sticks and shed feathers of the parent birds built up over many seasons of use.

The phoenix is solitary outside the brief breeding season; mature birds hold ranges marked by the song the male utters at dawn and dusk. The range may extend for many miles through the high country. It is most active in daylight hours, circling on updraughts above volcanic peaks.

## Hunter of the High Country

The phoenix is a bird of considerable appetite that feeds upon a wide range of prey according to opportunity. The principal prey is smaller creatures of the high country, including mountain hares and marmots of the alpine meadows; a phoenix will take several such creatures in the course of a day. It is also a predator of birds of the high country, taking chicks of cliff-nesting birds when the nests are unguarded. The phoenix does not ordinarily attack [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans), but accounts of the high country include incidents in which a phoenix has defended a nest with a ferocity the mountain communities have learned to respect.

The hunting technique is to quarter the high country at a height sufficient for the keen eyes to detect movement, then descend in a swift powerful stoop. Prey is seized in the talons of the great feet and borne to a sheltered spot upon the cliff to be consumed, torn by the hooked beak. The phoenix is capable of long sustained flight and will range many miles from the nest in a day's hunting. It is also an active forager upon the ground, walking the alpine meadows in search of insects and small creatures found among the rocks.

## Renewal from Ashes

The phoenix is the subject of extensive legendary traditions across all three continents. The [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves) of the high country consider the phoenix the eldest of the birds and accord it high status. The [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) do not hunt it, sealing the upper passages of their halls against intrusion.

The most widely known tradition concerns the renewal of the bird from the ashes of its own pyre. Accounts describe how a phoenix at the end of its life builds a nest of sweet-scented woods and sets it alight, being consumed in the flames while a young phoenix rises from the ashes. Naturalists have debated this claim for centuries without reaching consensus, but high-country chroniclers agree that the bird's natural lifespan exceeds that of any other known creature, with confirmed records of individual birds observed over spans exceeding two hundred years.

## Symbol of Bright Persistence

The phoenix regulates populations of mountain hares, marmots, and cliff-nesting birds in high-country ecosystems. Loss of phoenixes from any district leads to measurable increases in small mammal populations and consequent overgrazing of alpine meadows. The bird is protected by local accord across all three continents; the taking of a phoenix or its eggs carries severe penalties in every highland community's customary law. In the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), the penalty for killing a phoenix is permanent exile from the hold, while among the Aethelaran highland communities a fine of five hundred silvers is standard.

The current population is estimated at fewer than one hundred mature birds across the entire known world. Nests are counted every five years by the High Mountain Survey, a cooperative effort of elven wardens and dwarven cartographers established in 4600. The last census, conducted in 4915, recorded forty-three active nests across the three continents: eighteen in the Aethelaran ranges, fifteen in the eastern volcanic peaks, and ten in the Sahrani highlands. A nest is considered active if it has been occupied in at least one of the two most recent survey periods. The survey also records feather shed, which is collected by licensed gatherers and sold through authorised markets at prices ranging from twenty to fifty silvers per feather, depending on quality and colour intensity. Phoenix feather is used in the manufacture of ceremonial regalia, particularly the coronation mantles of the high kings of [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), which incorporate between twelve and twenty feathers in an arrangement prescribed by the Coronation Ordinance of 3800. Powdered phoenix feather, mixed with linseed oil, produces a gold-crimson pigment used in the illuminated manuscripts of the Cloisters of Reading, valued for its persistent warm tone that does not fade even after many centuries of continuous exposure. The pigment, known commercially as phoenix crimson, is produced in quantities of less than two ounces annually.
