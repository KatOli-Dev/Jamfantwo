## The Long-Bodied Predator of the Strait

The [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) reef serpent is the largest of the marine reptiles of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), a long-bodied predator of the warm coastal waters that inhabit the reefs and submerged ridges of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean). The serpent is held in wary respect by the maritime communities of the strait, who regard it as the foremost predator of the coastal waters; fishermen take care to read the signs of its presence before they set their nets upon the reefs. The serpent is not a creature of the open ocean, for it prefers the broken country of the reefs and the straits, and it is rarely encountered by vessels that ply the deep waters of the Auric.

## Sinuous Body and Backward-Curving Teeth

The [Velden](/content/location/settlement/city/velden) reef serpent is a long and slender reptile, with a body that may exceed the length of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) span by several times, though the great majority encountered by fishermen are of more modest size. The body is sinuous and muscular, adapted to the sinuous motion that is its characteristic mode of swimming; the tail is flattened from side to side to form a vertical fin that is the principal organ of propulsion. The skin is covered in small scales that are smooth to the touch and shed water readily; the colour varies from the dark green of adults to the lighter olive of the young.

The head is narrow and elongated, with a jaw set with rows of sharp backward-curving teeth suited to seizing and holding slippery prey. The eyes are small and adapted to the moderate light of coastal waters; the serpent is reputed to detect the movement of a fish at considerable distance in the clear water of the strait. The nostrils are set high upon the snout and can be closed when the serpent dives; it is capable of remaining submerged for long periods, drawing upon air held in the expanded lung. The serpent is not venomous; the danger it presents to the swimmer or diver is from the great jaws and muscular coils rather than from any poison.

## Warm Reefs and Submerged Ridges

The reef serpent inhabits the coastal waters of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean), where the warm currents support a rich fauna of fish and smaller marine creatures upon which it feeds. The serpent is most numerous in the vicinity of reefs and submerged ridges, where the broken country provides cover for ambush and where prey is concentrated by currents. It does not range into the colder waters of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) and is seldom encountered outside the strait and adjacent coast, though an occasional individual is reported from the harbours of the eastern Aethelaran coast.

The serpent is a creature of the warm season; it is most active in late spring and summer when the waters of the strait are at their warmest and prey is most abundant. In winter it retires to deeper water beyond the reefs, where it lies in a torpor upon the bottom and draws upon the fat laid down during the active months. The serpent does not migrate in the manner of great fishes of the open ocean; the populations of the strait are largely resident, individuals holding the same range through the seasons and the years.

## Ambush Among the Reefs

The principal prey is fish of the coastal waters; the serpent is an ambush predator that lies in wait among the rocks of the reef and submerged ridges. It is coloured in a manner that blends with weed and shadows of the reef and remains motionless for long periods, waiting for the approach of a fish of suitable size. The strike is sudden; the prey is seized in the jaws and held by the backward-curving teeth, and the serpent retires to a sheltered spot to consume the catch at leisure. It takes the larger fish of the reef, including grouper and snapper; smaller individuals take squid and octopus that haunt the rocky bottom.

The serpent does not ordinarily attack [humans](/content/species/sapient/humans); divers of the pearl beds of the strait are able to work the reefs in safety except in rare circumstances when a serpent is provoked or when the water is too warm and the serpent is unusually active. Fishermen regard the serpent as a competitor for the fish of the reef and take every opportunity to drive it from the vicinity of their nets, though they are careful not to engage a large adult at close quarters. The serpent has been known to take a dog swimming from a fishing boat; there are records of attacks upon bathers in shallow water, though such attacks are rare and usually attributable to a serpent that has been provoked or that has mistaken the swimmer for a seal.

## Eggs Buried in Coastal Sand

The female comes ashore to lay eggs upon the sandy beaches of the strait, selecting a site above the high-water line where the sand is warm and the sun will provide the heat required for incubation. She excavates a hollow in the sand with her hind flippers and lays a clutch of several dozen eggs, covering them with sand before returning to the water.

The eggs are left to incubate in the warmth of the sand; the young serpents hatch after a period of several weeks, the timing varying with the temperature of the sand and conditions of the season. The young make their way to the water as soon as they hatch and are independent from the moment of hatching. Mortality among the young is high, for eggs and hatchlings are preyed upon by a number of coastal creatures, including crabs and gulls; only a small fraction survives to maturity. The gathering of the eggs is regulated by the customs of coastal villages; the taking of eggs is permitted only during a brief season and only in quantities the community may require.

## Lore, Pearlers, and Practical Hazard

The communities of the strait have a long acquaintance with the reef serpent; the lore of the fishermen includes a substantial body of knowledge of its habits and moods. Fishermen read signs of its presence in the behaviour of fish and birds; a surface of unusually calm water upon the reef or a sudden scattering of gulls may indicate a serpent lying in wait beneath. Fishing boats keep a distance from any serpent sighted; crews carry long poles with which to fend off one that approaches too closely.

Pearl divers of the strait are the most exposed of the maritime communities to the danger; traditions of the diving guilds include practices designed to reduce risk. Divers work in pairs, with one in the water while the other remains upon the boat, and carry a sharp knife with which they may cut themselves free from the coils of a serpent that has seized them. The guilds maintain careful records of sightings; captains of diving boats consult the records before setting out. The serpent is not held in superstition; the strait peoples are a practical folk, and the creature is regarded as a hazard to be managed rather than a spirit to be propitiated.

## Foremost Predator of the Strait

The Velden reef serpent is the foremost predator of the coastal waters of the strait; its presence in the reef country is a measure of the health of the marine ecosystem. It is the predator that keeps the populations of reef fish in balance with the productivity of the waters; the loss of the serpent from any district would be followed by an overpopulation of prey species that would in time upset the order of the reef. The serpent is not held in particular reverence by the coastal communities, but it is respected as a creature of the waters, and the customary practices that have grown up around its management are among the most practical of the maritime traditions of the strait. The serpent endures, as it has endured for centuries, in the warm waters of the strait and the neighbouring reaches of the Auric; the fishermen of the coastal villages continue to read the signs of its presence and to give it the wide berth that its size and power demand.
