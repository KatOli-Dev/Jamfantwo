## The Small Dark Seabird of the Stormfall Sea

The storm petrel is the most numerous of the seabirds of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea), a small dark bird whose presence upon the waters is a familiar sight to the mariners of the strait and adjacent coasts. The petrel takes its name from the belief of sailors that the bird appears upon the water in advance of storms; this belief has been the subject of considerable learned debate but retains its hold upon the lore of maritime communities. The storm petrel is one of the most extensively studied seabirds of the northern waters; naturalists of the strait towns have contributed substantially to the understanding of the bird's life and habits.

## Sooty Plumage and Pattering Flight

The storm petrel is a small bird, with a body roughly the size of a [human](/content/species/sapient/humans) hand and a wingspan several times the length of the body. The plumage is a dark sooty brown across the back, wings, and head, with a paler grey or white upon the underparts and a distinctive white patch upon the rump visible when the bird is in flight. The bill is short and hooked, suited to taking small creatures that constitute the bird's prey; the legs are short and slender with webbed feet adapted to swimming.

The flight is the most distinctive feature and the source of the bird's reputation as a herald of weather. The bird flies low over the water with a fluttering and erratic motion, the wings beating rapidly and the body seeming to dance upon the surface of the waves; the flight is unlike that of any other seabird of the [Stormfall](/content/location/natural/continent/stormfall). The feet are often seen to patter upon the surface as it flies, a behaviour naturalists have interpreted as a means of detecting small creatures taken from the surface; the pattering is one of the characteristic sounds of the sea in the vicinity of a flock of petrels.

## Open Water and Offshore Colonies

The storm petrel is found upon the waters of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea) and the adjacent reaches of the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden), with the greatest concentration in the vicinity of rocky islands and cliffs of the strait. The bird breeds upon offshore islands and inaccessible cliffs of the mainland, where nests are placed in burrows excavated in peaty soil or in crevices among the rocks; colonies upon larger islands may number many thousands of pairs. The birds are present upon the breeding grounds from early spring until late autumn; they disperse to open waters for the winter months.

The bird is most numerous in the vicinity of fishing grounds and upwellings that bring small creatures to the surface; flocks gathering upon the water in the wake of a fishing vessel may number many hundreds. The petrel is a bird of the open sea rather than the coast; the birds do not ordinarily approach the shore except at breeding colonies, and maritime communities regard the appearance of the petrel upon the water as a sign that the vessel is well out to sea.

## Surface Feeding and Ship-Following

The storm petrel feeds upon small creatures of the surface waters, including tiny shelled creatures and fish fry brought to the surface by currents and upwellings. The bird takes prey from the surface as it flies, the bill snapping upon small creatures with rapid precise motion; it is also capable of pattering upon the surface with the feet to detect prey lying just beneath. The bird is an opportunistic feeder; flocks gather upon any spot where prey is concentrated, including slicks of oil and floating weed that mark the convergence of currents.

The bird is also a follower of ships; flocks gathering in the wake of a vessel may persist for as long as the vessel is underway, the birds feeding upon small creatures disturbed by the passage of the hull and upon refuse discharged from the galley. The presence of petrels in the wake is a familiar sight to mariners; the birds are a common sight on long passages. The bird does not dive beneath the surface in pursuit of prey, for its plumage is not adapted to submersion; it relies upon surface-feeding for the entirety of its sustenance.

## Herald of Weather and Souls of Sailors

The storm petrel has long been associated in the lore of maritime communities with the weather that moves across the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea); the bird is popularly believed to appear upon the water in advance of storms. Naturalists have offered several explanations, the most plausible of which holds that the bird is driven to the surface by disturbance of the water preceding the storm and that its appearance is therefore a consequence rather than a cause of the coming weather. Mariners of the strait are not disposed to abandon the older belief; the sight of petrels gathering upon the water is still accounted a reliable sign that the weather is about to change.

The petrel is also associated in maritime lore with the souls of sailors who have died upon the sea; it is sometimes called the storm soul or the sailor's bird by communities of the strait. The belief is not held with the force of religious conviction, but it persists in the lore of the strait communities. Mariners of the strait are accustomed to speak to the petrels that gather in the wake of the vessel, as though the birds were the spirits of those who have gone before. The practice is recorded in maritime accounts as early as the seventh century AR, with the earliest written reference appearing in the ship-log of the whaler Red Wake in 623 AR.

## Burrows, Eggs, and Colony Life

The storm petrel breeds upon offshore islands and inaccessible cliffs of the strait; nests are placed in burrows excavated in peaty soil or in crevices among the rocks. The female lays a single egg, incubated by both birds over a period of several weeks. The chick is cared for by both parents and remains in the nest for a further period of several weeks before it is capable of flight. Young petrels are independent of the parents from the moment of fledging; they disperse to open waters without further parental care.

Colonies are vulnerable to the introduction of predators; rats and cats brought to some offshore islands by fishing communities have had a serious effect upon breeding populations. Naturalists of the strait have worked to remove predators from principal breeding islands; populations have recovered in districts where eradication has been successful. Petrels are long-lived birds for creatures of their size; one that survives the hazards of early years may live for several decades, the oldest returning to the same burrow year after year to breed.

## The Mariner's Companion

The storm petrel is the most numerous of the seabirds of the [Stormfall Sea](/content/location/natural/feature/stormfall-sea); surveys of the largest breeding colony on Skell Isle, conducted by naturalists of the Port Velden Academy between 840 and 850 AR, recorded approximately sixty thousand breeding pairs. The bird is a common sight on the waters of the strait and the adjacent sea; the lore that surrounds the petrel reflects the attention the maritime communities pay to the life of the waters. Colony counts on monitored islands have remained stable across the forty-year period of record.
