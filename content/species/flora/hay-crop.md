The hay meadows that are found in the river valleys of Aethelar are the most productive of the pasture lands, and the hay that is cut from these meadows is the foundation of the winter feeding of the livestock.

## The Hay Meadows of the River Valleys

The meadows are flooded by the rivers in the spring, and the silt that is deposited by the flood water enriches the soil and promotes the growth of the grasses and the legumes.

The hay is cut in the late spring or the early summer, when the grasses have reached the peak of the growth and the nutrient content of the plants is at the highest. The grass is cut with the scythes and left to dry in the field, and the dried hay is turned and stacked before it is carried to the barns for the storage.

The quality of the hay depends upon the timing of the cutting and the care that is taken in the drying and the storage of the hay. The hay that is cut too late in the season is coarse and less nutritious, and the hay that is not properly dried before it is stored will rot and become mouldy.

## The Flood Enrichment of the Meadows

The hay meadows of the river valleys are the beneficiaries of the natural fertilisation that the spring floods provide, the rivers that overflow their banks depositing the silt and the organic matter that the flood water carries from the upstream regions. The flood enrichment is the process that makes the river valley meadows the most productive of the pasture lands, the soil being renewed each year with the nutrients that the flood water brings, and the hay that is cut from the flood-enriched meadows is richer and more nutritious than the hay that is cut from the upland pastures that do not receive the flood water.

The management of the flood meadows is a practice that has been developed over the centuries by the farmers of the river valleys, the meadows being prepared in the winter for the flooding that will arrive in the spring. The ditches and the channels that control the flow of the flood water are maintained by the farmers who share the rights to the meadow, and the gates and the sluices that regulate the flooding are opened and closed at the times that the customs of the meadow prescribe. The flooding of the meadows is a community undertaking, the farmers who share the meadow working together to manage the water and to ensure that the enrichment is distributed evenly across the land.

## The Grasses and Legumes of the Meadow

The hay meadows of the river valleys are composed of the mixture of the grasses and the legumes that produce the best hay, the composition of the sward being the result of the centuries of the management and the selection that the farmers have practiced. The grasses that dominate the meadow are the timothy and the meadow foxtail and the smooth-stalked meadow grass, the grasses that produce the bulk and the fibre that the livestock need in the winter diet. The legumes that are found in the meadow are the clover and the vetch and the bird's-foot trefoil, the legumes that fix the nitrogen from the air and that provide the protein that enriches the hay and improves the quality of the feed.

The [clover](/content/species/flora/clover) is the most valuable of the legumes in the hay meadow, the plant being rich in the protein and the minerals that the livestock need, and the presence of the clover in the hay is the sign of the good quality that the buyers recognise. The farmers who manage the hay meadows encourage the growth of the clover by the practices that promote the nitrogen fixation and the spread of the legume, and the meadows that have a high proportion of the clover are the meadows that command the highest prices in the hay market. The management of the clover in the meadow is a skill that is passed from the father to the son in the farming families of the river valleys, and the knowledge of the clover and the conditions that it requires is among the most valued of the agricultural traditions.

## The Making of the Hay

The making of the hay is the critical operation of the summer, the work being timed to coincide with the period of the settled weather that the late spring and the early summer bring. The grass is cut with the scythes in the early morning, the dew having dried from the grass but the heat of the day not yet being at its peak, and the cut grass is left in the swaths that fall where the scythe has laid them. The grass is turned by the forks and the tedders that shake the hay and expose the wet underside to the sun and the wind, the turning being repeated until the hay is dry enough to be stacked without the risk of the mould or the spoilage.

The drying of the hay is the process that requires the most skill and the most attention, the hay that is too dry losing the leaves that contain the most nutrition, and the hay that is too wet being prone to the mould that would make it unsafe for the livestock to eat. The farmer who is making the hay tests the moisture by the feel of the hay in the hand, the experienced farmer knowing the exact stage of the dryness that is required for the stacking. The weather is the factor that the farmer cannot control, and the haymaking season is the time of the anxiety and the urgency, the farmer who is caught by the rain losing the quality and the value of the hay that the wet weather damages.

## Storage and the Hay Barns

The storage of the hay is the final stage of the haymaking, the dried hay being carried to the barns and the stacks that protect it from the rain and the snow of the winter. The hay barns are the structures that are built for the purpose of the hay storage, the barns being open on the sides to allow the ventilation that prevents the heating and the spoilage that would occur in the hay that is stored without the circulation of the air. The hay is stacked in the barns in the neat layers that the experienced stacker builds, the stack being arranged to shed the water and to remain stable through the winter.

The hay that is stacked in the fields is built into the ricks and the cocks that are thatched with the straw or the reeds that protect the hay from the rain, the field stacks being the method of the storage that is used when the hay barn is not available or when the distance from the meadow to the barn is too great. The building of the hay rick is a skilled craft, the rick being shaped to shed the water and the thatch being applied in the manner that ensures the protection of the hay through the winter. The hay ricks are a characteristic feature of the landscape of the river valleys in the autumn and the winter, the ricks being the visible sign of the harvest that has been gathered and the provision that has been made for the winter months.

## The Hay Trade and the Winter Economy

The hay that is produced in the river valleys is not only consumed on the farms where it is grown, the surplus being traded to the farmers and the horse-keepers who do not have the meadow land to produce their own hay. The hay trade is a significant component of the [agriculture](/content/economy/agriculture) of the river valleys, the hay being sold by the load and the stack to the buyers who come from the upland farms and the cities where the [horses](/content/species/fauna/horse) and the [cattle](/content/species/fauna/cattle) are kept. The price of the hay varies with the season and the yield, the wet summers that produce the poor hay driving the prices up and the good seasons that produce the abundant hay driving the prices down.

The [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the hay merchants regulate the trade in the hay that is sold in the cities, the guilds setting the standards of the quality and the weights and the measures that are used in the trade. The hay that is sold in the cities is used by the stable-keepers and the innkeepers who maintain the [horses](/content/species/fauna/horse) for the travellers and the post, and the quality of the hay that is available in the city stables is a factor in the speed and the reliability of the communication and the transport that the horses provide. The hay crop is the foundation of the winter economy of the northern settlements, and the prosperity of the river valley farmers who produce the hay is the prosperity that supports the trade and the industry of the wider world.
