## The Grasses of the Sunfire Steppe

The [Sunfire Steppe](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sunfire-steppe) is a region of the eastern [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) where the climate is intermediate between the desert and the savanna, and the plants of the steppe are adapted to the seasonal rainfall that is more abundant than the rainfall of the desert interior but less reliable than the rainfall of the coastal strip.

The grasses of the [Sunfire Steppe](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sunfire-steppe) are the dominant plants of the region, and the species that grow in the steppe are the same species that grow in the [Great Grass Plains](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/great-grass-plains) of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), adapted to the warmer conditions of the southern continent. The grasses of the steppe support the herds of the antelope and the other grazing animals that are the prey of the predators of the region, and the grasses are also the principal food of the herds of the domesticated animals that the steppe peoples keep.

## The Acacia Trees of the Steppe

The acacia trees of the [Sunfire Steppe](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sunfire-steppe) are the same species as the acacias of the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh), but the trees of the steppe are larger and more numerous than the trees of the desert, for the rainfall of the steppe is sufficient to support the growth of the trees in the regions where the soil is deep enough to retain the moisture. The [acacia](/content/species/flora/acacia) provides the shade that the herds seek in the heat of the day, and the pods of the acacia are a food that the goats and the camels of the steppe peoples consume in the dry season when the grasses have dried.

## Physical Characteristics

The sunfire grasses are the warm-season species that grow in the tussocks and the spreading mats that cover the soil of the steppe, the roots of the grasses extending deep into the earth to reach the moisture that remains in the soil after the seasonal rains. The blades of the sunfire grasses are narrow and tough, the texture of the leaves being adapted to the grazing that the herds inflict, and the colour of the grasses shifting from the green of the growing season to the golden-brown of the dry season that gives the steppe its name. The seed heads of the grasses are produced in the late summer, the seeds being small and hard and dispersed by the wind that sweeps across the open ground of the steppe.

The grasses of the steppe include both the perennial species that persist from year to year through the drought-resistant root systems and the annual species that germinate with the seasonal rains and complete the cycle of the growth and the seed production before the dry season returns. The perennial grasses are the foundation of the pasture, the deep roots holding the soil against the erosion that the wind and the occasional torrential rains would cause, and the annual grasses provide the flush of the rapid growth that sustains the herds in the weeks following the rains.

## Habitat and Climate

The [Sunfire Steppe](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sunfire-steppe) occupies the transitional zone between the [Kharesh](/content/location/natural/feature/kharesh) desert and the [Sahrani Coastal Strip](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sahrani-coastal-strip), the climate of the steppe being characterised by the seasonal rainfall that arrives in the brief but intense storms of the spring and the autumn. The rainfall of the steppe is sufficient to support the growth of the grasses and the scattered acacia trees but insufficient for the cultivation of the crops that require the reliable moisture, and the agriculture of the steppe peoples is limited to the grazing of the herds rather than the planting of the fields.

The soils of the steppe are a thin layer of the sandy loam that overlies the gravel and the bedrock of the desert margin, the soil being richer in the organic matter than the soils of the desert interior but poorer than the soils of the coastal strip. The grasses of the steppe contribute the organic matter to the soil through the decay of the roots and the litter of the dead leaves, and the soil of the steppe is built and maintained by the grasses that grow in it.

## Grazing and Pastoralism

The sunfire grasses are the principal pasture of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) and the other steppe peoples who keep the herds of the goats, the camels, and the [cattle](/content/species/fauna/cattle) that are the basis of the pastoral economy of the region. The herds are moved across the steppe in the seasonal migrations that the pastoral peoples follow, the movement of the herds being timed to the growth of the grasses and the availability of the water that the steppe provides. The grazing of the herds maintains the grasses in the condition that favours the regrowth, and the removal of the old and the dry material by the grazing animals stimulates the production of the new growth when the rains return.

The management of the grazing is governed by the traditions and the agreements that the steppe peoples have developed over the centuries of the pastoral use of the land. The [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) coordinates the movement of the herds among the member tribes, and the access to the grazing lands is allocated according to the seasonal conditions and the needs of the communities. The overgrazing of the steppe is prevented by the rotation of the herds and the periodic rest of the pasture, and the steppe peoples understand that the sustainability of the grasses is the foundation of the economy that the herds support.

## Seasonal Cycle

The seasonal cycle of the sunfire grasses begins with the arrival of the spring rains that break the drought of the dry season, the moisture stimulating the germination of the annual seeds and the regrowth of the perennial tussocks. The grasses grow rapidly in the weeks following the rains, the warmth of the steppe and the moisture in the soil providing the conditions that the grasses exploit to produce the biomass that the herds consume. The growth of the grasses slows as the soil dries and the temperatures rise, the grasses shifting from the green of the active growth to the golden colour of the dormancy that characterises the steppe in the long dry season.

The dry season is the period of the hardship for the herds and the pastoral peoples, the grasses having lost the nutritional value that the green growth provides, and the herds relying on the dry stalks and the pods of the [acacia](/content/species/flora/acacia) trees for the sustenance that the steppe yields. The return of the autumn rains brings the second flush of the growth that sustains the herds through the winter, the cycle of the two rainy seasons being the pattern that the pastoral economy of the steppe follows.

## Ecological Significance

The sunfire grasses are the foundation of the ecosystem of the [Sunfire Steppe](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sunfire-steppe), the grasses supporting the herbivores that are the prey of the predators of the region, and the grasses also maintaining the soil that the acacia trees and the other plants of the steppe require. The [Red Deer](/content/species/fauna/red-deer) and the other wild grazers of the steppe depend on the grasses for the food that sustains the wild populations, and the predators that hunt the grazers are dependent on the grasses through the chain of the relationships that the ecosystem comprises.

The grasses of the steppe are also the habitat of the smaller animals that live among the tussocks and the roots, the [hare and rabbit](/content/species/fauna/hare-and-rabbit) and the [marmot](/content/species/fauna/marmot-and-pika) finding the shelter and the food in the grasses that cover the ground. The birds of the steppe, the [golden eagle](/content/species/fauna/golden-eagle) and the [peregrine falcon](/content/species/fauna/peregrine-falcon) that hunt the smaller animals, are dependent on the grasses for the prey that the grasses support, and the grasses are the base of the ecological pyramid that the steppe sustains.

## Economic Significance

The economic significance of the sunfire grasses extends beyond the pastoral use of the pasture, for the grasses are also the source of the thatch and the fibre that the steppe peoples use in the construction of the shelters and the production of the mats and the baskets. The dry stalks of the grasses are harvested in the late summer and bundled into the thatch that covers the roofs of the dwellings, and the fibre that is extracted from the leaves is spun into the coarse yarn that is woven into the mats and the screens that the steppe peoples use in the daily life.

The [spices and dyes](/content/economy/spices-and-dyes) trade of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast is partly dependent on the grasses of the steppe, for the [saffron](/content/species/flora/saffron) and the other spices that are traded from the interior are transported on the backs of the camels that the grasses sustain. The [livestock and herding](/content/economy/livestock-and-herding) economy of the steppe is a significant component of the commerce of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), and the grasses that support the herds are the foundation of the wealth that the pastoral trade generates.
