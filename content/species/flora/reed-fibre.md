## Reed Fibre

The reed that grows in the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) is the most important of the thatching materials in the known world, and the reed is also used in the production of the mats, the baskets, and the paper that are produced in the marsh settlements.

The reed is a tall and hollow-stemmed grass that grows to a height of twelve feet or more, and the stems of the reed are harvested in the winter when the plant is dead and dry. The thatching reed of the Greywash is the finest in the known world, and the roofs that are thatched with the Greywash reed are durable and watertight, lasting for thirty years or more before they must be replaced. The reed is also used in the production of the mats that cover the floors of the houses and the baskets that are used in the transport and the storage of the goods.

The cutting of the reed in the marshes is regulated by the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the thatchers and the reed merchants, who manage the harvest of the reed to ensure that the reed beds are not depleted and that the wildlife of the marsh is not harmed.

## Physical Characteristics of the Reed

The reed is a tall and hollow-stemmed grass that grows to a height of twelve feet or more, the stems being cylindrical and smooth, and the leaves being long and narrow with the sheaths that wrap the stem at the base. The reed spreads by the rhizomes that creep through the mud of the marsh, the rhizomes sending up the new stems at intervals along their length, and the dense stands that the reed forms are the result of the vigorous growth of the rhizomes.

The reed produces the flowers in the late summer, the flowers being borne in the feathery plumes that stand above the stems, the plumes being purple at first and fading to the silver as the seeds ripen. The seeds of the reed are small and light, the seeds being dispersed by the wind and the water, and the seeds are a food source for the birds that inhabit the marshes. The stems of the reed are the part of the plant that is harvested, the stems being cut at the base and dried for the use in the thatching and the other industries.

## The Thatching of the Roofs

The thatching of the roofs is the most important of the uses of the reed, and the thatched roof is the most common form of the roof in the northern settlements where the reed is abundant. The thatching reed of the Greywash is the finest in the known world, and the roofs that are thatched with the Greywash reed are durable and watertight, lasting for thirty years or more before they must be replaced. The thatchers who work with the reed are the skilled craftsmen who are trained in the techniques of the thatching, the reed being tied in the bundles that are fixed to the roof with the sways and the spars that hold the reed in the overlapping layers that shed the water.

The thatching of the roof is a process that requires the skill and the experience of the thatcher, the reed being laid in the courses that start at the eaves and rise to the ridge, each course being secured to the roof with the sways that are twisted from the straw or the wire. The ridge of the roof is the most vulnerable part of the structure, and the ridge is finished with the elaborately patterned caps that are the signature of the thatcher who has done the work, the patterns being the tradition of the region and the mark of the craftsman.

## The Mats and the Baskets of the Marsh

The reed is also used in the production of the mats that cover the floors of the houses and the baskets that are used in the transport and the storage of the goods. The mats are woven from the split reeds that are prepared by the flattening of the stems, the split reeds being woven in the patterns that are the tradition of the marsh communities. The mats are used as the floor coverings and the sleeping mats, and the mats are also used as the screens that divide the interiors of the houses into the rooms.

The baskets that are made from the reed are the containers that are used in every household and every trade, the baskets being woven in the shapes and the sizes that are suited to the purposes for which they are intended. The baskets of the Greywash are renowned for the quality of the weaving and the durability of the construction, and the baskets are exported to the markets of the north where they are traded for the goods that the marsh communities cannot produce.

## The Harvest and the Management of the Reed Beds

The cutting of the reed in the marshes is regulated by the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the thatchers and the reed merchants, who manage the harvest of the reed to ensure that the reed beds are not depleted and that the wildlife of the marsh is not harmed. The reed is harvested in the winter when the plant is dead and dry, the stems being cut with the sickles that are the tools of the reed cutters, and the cut reed being tied in the bundles that are carried to the settlements on the flat-bottomed boats that navigate the channels of the marsh.

The management of the reed beds includes the burning of the dead growth in the spring, the burning being the practice that stimulates the growth of the new stems and prevents the invasion of the reed beds by the other plants. The burning is controlled by the marsh communities, the fires being lit in the sections of the marsh that are scheduled for the burning, and the fires being extinguished before they can spread to the areas that are to be left unburned. The burning of the reed beds is the occasion of the gathering of the communities, the burning being the work that is shared by the families that depend on the reed for their livelihood.

## The Paper of the Reed

The reed is also used in the production of the paper that is used by the scribes of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) for the production of the manuscripts, the reed being one of the sources of the fibre that is pulped and pressed into the sheets that are the writing material. The paper that is produced from the reed is coarser than the [papyrus](/content/species/flora/papyrus) that is produced in the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) interior, but the reed paper is more abundant and less expensive, and the reed paper is the material that is used for the everyday records and the correspondence that are the bulk of the work of the scribes.

The production of the reed paper involves the soaking of the reed in the water that breaks down the fibres, the soaking being followed by the pounding of the reed in the mortars that reduces the fibre to the pulp that is suspended in the water. The pulp is poured onto the screens that are lifted from the water, the water draining through the mesh and leaving the sheet of the paper that is dried on the boards that are stacked in the drying sheds. The reed paper is a brownish colour, and the surface is smoothed with the stone that is rubbed over the dried sheet to prepare it for the writing.
