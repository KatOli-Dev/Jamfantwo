## The Common Reed of the Greywash

The reed is among the most important of the aquatic plants for the economy of the Greywash region. The common reed of the Greywash is a tall grass that can grow to a height of twelve feet or more, and the stems of the reed are used for the thatching of the roofs of the buildings, for the making of the mats and the baskets, and for the production of the paper that the scribes of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) use in the production of the manuscripts.

The [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) are the largest wetland in the known world, and the reeds that grow in the marshes are central to the economy of the region. The harvest of the reeds is the principal industry of the marsh-dwelling communities, and the reed cutters work in the winter months when the water is low and the reeds have died back for the season. The cut reeds are tied into bundles that are carried to the settlements on the flat-bottomed boats that navigate the shallow channels of the marsh. The bundles of the reeds are stacked in the yards of the merchants and the thatchers, who purchase the reeds for the use in the construction and the manufacturing industries.

## The Reed and the Wildlife of the Marsh

The reed that is not harvested for the use of the human communities provides the habitat for the birds and the animals of the marsh, and the dense stands of the reeds are the nesting sites of the waterfowl and the hiding places of the fish and the amphibians that inhabit the waters of the marsh. The reed beds are the home of the reed warbler and the bittern, the birds that are the most characteristic of the avian species of the wetland, and the reed beds are also the hunting ground of the marsh harrier and the other predators that prey on the birds and the small mammals of the marsh.

## Physical Characteristics of the Reed

The common reed is a perennial grass that spreads by the rhizomes that creep through the mud of the marsh, the rhizomes being thick and sturdy and sending up the new stems at the intervals along their length. The stems of the reed are hollow and cylindrical, the walls being thin and tough, and the stems are topped by the feathery plumes that are the flowers of the plant. The leaves of the reed are long and narrow, the blades being flat and the sheaths being wrapped around the stem at the base.

The reed is a plant that is adapted to the conditions of the wetland, the roots being able to tolerate the saturation of the soil and the stems being able to withstand the submersion that occurs during the floods. The reed is also tolerant of the salinity that is found in the marshes where the fresh water and the salt water mingle, and the reed is one of the few plants that can grow in the brackish conditions of the lower marsh. The reed is a vigorous grower, the stems emerging from the rhizomes in the spring and reaching the full height by the midsummer, the growth being so rapid that the reed can outcompete the other plants that attempt to establish themselves in the marsh.

## The Thatching of the Roofs

The most important of the uses of the reed is the thatching of the roofs of the buildings, and the reed that is harvested in the [Greywash Reed Marshes](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/reed-marshes-of-the-greywash) is the most valued of the thatching materials in the known world. The roofs that are thatched with the Greywash reed are durable and watertight, lasting for thirty years or more before they must be replaced, and the thatched roof is the most common form of the roof in the northern settlements where the reed is abundant and the other roofing materials are scarce.

The thatching of the roof is a skilled trade that is practised by the thatchers who are trained in the craft, the thatcher being responsible for the selection of the reed, the preparation of the roof, and the laying of the courses that shed the water. The thatcher uses the tools that are the tradition of the craft, the legg being the crooked knife that is used to trim the reed, and the sway being the twisted wire or the straw that secures the bundles to the roof. The thatcher works from the eaves to the ridge, each course of the reed being laid over the preceding course and secured to the battens that are fixed to the roof.

## The Mats and the Baskets

The reed is also used in the production of the mats that cover the floors of the houses and the baskets that are used in the transport and the storage of the goods. The mats are woven on the looms that are set up in the houses of the marsh communities, the reed being split and dried and the split reed being woven in the patterns that are the tradition of the region. The mats are used as the floor coverings and the sleeping mats, and the mats are also used as the screens that divide the interiors of the houses.

The baskets that are made from the reed are the containers that are used in every household and every trade, the baskets being woven in the shapes and the sizes that are suited to the purposes for which they are intended. The baskets of the Greywash are renowned for the quality of the weaving and the durability of the construction, and the baskets are traded to the markets of the north where they are sold for the goods that the marsh communities require.

## The Harvest and the Management of the Reed Beds

The cutting of the reed in the marshes is the principal industry of the marsh-dwelling communities, and the reed cutters work in the winter months when the water is low and the reeds have died back for the season. The reed cutters use the sickles that are the tools of the trade, the sickles being used to cut the reed at the base and to bundle the cut reed into the sheaves that are carried to the settlements. The cut reed is dried in the yards of the merchants, the drying being the process that prepares the reed for the use in the thatching and the other industries.

The management of the reed beds is the responsibility of the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the thatchers and the reed merchants, who regulate the harvest to ensure that the reed beds are not depleted and that the wildlife of the marsh is not harmed. The management includes the burning of the dead growth in the spring, the burning being the practice that stimulates the growth of the new stems and prevents the invasion of the reed beds by the other plants. The burning is controlled by the marsh communities, the fires being lit in the sections of the marsh that are scheduled for the burning.

## The Paper of the Reed

The reed is also used in the production of the paper that is used by the scribes of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) for the production of the manuscripts. The reed paper that is produced in the marsh settlements is coarser than the [papyrus](/content/species/flora/papyrus) that is produced in the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) interior, but the reed paper is more abundant and less expensive, and the reed paper is the material that is used for the everyday records and the correspondence that are the bulk of the work of the scribes. The production of the reed paper involves the soaking of the reed in the water that breaks down the fibres, the soaking being followed by the pounding of the reed in the mortars that reduces the fibre to the pulp that is pressed into the sheets.

## The Reed in the Ecology of the Marsh

The reed plays a role in the ecology of the marsh that extends beyond the provision of the habitat for the wildlife, for the reed is the plant that stabilises the banks of the channels and the edges of the islands that are the dry land of the marsh. The roots of the reed bind the mud of the marsh and prevent the erosion that would be caused by the flow of the water and the action of the waves, and the reed beds are the structures that maintain the form of the marsh and the distribution of the land and the water that are the character of the wetland.

The reed also plays a role in the purification of the water that flows through the marsh, the reed being the plant that absorbs the nutrients and the pollutants that are carried by the water from the uplands, and the reed beds are the natural filters that clean the water before it reaches the rivers and the sea. The capacity of the reed to absorb the nutrients and the pollutants is the property that is valued by the communities that live downstream of the marsh, and the preservation of the reed beds is the concern of the authorities that are responsible for the quality of the water.
