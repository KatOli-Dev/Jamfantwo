## Olive

The olive is the characteristic fruit of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) coast, grown in groves that cover the slopes of the hills overlooking the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean).

The olive tree is long-lived and drought-tolerant, capable of surviving the dry summers of the [Sahrani Coastal Strip](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/sahrani-coastal-strip) and producing fruit from soils too shallow and stony for the cultivation of grains or vegetables. The olive groves of the [Green Crescent](/content/location/natural/ecosystem/green-crescent) are among the oldest cultivated landscapes in [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana), and the oil that is pressed from their fruit is the foundation of the Sahrani cuisine and a significant export to the northern markets.

The cultivation of the olive requires patience, for the tree does not bear fruit in commercial quantities until it has been established for at least five years, and the full production of a grove is not reached until the trees are twenty years old or more. The olive harvest begins in late autumn, when the fruit has changed from green to black and the oil content is at its highest. The olives are beaten from the branches with long poles, gathered in nets spread beneath the trees, and carried to the presses that extract the oil.

The oil of the first pressing, called virgin oil, is the finest and commands the highest price in the markets of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra). The oil of the second pressing is used for cooking, and the residue of the pressing is used as fuel for the lamps that light the houses of the coastal settlements. The trade in olive oil is regulated by the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the oil merchants, who maintain the standards of quality that ensure the reputation of Sahrani oil in the markets of the north.

## Physical Characteristics of the Olive Tree

The olive tree is an evergreen that grows to a height of twenty to thirty feet in the cultivated groves, though the wild olives of the uncultivated slopes may reach forty feet or more. The trunk of the olive is gnarled and twisted, the bark grey and furrowed, and the wood beneath the bark is hard and dense, with a grain that is prized by the woodworkers for the carving of bowls and the turning of small implements. The leaves of the olive are narrow and silvery-green, adapted to the dry conditions of the coast by a waxy coating that reduces the loss of water through the surface of the leaf, and the leaves persist on the tree for two or three years before they are shed.

The olive produces small white flowers in the late spring, the flowers being borne in clusters on the branches that grew in the preceding year. The flowers are wind-pollinated, and the olive does not require the presence of bees or other insects for the production of the fruit, though the pollination is more reliable in the groves where the trees are planted in rows that allow the wind to carry the pollen from tree to tree. The fruit of the olive is a drupe, a fleshy fruit with a single stone, and the fruit ripens from green to black over the course of the autumn months.

## Cultivation and Harvest

The cultivation of the olive is a long-term investment that requires the patience and the foresight of the grower, for the tree does not begin to bear fruit until the fifth or sixth year after planting, and the full production of a grove is not achieved until the trees are twenty years old or more. The olive is propagated from the cuttings of the selected varieties, the cuttings being taken from the trees that produce the highest quality of fruit and the highest yield of oil. The cuttings are rooted in the nurseries and transplanted to the groves in the winter, when the rains provide the moisture that the young trees need to establish their roots in the stony soils of the coast.

The olive groves are planted in rows that follow the contours of the hills, the trees spaced at intervals of twenty feet or more to allow the roots to spread and the branches to catch the sun. The soil between the rows is ploughed in the spring to control the weeds and to break the crust that forms on the surface of the soil during the dry summer. The olive does not require irrigation in the established groves, for the roots of the tree are deep and they reach the moisture that lies beneath the dry surface of the soil, but the young trees must be watered in the first years after planting until the roots have grown deep enough to sustain the tree through the summer drought.

The harvest of the olive begins in the late autumn, when the fruit has changed from green to black and the oil content is at its highest. The olives are beaten from the branches with long poles, the beaters working in pairs to shake the branches and dislodge the fruit, and the olives are gathered in the nets that are spread beneath the trees. The harvest is a labour-intensive operation that employs the whole community, and the workers are paid in a share of the oil that they produce, a practice that distributes the product of the groves among the families that depend on the harvest for their livelihood.

## The Pressing of the Oil

The olives that are gathered in the harvest are carried to the presses that are located in the settlements of the coast, and the pressing of the oil is a process that must be completed within a few days of the harvest to prevent the deterioration of the fruit. The olives are first crushed by the stone wheels that turn in the basins, the wheels being driven by the animals or by the water mills that are built on the streams of the coast. The crushed olives are then pressed in the screw presses or the beam presses, and the oil that flows from the press is collected in the jars that are stored in the cellars of the merchants.

The oil of the first pressing, called the virgin oil, is the finest and commands the highest price in the markets of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra). The oil of the second pressing is used for cooking, and the residue of the pressing is used as fuel for the lamps that light the houses of the coastal settlements. The trade in olive oil is regulated by the [guilds](/content/economy/guilds) of the oil merchants, who maintain the standards of quality that ensure the reputation of Sahrani oil in the markets of the north.

## Culinary Uses and Cultural Significance

The olive oil is the foundation of the [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) cuisine, the oil being used in the preparation of every dish from the simple bread that is dipped in the oil to the elaborate stews that are simmered for hours in the clay pots of the coastal kitchens. The oil is used as a cooking medium, as a flavouring, and as a preservative, and the olives themselves are cured in brine or in the oil and eaten as a relish that accompanies the meals of the region.

The olive also has a religious and a symbolic significance in the cultures of the Sahrani coast. The olive branch is a symbol of peace and of reconciliation, and the oil of the olive is used in the anointing of the priests and the sovereigns of the Sahrani states. The lamps that burn in the temples of the coast are fuelled by the olive oil, and the light of the olive lamp is considered the purest of the lights that are offered to the gods. The olive tree itself is a symbol of the longevity and the endurance of the communities that have cultivated the groves for generations, and the oldest trees in the groves are venerated as the witnesses of the history of the settlements.

## Trade and Export

The trade in olive oil is one of the most important components of the Sahrani economy, and the oil that is produced in the groves of the coast is exported to every region of the known world. The oil is carried in the amphorae that are produced in the potteries of the coast, the amphorae being shaped to stack in the holds of the ships and to be carried on the backs of the pack animals that travel the caravan routes to the interior. The olive oil that is exported to the north is traded for the timber, the iron, and the wool that the Sahrani coast cannot produce, and the balance of the trade is favourable to the producers of the oil.

The oil merchants of [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) and [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) are among the wealthiest of the merchant classes of the Sahrani states, and the guilds that regulate the trade maintain warehouses and trading houses in every city of the coast and the interior. The standards of the quality that the guilds enforce include the grading of the oil by the pressing and by the variety of the olive, and the seals of the guilds that are stamped on the amphorae are the guarantee of the quality that the buyers in the northern markets rely upon.

## By-Products and Secondary Uses

The olive tree produces a number of by-products that are used in addition to the oil and the fruit. The wood of the olive is hard and dense, and the grain is beautiful when polished, making it a favoured material for the carving of the bowls, the spoons, and the small implements that are used in the kitchens of the coast. The wood is also used in the turning of the handles for the tools and the weapons, and the olive wood handles are prized for their durability and their appearance.

The leaves of the olive are used in the preparation of a medicinal tea that is drunk by the people of the coast for the treatment of the fevers and the infections that afflict the settlements. The leaves are gathered in the spring and dried in the shade, and the tea that is brewed from the leaves is bitter but effective. The residue of the pressing, called the pomace, is used as a fuel for the lamps and the ovens, and the pomace is also spread on the fields as a fertiliser that returns the nutrients to the soil.
