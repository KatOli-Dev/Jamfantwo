## The Arrangements of Silver and Trust

The banking and credit of the known world are the arrangements by which the merchant houses and the wealthy individuals of the trading cities lend and borrow the silver that fuels commerce beyond the reach of any single purse. Banks in the sense that the scholars of the academies would recognise do not exist, for there is no institution that receives deposits from the public and lends them out at interest under the regulation of a sovereign authority. What exist instead are the counting houses of the great merchant families, the vaults of the guilds, and the private arrangements of individuals who have silver to lend and individuals who need to borrow it. The system is not codified in any single body of law, but it operates with a reliability that has sustained the commerce of the three continents for centuries, and the mechanisms of credit that have developed within it are among the most sophisticated achievements of the commercial civilisation of the known world.

## Merchant Family Records and Vaults

The counting house is the establishment in which a merchant family keeps its records, stores its silver, and conducts its financial business. Every merchant house of any size maintains a counting house in the city where it is based, and the larger houses maintain counting houses in the principal cities of the three continents. The counting house is not a bank in the modern sense, for it does not solicit deposits from the public, but it performs many of the functions of a bank for the merchants and traders who are connected to the house by ties of kinship, marriage, or long commercial association.

The counting house of a great merchant house is a substantial building of stone, with a strong room in the cellar or on the ground floor protected by iron doors and locks of gnomish manufacture. The strong room contains the house's own silver, the silver of correspondents who have placed it in the house's keeping, and the securities and bills of exchange that represent the house's outstanding credits. The counting house is staffed by a chief clerk, a deputy clerk, and a number of junior clerks who maintain the ledgers, verify the weights of coin, and prepare the correspondence with the house's correspondents in other cities.

The chief clerk of a counting house is a person of considerable standing in the commercial life of the city, for the chief clerk knows the state of the house's finances and the creditworthiness of every merchant with whom the house deals. The chief clerk is the person to whom a merchant seeking a loan applies, and the chief clerk's assessment of the merchant's character and prospects is the foundation upon which the decision to lend is made. The chief clerks of the great counting houses of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) and [Khalisar](/content/location/settlement/city/khalisar) are among the most knowledgeable people in the known world about the state of trade, and their opinions are sought by sovereigns and guildmasters as well as by merchants.

## Guild Common Funds and Lending to Members

The guilds of the trading cities maintain vaults in which they store the silver that constitutes their common funds, and these vaults serve as a source of credit for the members of the guild. The guild of the [money-changers](/content/economy/currency) and the guild of the dealers in credit in each city maintains the most substantial vault, for the money-changers are the dealers in silver and the guild's vault is the place where the standard weights and reference coins are kept. The guild of the merchants in each city maintains a separate vault, from which loans are made to members who need silver to finance a trading voyage or to purchase a cargo.

The terms on which the guild lends to its members are set by the guild's council and published in the register of the guild. The interest charged on a guild loan is typically lower than the interest charged by a private lender, for the guild's purpose is to support the trade of its members rather than to profit from their need. The guild also requires security for its loans, which may take the form of a pledge of goods, a mortgage on property, or the guarantee of a fellow member of good standing. The guild has the power to seize the security if the loan is not repaid, and the records of the guild record the names of members who have defaulted and the securities that were forfeited.

The vault of the guild of the [Vintners and Cellarers](/content/government/local/vintners-and-cellarers) in [Vassholm](/content/location/settlement/city/vassholm) is an example of the type. The vault is a room in the cellar of the guild hall, lined with stone and protected by a door of iron with three locks, the keys of which are held by the master, the senior warden, and the clerk of the guild. The vault contains the guild's silver, which is lent to members at an interest of one penny per mark per month, a rate that the guild has maintained for more than a century. The loans are made for the purchase of wine and ale, the repair of barrels and brewing vessels, and the fitting out of the vessels that carry the guild's products down the rivers to the coast.

## Houses of Account and Depositor Trust

The Silver Mark tradition, though it is a religious observance rather than a banking institution, maintains a network of houses of account that serve as centres of credit in the trading cities where the tradition is practised. The houses of account are attached to the guilds of the market cities, and they are the places where the trading records are kept and verified and where the discipline of mark and measure is maintained. The houses of account also serve as depositories for the silver of the faithful, who entrust their savings to the keeping of the master of account on the understanding that the silver will be kept safe and returned on demand.

The master of account does not lend the silver deposited in the house of account, for the doctrine of the Silver Mark teaches that the silver of the depositor is held in trust and that to lend it without the depositor's consent is a violation of the discipline of measure. The house of account is a place of safekeeping rather than a source of credit, and the faithful who deposit their silver in the house do so in the confidence that it will be there when they return for it. The discipline of the Silver Mark, which teaches that the honest keeping of the depositor's silver is a form of witness to the Truemarker, reinforces the trust that the faithful place in the house of account.

The houses of account nonetheless play a significant role in the credit of the trading cities, for the masters of account are among the most trusted figures in the commercial life of the city. A merchant who seeks a loan from a private lender will ask the master of account for a letter of recommendation, and the master's assessment of the merchant's honesty and capacity is a valuable endorsement. The houses of account also maintain registers of the creditworthiness of the merchants of the city, compiled from the records of the guilds and the observations of the masters, and the registers are consulted by lenders and borrowers alike.

## Financing the Perils of the Sea Voyage

The maritime loan is the oldest and most important form of credit in the intercontinental trade, for the voyage across the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) or through the [Velden Strait](/content/location/natural/feature/strait-of-velden) is expensive to fit out and the merchant who finances the voyage must wait many months for the return of the vessel and the sale of the cargo. The maritime loan is made by a lender to the merchant who owns the cargo or the captain who commands the vessel, and the loan is secured against the vessel and the cargo. The interest on the maritime loan is higher than the interest on a loan secured against property on land, for the risk of loss at sea is considerable and the lender must be compensated for bearing it.

The terms of the maritime loan are set by the contract between the lender and the borrower, drawn up before witnesses and registered with the guild of the merchants in the port of departure. The contract specifies the amount of the loan, the interest to be paid, the voyage for which the loan is made, and the conditions under which the loan is to be repaid. If the vessel and its cargo are lost at sea, the loan is forgiven and the lender bears the loss. If the vessel returns safely and the cargo is sold, the borrower repays the loan with interest. The rate of interest varies with the length of the voyage, the season of the year, and the reputation of the borrower, but the typical rate for a voyage from Eldreth to Khalisar and back is one part in eight, or twelve and a half per cent of the amount of the loan.

The [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) maintains a system of maritime insurance that supplements the system of maritime loans. The league's insurance fund is maintained by contributions from the merchant houses of the member cities, and it compensates the insured for the loss of a vessel or cargo. The insurance is not compulsory, but the merchant houses that trade regularly across the Auric Passage typically subscribe to the fund, for the cost of the contribution is small compared to the potential loss of a cargo of [Amurra](/content/location/settlement/city/amurra) spices or [Sahrani](/content/language/spoken/sahrani) dyes. The league's admiralty court, which sits permanently in Khalisar, adjudicates disputes arising from insurance claims, and its judgments are enforced by the collective authority of the member cities.

## Dwarven and Karhold Banking Institutions

The mining cities of the mountains have developed forms of banking that are distinct from the practices of the coastal trading cities. The dwarven banks of [Stoneheim](/content/location/settlement/city/stoneheim) and the human banks of the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm) are institutions that have grown out of the need to finance the extraction and transport of the mineral wealth of the mountains. The banks of the mining cities are the most securely vaulted in the known world, for the silver and gold and precious stones that pass through their strong rooms are the produce of the mines that the same banks have financed.

The dwarven bank of Stoneheim is the largest and most reputable institution of its kind, operating under the authority of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) and subject to the oversight of the council of the holds. The bank receives deposits from dwarven families and from the treasuries of the holds, lends silver to mining enterprises and to merchant houses that trade in dwarven metalwork, and issues bills of exchange that are accepted at face value in the markets of every city where dwarven goods are traded. The bank maintains a correspondence with the counting houses of Eldreth, Khalisar, and Nordhaven, and the exchange of bills between the dwarven bank and the human counting houses is the mechanism by which the proceeds of the dwarven metal trade are transferred from the coastal markets to the mountain vaults.

The banks of the Karhold Realm are smaller institutions, operating under the authority of the king and subject to the regulation of the guild of the moneyers of the realm. The banks of [Karhold](/content/location/settlement/city/karhold) and [Highgate](/content/location/settlement/city/highgate) finance the extraction and transport of ore along the [Deep Realm Tradeway](/content/location/route/trade/deep-realm-tradeway), lending silver to the mine operators for the purchase of tools and the payment of wages and receiving repayment in the form of ore delivered to the mills. The banks of the Karhold Realm are noted for their conservatism, for the dwarven and human merchants who operate them are careful lenders who require substantial security for every loan they make.

## Regulating Interest and the Unlicensed Lender

The charging of interest on loans is a subject of debate among the learned and the pious of the known world, and the limits of acceptable interest are a matter of custom and regulation in every trading city. The tradition of the Silver Mark teaches that the charging of interest is permissible within the discipline of measure, but that the rate must not exceed the just compensation for the use of the silver and the risk of its loss. The doctrine of the Truemarker holds that silver is a measure of value and that to charge interest is to demand payment for the use of a measure, which is in itself a form of deception. The teaching of the church is not that interest is always wrong, but that it must be moderate and that the lender must not take advantage of the borrower's necessity.

The regulation of interest is maintained by the guild of the money-changers in each city, which publishes the maximum rate at which loans may be made within the jurisdiction of the guild. The maximum rate in the cities of the [Northern Coast League](/content/government/national/northern-coast-league) is fixed at one part in twelve per year, a rate that the guild considers just and that the church of the Silver Mark accepts as within the discipline of measure. The rate in the cities of Aethelar is similar, though the guild in Eldreth permits a slightly higher rate for maritime loans. The rate in the interior of Sahrana, where the risk of loss is greater and the supply of silver is smaller, is permitted to rise to one part in eight, and the loans made by the caravaneers of the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact) are routinely made at this rate.

The lender who charges more than the permitted maximum is subject to the discipline of the guild, which may fine the lender, suspend the licence to lend, or in the most serious cases expel the lender from the guild. The discipline is effective in the cities where the guild has authority, but it is less effective in the countryside and in the smaller towns where the guild's reach is limited. The borrower who cannot obtain credit from the guild or from a licensed lender may turn to the unlicensed lenders who operate outside the guild's authority, but the rates they charge are considerably higher and the terms of their loans are less favourable. The unlicensed lenders are a presence in every market town, and the magistrates of the towns are aware of their existence, but the enforcement of the guild's regulations in the countryside is a challenge that the guild has not yet solved.

## Royal Borrowing and Pledged Revenues

The sovereigns of the kingdoms and the councils of the trading cities are among the largest borrowers in the known world, for the cost of maintaining fortifications and navies and paying officials exceeds the revenue that can be raised from taxes and tolls in any given year. The sovereign who needs to borrow silver may seek a loan from the counting houses of the great merchant families, from the vaults of the guilds, or from the banks of the mining cities. The terms of the sovereign's loans are negotiated individually with each lender, and the interest charged reflects the sovereign's reputation for repayment and the security that the sovereign can offer.

The credit of the [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth) is the strongest of the sovereign borrowers, for the Aldreth has never defaulted on a loan and the revenues of the harbour of Eldreth provide ample security. The credit of the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation) is also strong, for the grain of the plains is a reliable source of revenue and the confederation's cities are governed by merchants who understand the importance of maintaining a good reputation with the lenders. The credit of the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes) and the smaller kingdoms of the interior of Sahrana is weaker, for their revenues are less predictable and their access to the lenders of the coastal cities is more limited.

The relationship between the sovereign and the lender is a delicate one, for the sovereign who defaults on a loan cannot be compelled to repay by the ordinary mechanisms of the guild or the courts. The lenders protect themselves by requiring that the sovereign pledge a source of revenue as security for the loan, such as the tolls of a harbour or the taxes of a province. If the sovereign defaults, the lender may take possession of the pledged revenue and administer it until the loan is repaid. The practice has given rise to conflicts in the past, and the history of the [Twin-Reaches Wars](/content/history/twin-reaches-wars) and the [Stoneheim Pass War](/content/history/stoneheim-pass-war) includes disputes between sovereigns and lenders that contributed to the outbreak of those conflicts.
