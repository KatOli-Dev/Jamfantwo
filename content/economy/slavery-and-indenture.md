## The Systems of Bound Labour

Binding a person to the service of another is a practice found in every region of the known world, though the forms it takes and the conditions under which the bound person lives and work vary widely across cultures and legal traditions. A slave is a person owned by another, bought and sold as chattel; the slave's labour is the property of the owner. An indentured servant is a person bound to another by contract for a fixed term, an obligation entered into voluntarily or imposed by a court's judgment.

Slavery is concentrated in the Sahrani interior and the warm coastal regions, where demand for labour in spice plantations, salt pans, and wealthy households is high. Indenture is more common in the Aethelaran regions, where guilds and courts have developed systems of bound apprenticeship and debt servitude.

## The Slave Trade of the Sahrani Coast

The slave trade of the Sahrani coast is the largest and most organised in the known world, supplying labour for the spice plantations, salt pans, and households of the wealthy cities. Slaves are taken from among prisoners of war that the tribes of the interior fight among themselves, and from families sold into slavery by creditors who have seized debtors and their families.

Slaves are brought to the coastal markets along the same routes the caravans use, and the slave market of Khalisar is the largest in the known world. Slaves are sold at auction in the market square, and the price a slave commands depends on age, strength, and skills.

The conditions of a slave's life depend on the master and the work required. A slave who works in the household of a wealthy merchant lives in relative comfort, fed, clothed, and housed in quarters attached to the master's residence. A slave who works in the salt pans or spice plantations lives in conditions that are harsh and shorten life.

## The Indenture of the Debtor

Debt indenture is a form of bound labour more common in the Aethelaran regions, where courts have the power to impose servitude on a debtor who cannot repay. A debtor who owes silver to a creditor and cannot pay may be bound to the creditor's service for a term of years until the debt is worked off.

Debt indenture is regulated by courts and guilds, and the term of service is determined by the amount of the debt and the value of the labour the debtor can perform. The debtor's labour is valued at a rate set by the guild, and the debtor's obligation is reduced by the value of the labour performed.

Debt indenture is as much a form of punishment as a means of repayment, and the indentured debtor is subject to the master's discipline. The master may not beat or abuse the debtor, for the indenture is a legal contract and not ownership of the person, but the master may impose penalties for failure to perform the labour the contract requires.

## The Apprenticeship of the Guild

Guild apprenticeship is the most common form of indenture in the Aethelaran cities, the means by which a young person is trained in a trade the guild regulates. The apprentice is bound to the master by a contract of indenture, typically for seven years, and lives in the master's household and works in the master's workshop.

The apprenticeship contract is registered with the guild, which has the power to enforce the terms against both master and apprentice. The master must provide complete training in the trade, and the apprentice must serve with diligence and obedience.

Apprenticeship is not slavery, and an apprentice mistreated by the master may bring a complaint to the guild. The guild investigates and may release the apprentice from the contract if the master has failed to meet obligations. An apprentice who completes the term is admitted to the guild as a journeyman, who may then work for wages and travel from city to city.

## Debt Bondage in the Rural Regions

Debt bondage in the rural regions is a form of bound labour practised where the money economy is less developed and access to credit is limited. A farmer who needs seed and tools for planting may borrow from a landlord or merchant, pledging the family's labour as security for the loan.

Debt bondage is the most common form of bound labour in the rural regions of Aethelar and the Sahrani interior. A farmer who cannot repay the loan is bound to the lender's service, and the farmer and the farmer's family work the lender's land until the debt is repaid. The debt may pass from parent to child, and a family may remain in bondage for generations.

Debt bondage is the subject of debate among reformers, who argue that the system keeps the poor in perpetual servitude and prevents improvement in the condition of the rural population. Guilds and city councils have taken some measures to regulate debt bondage, but the practice remains common in regions where the guild's authority is limited.

## The Moral and Legal Debates

Slavery and indenture are subjects of debate among the learned and the pious of the known world. The Still Flame teaches that the warmth of the hearth should be extended to all who come to it, and the hearth-keepers who extend hospitality to slave and indentured alike are witnesses to this teaching. The Unwritten Name teaches that naming a person as a slave is a naming the discipline of nouns must attend to, for the name of the slave is not the name of the person but the name of the condition.

The legal status of the slave varies across jurisdictions. A slave who escapes from a master in Khalisar may be reclaimed, and the person who harbours the slave is subject to penalty. A slave who reaches the territory of the Aldreth is free, for the courts of the Aldreth do not recognise ownership of one person by another.

## The Abolitionist Movements

Movements for the abolition of slavery have grown in recent generations, particularly in the cities of the Aethelaran coast. The scholars of the Unwritten Name have written treatises arguing that the condition of the slave is a violation of the discipline of nouns, for the slave is named as a thing and not as a person. The hearth-keepers of the Still Flame have provided shelter to escaped slaves, and the houses of memory of the Shield and Song have recorded the names of slaves who have died in bondage.

Arguments for abolition have not yet won wide acceptance, and the slave trade of the Sahrani coast continues to supply the labour the plantations and salt pans require. The reformers continue to press for change, and the debate is one of the most contentious in the public life of the known world.
