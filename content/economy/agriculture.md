## The Cultivation of the Soil

Agriculture is the foundation upon which the settled civilisations of the known world rest, for without the reliable production of grain, vegetables, and fruit the great cities could not support their populations and the trade that connects the three continents would lack the staple goods that fill the holds of the merchant vessels. The practice of agriculture varies widely across the regions, from the vast grain fields of the Great Grass Plains to the terraced hillsides of the Sahrani highlands and the intensive garden plots of the halfling valleys. The techniques that farmers employ, the crops they grow, and the organisation of the agricultural year are determined by the climate, the soil, and the traditions that have accumulated over the centuries of settlement.

The human peoples of Aethelar are the most extensive agriculturalists of the known world, and the grain fields of the Plains Confederation produce the largest surplus of any region. The centaur kindreds of the plains practise a more mobile form of cultivation, tending the grasslands deliberately without the fixed fields that characterise human agriculture. The halfling communities of the lowland valleys are the most intensive cultivators, producing a variety of crops from small plots that are worked with a patience and skill that other peoples admire. The dwarven peoples of the mountains rely on trade for the greater part of their food, though they cultivate fungi in the deep chambers of their holds and maintain terraced fields in the sheltered valleys.

## The Grain Fields of the Plains

The grain fields of the Plains Confederation are the largest continuous cultivated area in the known world, stretching from the foothills of the eastern mountains to the river valleys that drain into the western sea. The soil of the plains is a deep loam that has accumulated over millennia, rich in the nutrients that the grain crops require, and the rainfall is sufficient in most years to produce a reliable harvest. The farmers of the plains grow wheat, barley, and oats in rotation, allowing the fields to lie fallow every third year to restore the fertility that the crops exhaust.

The plough is the principal tool of the plains farmer, drawn by a team of oxen or horses across the deep soil of the fields. The plough used in the plains is a heavy wheeled plough that cuts a deep furrow and turns the soil, burying the weeds and the stubble of the previous crop. The ploughing is conducted in the autumn after the harvest, and the field is left to lie through the winter before the sowing in the spring. The sowing is done by hand, the seed broadcast across the furrowed field and then covered by a light harrowing.

The harvest is the critical period of the agricultural year, for the grain must be cut and gathered before the autumn rains spoil the crop. The farmers of the plains work from before dawn until after dusk during the harvest weeks, cutting the grain with sickles and binding it into sheaves that are stacked in the field to dry. The sheaves are then carried to the threshing floor, where the grain is beaten from the straw and winnowed in the wind to separate the chaff. The threshing is a communal activity, with the farmers of the neighbouring fields gathering at each farm in turn to complete the work before the winter sets in.

The yield of the plains fields is the highest in the known world, with a good harvest producing thirty or forty bushels of grain for every bushel sown. The surplus is carried along the Grassland Traderoad to the coastal cities and loaded onto vessels for the trade across the Auric Passage to Sahrana. The grain trade is the foundation of the prosperity of the Plains Confederation, and the price of grain in the markets of Eldreth and Khalisar is the measure of the health of the harvest in the plains.

## The Terraced Hillsides of Sahrana

The agriculture of Sahrana is shaped by the different conditions of the climate and the terrain. The coastal strip is fertile and well watered, but the interior is dry and the soil is thin. The farmers of the Sahrani interior have developed the technique of terracing to make the most of the limited rainfall and the steep hillsides, cutting the slopes into a series of flat platforms that hold the water and prevent the soil from washing away. The terraces are irrigated by channels that carry water from the springs and the seasonal streams, and the fields are planted with wheat, barley, and millet in the cooler months and with sorghum and vegetables in the warmer months.

The terrace is a permanent structure, built of stone walls that hold the soil in place and require regular maintenance to prevent collapse. The farmers of the Sahrani highlands are skilled in the construction and repair of the terraces, and the knowledge of the terrace-building is passed from parent to child through the generations. The terrace system of the highlands is among the oldest continuous agricultural practices in the known world, and the hillsides of the interior bear the marks of centuries of careful cultivation.

The date palm is the most important tree crop of the Sahrani oases, providing fruit, timber, and shade for the other crops that are grown beneath its canopy. The date harvest is the principal event of the agricultural year in the oasis settlements, and the dates are dried and pressed and packed for transport to the coastal markets. The date trade is a significant component of the economy of the Sahrani interior, and the oases that produce the finest dates are renowned throughout the known world.

## The Garden Plots of the Halfling Valleys

The halfling communities of the lowland valleys practise a form of agriculture that is distinct from the field cultivation of the plains and the terracing of the highlands. The halfling garden is a small plot of ground, intensively cultivated and carefully managed, that produces a greater variety of crops from a smaller area than any other form of agriculture in the known world. The halfling garden is a work of art as much as a source of food, with the vegetables, herbs, and flowers arranged in patterns that are pleasing to the eye and beneficial to the growth of the plants.

The halfling farmer works the soil by hand, using a small spade and a fork to turn the earth and a hoe to keep the weeds at bay. The soil is enriched with compost and manure, and the crops are rotated through the beds in a cycle that maintains the fertility of the soil without the need for fallow periods. The halfling garden produces beans, peas, carrots, turnips, cabbages, leeks, onions, and a dozen other vegetables, together with herbs for seasoning and flowers for the table. The garden also includes fruit trees, berry bushes, and a small patch of grain for the baking of the daily bread.

The halfling farmer is a patient and observant cultivator, attending to the needs of each plant with the same care that the craftsman attends to the work of the hands. The halfling tradition of agriculture is a calling as much as a trade, and the farmers who tend the gardens of the valleys are respected in their communities for the knowledge and the skill that they bring to the work. The halfling garden is not a source of surplus for the grain trade, but it is the foundation of the self-sufficiency that characterises the halfling way of life, and the abundance of the garden is the basis of the hospitality for which the halflings are known.

## The Vineyards and Orchards of the River Valleys

The cultivation of the vine and the fruit tree is a specialised branch of agriculture concentrated in the temperate river valleys of Aethelar and the coastal hills of Sahrana. The vine requires a well-drained soil and a sunny aspect, and the vineyards of the river valleys are planted on the south-facing slopes where the sun reaches for the longest part of the day. The cultivation of the vine is a labour-intensive process, requiring the pruning of the branches, the training of the shoots along the supports, and the careful management of the ripening of the grapes.

The vintage is the harvest of the grapes, conducted in the autumn when the fruit has reached the peak of its ripeness. The grapes are picked by hand and carried to the press, where the juice is extracted and set to ferment in casks and vats. The wine that results is the principal beverage of the temperate regions, consumed at every meal in the households of the river valleys and traded along the routes that connect the wine-growing regions to the cities of the coast.

The orchards of the river valleys produce apples, pears, plums, and cherries, and the fruit is consumed fresh in the season of the harvest and preserved for the winter by drying, pressing, and the making of conserves. The orchard is a long-term investment, for the fruit tree takes several years to reach full production, and the farmer who plants an orchard is providing for the children and the grandchildren as much as for the present household. The orchards of the river valleys are the most beautiful of the agricultural landscapes, with the trees in blossom in the spring and in fruit in the autumn, and the farmers who tend them are among the most respected in their communities.

## The Agricultural Year and the Labour of the Seasons

The agricultural year follows the cycle of the seasons, with each period of the year bringing its own tasks and its own demands on the labour of the farming household. The winter is the season of rest and preparation, when the fields lie fallow and the farmer repairs the tools and the fences and tends to the livestock in the barns and the byres. The spring is the season of the sowing, when the fields are ploughed and the seed is broadcast and the hope of the harvest is committed to the ground.

The summer is the season of growth and of the constant labour of the weeding and the watering and the protection of the crops from the pests and the diseases that threaten them. The farmer rises before the sun and works through the heat of the day, checking the fields for signs of trouble and attending to the needs of the growing plants. The autumn is the season of the harvest, the culmination of the year's work and the time of the greatest labour and the greatest reward.

The labour of the agricultural year is shared by the entire household, from the youngest children who chase the birds from the newly sown fields to the oldest grandparents who mind the infants and prepare the meals. The farming household is a unit of production and consumption, and the work of the fields is the work of the family, with the neighbours and the kinfolk gathering at the times of the greatest need to help with the ploughing and the harvest and the threshing.

## The Role of the Guilds and the Markets

The guilds of the farmers and the merchants play a significant role in the agriculture of the known world, regulating the quality of the grain and the wine and the fruit that are brought to market and setting the prices at which they may be sold. The guild of the bakers sets the weight and the price of the loaf, and the guild of the vintners sets the standards of quality for the wine that is sold in the inns and the taverns. The guilds of the merchants control the trade in grain and wine and fruit, and the merchants of the coastal cities are the link between the farmers of the interior and the consumers of the ports.

The market is the place where the produce of the fields is exchanged for the goods that the farming household cannot produce for itself, and the market towns of the river valleys and the plains are the centres of the agricultural economy. The farmer who brings a wagonload of grain to the market exchanges it for salt and iron and cloth and the other necessities of the household, and the merchant who buys the grain arranges its transport to the cities where it is needed. The system of markets and guilds and merchants connects the farmer of the interior to the consumer of the coast, and the chain of exchange that links them is the foundation of the commerce of the known world.
