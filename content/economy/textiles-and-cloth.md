## The Making of Fabric from the Fibres of the World

The production of textiles is among the most widespread of the manufacturing trades, for every household in the known world requires cloth for clothing, bedding, and the furnishing of the home. The fibres from which cloth is made are drawn from the plants and the animals of the regions where the textile trade is practised, and the quality and the character of the cloth reflect the nature of the fibre and the skill of the weaver. Wool from the sheep of the highlands, flax from the fields of the river valleys, cotton from the irrigated gardens of the warm coasts, and the silk that is produced by the worms of the eastern forests each have their place in the textile economy.

The weaving of cloth is conducted in the workshops of the towns and the villages, where the weavers sit at their looms through the daylight hours, turning the spun thread into the fabric that the tailors and the seamstresses will cut and sew into garments. The loom is the principal tool of the weaver, and the design of the loom determines the width and the pattern of the cloth that the weaver produces. The looms of the Aethelaran weavers produce cloth of a standard width of two ells, and the length of the cloth is measured in yards that are marked on the frame of the loom.

## The Wool Trade of the Highlands

Wool is the most common textile fibre in the known world, produced from the fleece of the sheep that are kept in the highlands and the hill country of Aethelar and the uplands of Morvind. The wool of the highland sheep is of the finest quality, and the cloth that is woven from it is the most sought-after textile in the markets of the coastal cities. The wool is shorn from the sheep in the late spring, after the weather has warmed and the sheep have shed the heaviest of their winter fleece.

The processing of the wool is a sequence of operations that begins with the washing of the fleece to remove the dirt and the grease that the wool has accumulated through the winter. The washed wool is then combed to align the fibres, and the combed wool is spun into thread on the spinning wheel. The spinning of the wool is conducted in the households of the hill country, where the women of the family spin the thread that the men will weave into cloth in the workshops of the valleys.

The weaving of the wool is conducted in the wool towns, which are the settlements concentrated along the rivers that provide the water power for the fulling mills. The fulling of the wool cloth is the process by which the cloth is cleansed and thickened and the fibres are felted together to produce a dense and durable fabric. The fulling mill uses a water wheel to drive the hammers that beat the cloth in the troughs of water and fuller's earth, and the noise of the fulling mills is a constant presence in the wool towns.

## The Linen of the River Valleys

Linen is the cloth that is woven from the fibres of the flax plant, which is cultivated in the river valleys of Aethelar and the temperate lowlands of the continent. The flax plant requires a rich soil and a reliable supply of water, and the fields where the flax is grown are among the most carefully managed in the agricultural regions. The flax is harvested in the summer, after the plant has flowered and the seeds have begun to ripen, and the stalks are pulled from the ground and laid out to dry.

The processing of the flax is a lengthy and labour-intensive sequence of operations that separates the fibres from the woody core of the stalk. The dried flax is retted, which is the soaking of the stalks in water to rot the pectins that bind the fibres to the core. The retted flax is then dried again and broken, which is the crushing of the stalks to separate the fibres from the woody material. The broken flax is scutched, which is the beating of the fibres to remove the last of the woody fragments, and the scutched flax is then combed into the long fibres that are spun into the linen thread.

The spinning of the linen thread is conducted on the same spinning wheels that are used for the wool, but the linen thread is spun more finely and with a tighter twist that gives the linen its characteristic smoothness and strength. The weaving of the linen is conducted in the workshops of the river towns, where the weavers produce the linen cloth that is used for the shirts and the shifts and the undergarments that the people of the temperate regions wear.

## The Cotton of the Warm Coasts

Cotton is the cloth that is woven from the fibres of the cotton plant, which is cultivated in the warm coastal regions of Sahrana and the islands of the warm seas. The cotton plant requires a long growing season and a reliable supply of water, and the fields where the cotton is grown are irrigated from the rivers and the wells that the farmers have dug. The cotton is harvested in the autumn, after the bolls have opened and the white fibres are exposed to the sun.

The processing of the cotton begins with the ginning, which is the separation of the fibres from the seeds that the boll contains. The gin is a simple machine that consists of a set of rollers that crush the boll and separate the fibres from the seeds, and the ginned cotton is then pressed into bales for transport to the spinning mills. The spinning of the cotton is conducted in the mills of the coastal cities, where the water wheels drive the spinning frames that produce the cotton thread.

The weaving of the cotton is conducted in the workshops of the Sahrani coast, where the weavers produce the cotton cloth that is the principal textile of the warm regions. The cotton cloth is lighter and more breathable than the wool and the linen, and it is the preferred fabric for the clothing of the people who live in the hot climates. The cotton cloth of Sahrana is exported to the colder regions of the north, where it is used for the lining of garments and the making of the underclothing that the northern peoples wear against the cold.

## The Silk of the Eastern Forests

Silk is the most luxurious of the textile fibres, produced from the cocoons of the silkworm that feeds on the leaves of the mulberry tree. The cultivation of the silkworm is practised in the eastern forests of the continent, where the mulberry tree grows in the sheltered valleys and the climate is warm and humid enough for the silkworm to thrive. The production of silk is the most labour-intensive of the textile trades, and the value of the silk cloth reflects the care and the skill that the production requires.

The silkworm is raised in the households of the silk-producing regions, where the worms are fed on the leaves of the mulberry tree through the weeks of their growth. The worms spin their cocoons in the trays that are provided for them, and the cocoons are harvested before the moths emerge and break the continuity of the thread. The cocoons are boiled to soften the sericin that binds the fibres, and the threads are unwound from the cocoons and twisted together into the silk thread that is then woven into the cloth.

The silk cloth that is produced in the eastern forests is the finest and the most expensive textile in the known world, and it is traded along the routes that connect the eastern regions to the markets of the west. The silk is used for the garments of the wealthy and the powerful, for the vestments of the priests and the robes of the sovereigns, and the silk trade is the foundation of the prosperity of the eastern cities.

## The Dyeing of the Cloth

The dyeing of the cloth is a separate trade that is practised in the towns and the cities where the textile workshops are concentrated. The dyer applies the colour to the cloth by immersion in the vats of the dye, and the quality and the permanence of the colour depend on the skill of the dyer and the quality of the dye stuffs that are used. The dyes are drawn from the plants and the minerals of the regions, and the trade in the dye stuffs is a significant component of the textile economy.

The most valuable of the dyes are the red dyes that are extracted from the bodies of the insects that live on the oaks of the warm regions and the purple dyes that are extracted from the shells of the molluscs of the warm seas. The blue dye that is extracted from the woad plant is the most common of the dyes, and the blue cloth of the Aethelaran weavers is the most widely traded textile in the known world. The yellow dyes that are extracted from the saffron crocus and the weld plant are used for the brightening of the other colours, and the black dyes that are extracted from the bark of the oak and the alder are used for the sombre garments of the mourning and the religious orders.
