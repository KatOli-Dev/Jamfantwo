## The Lending of Silver and the Charge of Interest

The lending of silver at interest is a practice that is as old as the trade itself, for the merchant who needs silver to finance a voyage or a purchase and the sovereign who needs silver to build a wall or to pay a garrison must borrow from those who have accumulated the surplus of the silver that the borrower lacks. The moneylender is the person who provides the silver, charging the interest for the use of the silver over the term of the loan. The moneylending trade is concentrated in the counting houses of the great merchant families and the vaults of the guilds, and the rates of interest that the lenders charge are regulated by the guilds and the councils of the cities.

The practice of charging interest is a subject of debate among the learned and the pious of the known world. The tradition of the Silver Mark teaches that the charging of interest is permissible within the discipline of the measure, but that the rate must not exceed the just compensation for the use of the silver and the risk of its loss. The doctrine of the Unwritten Name teaches that the naming of the interest is a form of the naming that the discipline of the nouns must attend to, and the senior readers have written at length about the distinction between the just interest and the exploitative charge.

## The Moneylender and the Counting House

The moneylender is a merchant who specialises in the lending of silver, and the counting house is the establishment where the moneylender conducts the business. The counting house is a building of the stone, with the strong room in the cellar or on the ground floor protected by the doors of the iron and the locks of the gnomish manufacture. The strong room contains the silver that the moneylender has accumulated from the profits of the trade and the deposits of the correspondents.

The moneylender receives the applications for the loans from the merchants and the craftsmen and the sovereigns who need the silver. The moneylender assesses the creditworthiness of the borrower, the security that the borrower can offer, and the purpose for which the loan is sought. The decision to lend is the judgment of the moneylender, and the moneylender who makes the poor judgment will lose the silver that has been lent.

The interest that the moneylender charges is determined by the risk of the loan and the term of the repayment. The loan that is secured by the pledge of the goods or the property is made at a lower rate of the interest than the loan that is unsecured. The loan that is made for the maritime voyage is made at a higher rate, for the risk of the loss at sea is considerable.

## The Regulation of the Interest

The guild of the money-changers in each city sets the maximum rate of the interest that the lenders may charge within the jurisdiction of the guild. The maximum rate in the cities of the Northern Coast League is fixed at one part in twelve per year, a rate that the guild considers just and that the church of the Silver Mark accepts as within the discipline of the measure.

The regulation of the interest is a matter of the public policy as much as the religious teaching, for the sovereign and the council of the city have an interest in the prevention of the exploitation of the borrowers by the unscrupulous lenders. The borrower who is charged the excessive interest is driven into the ruin, and the ruin of the borrower is the loss of the productive capacity of the city.

The lender who charges more than the permitted maximum is subject to the discipline of the guild, which may fine the lender, suspend the licence to lend, or in the most serious cases expel the lender from the guild. The discipline of the guild is effective in the cities where the guild has the authority, but it is less effective in the countryside and the smaller towns where the guild's reach is limited.

## The Unlicensed Lender

The borrower who cannot obtain the credit from the guild or from the licensed lender may turn to the unlicensed lenders who operate outside the guild's authority. The unlicensed lender charges the interest that is higher than the permitted maximum, and the terms of the loans are less favourable to the borrower.

The unlicensed lender is a presence in every market town, and the magistrates of the towns are aware of the existence of the trade, but the enforcement of the guild's regulations in the countryside is a challenge that the guild has not yet solved. The borrower who deals with the unlicensed lender is taking the risk of the excessive charges and the harsh terms, but the borrower who has no other access to the credit has little choice in the matter.

The unlicensed lender is not a member of the guild and is not subject to the discipline of the guild, and the recourse that the borrower has against the unlicensed lender is limited. The contract that is made with the unlicensed lender is enforceable in the courts, but the borrower who falls behind in the repayment may face the seizure of the property and the imprisonment.

## The Sovereign and the Public Debt

The sovereigns of the kingdoms and the councils of the trading cities are among the largest borrowers in the known world, for the cost of maintaining the fortifications and the navies and paying the officials exceeds the revenue that can be raised from the taxes and the tolls in any given year. The sovereign who needs to borrow the silver may seek a loan from the counting houses of the great merchant families, from the vaults of the guilds, or from the banks of the mining cities.

The credit of the Aldreth is the strongest of the sovereign borrowers, for the Aldreth has never defaulted on a loan and the revenues of the harbour of Eldreth provide the ample security. The credit of the Plains Confederation is also strong, for the grain of the plains is a reliable source of the revenue and the confederation's cities are governed by the merchants who understand the importance of maintaining the good reputation with the lenders.

The relationship between the sovereign and the lender is a delicate one, for the sovereign who defaults on a loan cannot be compelled to repay by the ordinary mechanisms of the guild or the courts. The lenders protect themselves by requiring that the sovereign pledge a source of the revenue as the security for the loan, such as the tolls of a harbour or the taxes of a province. If the sovereign defaults, the lender may take possession of the pledged revenue and administer it until the loan is repaid.

## The Debate on the Usury

The charging of the interest is a subject of the serious debate among the learned, and the positions that the different traditions maintain reflect their deeper teachings about the nature of the value and the justice. The tradition of the Silver Mark teaches that the silver is a measure of the value and that to charge the interest is to demand the payment for the use of the measure. The teaching of the church is not that the interest is always wrong, but that it must be moderate and that the lender must not take advantage of the borrower's necessity.

The scholars of the Unwritten Name have written that the interest is a form of the naming, and that the naming of the rate is the discipline that the reader must attend to with the care. The doctrine does not prohibit the charging of the interest, but it teaches that the lender must name the rate with the honesty and must recognise that the interest is the compensation for the risk and not the price of the time.

The Still Flame teaches that the warmth of the hearth is the measure of the hospitality, and that the lending of the silver should be conducted with the same warmth that the household extends to the guest. The hearth-keepers teach that the lender who charges the excessive interest is violating the duty of the hospitality that the faith requires.

## The Practices of the Different Peoples

The dwarven peoples of the mountains do not charge the interest on the loans that are made within the community, for the lending of the silver is regarded as the duty of the kin and the member of the hold. The dwarven bank of Stoneheim lends to the members of the holds without the charge of the interest, and the loan is repaid when the borrower is able to repay.

The halfling communities of the valleys practise the mutual lending among the households, and the interest is not charged on the loans that are made within the community. The halfling who needs the silver for the planting or the harvest borrows from the neighbour, and the repayment is made when the harvest is brought in.

The centaur kindreds of the plains do not practise the moneylending in the form that the settled peoples know, for the centaurs do not accumulate the silver in the quantities that the settled trade requires. The centaur who needs the assistance is helped by the kindred, and the obligation of the repayment is a matter of the honour and not of the interest.
