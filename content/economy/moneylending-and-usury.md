## The Lending of Silver and the Charge of Interest

The lending of silver at interest is a practice as old as trade itself, for the merchant who needs silver to finance a voyage and the sovereign who needs silver to build a wall or pay a garrison must borrow from those who have accumulated the surplus the borrower lacks. The moneylender provides the silver, charging interest for its use over the term of the loan. Moneylending is concentrated in the counting houses of the great merchant families and the vaults of the guilds, and the rates of interest are regulated by the guilds and the councils of the cities.

The practice of charging interest is a subject of debate among the learned and the pious of the known world. The tradition of the [Silver Mark](/content/religion/silver-mark) teaches that charging interest is permissible within the discipline of the measure, but that the rate must not exceed the just compensation for the use of the silver and the risk of its loss. The doctrine of the Unwritten Name teaches that the naming of interest is a form of naming that the discipline of the nouns must attend to, and the senior readers have written at length about the distinction between just interest and exploitative charges.

## The Moneylender and the Counting House

The moneylender is a merchant who specialises in lending silver, and the counting house is the establishment where the business is conducted. A counting house is a stone building, with the strong room in the cellar or on the ground floor protected by iron doors and locks of gnomish manufacture. The strong room contains the silver the moneylender has accumulated from trading profits and deposits from correspondents.

The moneylender receives applications for loans from merchants, craftsmen, and sovereigns who need silver. The moneylender assesses the creditworthiness of the borrower, the security that can be offered, and the purpose for which the loan is sought. The decision to lend is the moneylender's judgment, and the moneylender who judges poorly will lose the silver that has been lent.

The interest charged is determined by the risk of the loan and the term of repayment. A loan secured by a pledge of goods or property is made at a lower rate than an unsecured loan. A loan made for a maritime voyage commands a higher rate, for the risk of loss at sea is considerable.

## The Regulation of Interest

The guild of money-changers in each city sets the maximum rate of interest that lenders may charge within the guild's jurisdiction. The maximum rate in the cities of the Northern Coast League is fixed at one part in twelve per year, a rate the guild considers just and that the church of the Silver Mark accepts as within the discipline of the measure.

Regulation of interest is a matter of public policy as much as religious teaching, for the sovereign and the city council have an interest in preventing the exploitation of borrowers by unscrupulous lenders. A borrower charged excessive interest is driven to ruin, and the ruin of a borrower is the loss of the productive capacity of the city.

A lender who charges more than the permitted maximum is subject to the guild's discipline, which may fine the lender, suspend the lending licence, or in the most serious cases expel the lender from the guild. The guild's discipline is effective in cities where the guild holds authority, but less effective in the countryside and smaller towns where its reach is limited.

## The Unlicensed Lender

Borrowers who cannot obtain credit from the guild or from a licensed lender may turn to unlicensed lenders who operate outside the guild's authority. Unlicensed lenders charge interest higher than the permitted maximum, and the terms of their loans are less favourable to the borrower.

Unlicensed lenders are a presence in every market town, and the magistrates are aware of the trade, but enforcing guild regulations in the countryside is a challenge the guild has not yet solved. The borrower who deals with an unlicensed lender risks excessive charges and harsh terms, but the borrower with no other access to credit has little choice in the matter.

An unlicensed lender is not a guild member and is not subject to guild discipline, and the recourse a borrower has against such a lender is limited. A contract made with an unlicensed lender is enforceable in the courts, but a borrower who falls behind in repayment may face seizure of property and imprisonment.

## The Sovereign and the Public Debt

Sovereigns of the kingdoms and councils of the trading cities are among the largest borrowers in the known world, for the cost of maintaining fortifications, navies, and officials exceeds the revenue raised from taxes and tolls in any given year. A sovereign who needs to borrow silver may seek a loan from the counting houses of the great merchant families, from the vaults of the guilds, or from the banks of the mining cities.

The credit of the Aldreth is the strongest among sovereign borrowers, for the Aldreth has never defaulted on a loan and the revenues of the harbour of Eldreth provide ample security. The credit of the Plains Confederation is also strong, for the grain of the plains is a reliable source of revenue and the confederation's cities are governed by merchants who understand the importance of maintaining a good reputation with lenders.

The relationship between sovereign and lender is delicate, for a sovereign who defaults on a loan cannot be compelled to repay by the ordinary mechanisms of the guild or the courts. Lenders protect themselves by requiring the sovereign to pledge a source of revenue as security for the loan, such as the tolls of a harbour or the taxes of a province. If the sovereign defaults, the lender may take possession of the pledged revenue and administer it until the loan is repaid.

## The Debate on Usury

Charging interest is a subject of serious debate among the learned, and the positions the different traditions maintain reflect their deeper teachings about the nature of value and justice. The tradition of the Silver Mark teaches that silver is a measure of value and that to charge interest is to demand payment for the use of the measure. The church teaches not that interest is always wrong, but that it must be moderate and that the lender must not take advantage of a borrower's necessity.

The scholars of the Unwritten Name have written that interest is a form of naming, and that the naming of the rate is a discipline the reader must attend to with care. The doctrine does not prohibit charging interest, but it teaches that the lender must name the rate honestly and must recognise that interest is compensation for risk and not the price of time.

The Still Flame teaches that the warmth of the hearth is the measure of hospitality, and that lending silver should be conducted with the same warmth the household extends to a guest. The hearth-keepers teach that the lender who charges excessive interest is violating the duty of hospitality that the faith requires.

## The Practices of the Different Peoples

The dwarven peoples of the mountains do not charge interest on loans made within the community, for lending silver is regarded as a duty of kin and a member of the hold. The dwarven bank of Stoneheim lends to members of the holds without charging interest, and the loan is repaid when the borrower is able.

The halfling communities of the valleys practise mutual lending among households, and interest is not charged on loans made within the community. A halfling who needs silver for planting or the harvest borrows from a neighbour, and repayment is made when the harvest is brought in.

The centaur kindreds of the plains do not practise moneylending in the form the settled peoples know, for the centaurs do not accumulate silver in the quantities that settled trade requires. A centaur who needs assistance is helped by the kindred, and the obligation of repayment is a matter of honour, not of interest.
