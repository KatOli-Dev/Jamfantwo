## The Transformation of Hides into Useful Material

The tanning of animal hides into leather is a trade that is practised in every settlement of any size, for leather is the material from which the shoes and the belts and the saddles and the bags and the countless other articles of daily use are made. The tanning trade is one of the oldest of the manufacturing crafts, and the techniques that the tanners employ have been refined over centuries of experience. The process of tanning transforms the raw hide, which would rot and decay if left untreated, into a durable and flexible material that can be worked into the forms that the craftsmen require.

The tanning trade is concentrated in the towns and the cities where the slaughterhouses are located and where the supply of raw hides is abundant. The tanners work in the districts that are set aside for their trade, which are typically located on the outskirts of the settlement near the water that the tanning process requires. The odour of the tanning pits is strong and unpleasant, and the tanners are accustomed to the smell that the inhabitants of the other districts find objectionable.

## The Preparation of the Hide

The raw hide that the tanner receives from the slaughterhouse is the skin of the animal with the hair still attached. The first operation in the tanning process is the soaking of the hide in water to remove the blood and the dirt and to soften the skin. The soaking is conducted in the pits that the tanner maintains, and the water is changed at regular intervals to prevent the rotting of the hide.

The second operation is the removal of the hair from the hide, which is accomplished by the application of lime to the flesh side of the skin. The lime loosens the hair from the follicles, and the hair is then scraped from the hide with a curved knife that is called the fleshing knife. The hide is then washed again to remove the lime, and the hide is ready for the tanning proper.

The third operation is the splitting of the hide into layers, if the hide is thick enough to produce a grain layer and a suede layer. The splitting is conducted with a special knife that cuts the hide horizontally, and the split hides are tanned separately, with the grain layer producing the smooth leather that is used for the shoes and the belts and the suede layer producing the soft leather that is used for the gloves and the linings.

## The Tanning Proper

The tanning of the hide is the process by which the proteins of the skin are stabilised and the hide is made resistant to decay. The tanning is conducted in the pits that are filled with the tanning liquor, which is a solution of water and the tannins that are extracted from the bark of the oak and the other trees that contain the tannins. The hide is suspended in the tanning liquor for a period of weeks or months, depending on the thickness of the hide and the concentration of the liquor.

The tanner tests the hide at regular intervals to determine the progress of the tanning, and the hide is moved from one pit to another as the concentration of the tanning liquor is increased. The hide that has been fully tanned is then removed from the pits and dried in the air, and the dried leather is then oiled and softened by working it over the beam.

The quality of the leather that the tanner produces depends on the quality of the raw hide, the skill of the tanner in the preparation and the tanning, and the care that is taken in the finishing of the leather. The best leather is produced from the hides of the cattle of the plains, which are thick and strong and free from the defects that the hides of the lesser animals often bear. The leather of the goats and the sheep is thinner and more supple, and it is used for the making of the gloves and the garments that require a softer material.

## The Trade of the Currier

The currier is the craftsman who finishes the leather that the tanner has produced, adding the colour and the surface treatment that the leather requires for its intended use. The currier works in the workshop that is separate from the tanning yard, and the currier's trade is cleaner and more refined than the tanner's trade.

The currier begins by wetting the leather and stretching it on the frame, scraping the surface with the currier's knife to produce a smooth and even finish. The currier then applies the oil and the wax to the leather, working the oil into the fibres to make the leather soft and waterproof. The currier may also dye the leather, applying the colour with the brush and the sponge to produce the shade that the customer requires.

The currier's trade is a skilled occupation, and the currier who produces the finest leather is respected in the community for the quality of the work. The leather that the currier finishes is sold to the craftsmen who make the shoes and the belts and the saddles and the other articles of leather, and the reputation of the currier is the foundation of the success of the workshop.

## The Manufacture of Leather Goods

The making of leather goods is the third trade in the sequence of the leather industry, and it is practised in the workshops and the households of the towns and the cities. The shoemaker, the saddler, the belt maker, and the bag maker are the principal craftsmen of the leather goods trade, and each specialises in the production of the articles that the trade requires.

The shoemaker is the most numerous of the leather craftsmen, for every person in the known world requires shoes for the protection of the feet. The shoemaker measures the foot of the customer and cuts the leather to the pattern, stitching the pieces together with the waxed thread that the shoemaker prepares. The shoemaker also repairs the shoes that are worn out, and the workshop of the shoemaker is a familiar presence in every settlement of any size.

The saddler is the craftsman who makes the saddles and the harness for the horses and the other riding animals. The saddler's trade requires a knowledge of the anatomy of the horse and the distribution of the weight of the rider, and the saddle that is well made is a work of craftsmanship that can last for a generation of use. The saddler also makes the bridles and the reins and the girths and the stirrup leathers that complete the equipment of the horse.

## The Trade in Hides and Leather

The trade in raw hides and finished leather is a significant component of the livestock economy, connecting the slaughterhouses of the towns to the tanneries and the tanneries to the workshops of the leather craftsmen. The raw hide is a perishable commodity that must be salted or dried soon after the slaughter of the animal, and the trade in raw hides is conducted by the merchants who know the quality of the hides and the conditions in which the hides must be kept.

The finished leather is traded in the markets of the towns and the cities, where the craftsmen who make the leather goods purchase the materials that they need. The price of the leather varies with the quality, the thickness, and the finish, and the merchants who deal in leather maintain the stocks of the different types of leather that the craftsmen require.

The export of leather and leather goods from the regions where the livestock is abundant to the regions where the livestock is scarce is a source of profit for the merchants who undertake the trade. The leather of the Aethelaran plains is exported to the cities of Sahrana and Morvind, where the local supplies of leather are insufficient for the needs of the population. The leather goods of the Aethelaran workshops are also exported, and the saddles and the shoes and the bags that are made in the plains are found throughout the known world.

## The Leather of the Other Peoples

The dwarven peoples of the mountains are skilled in the working of leather, using the hides of the mountain goats and the sheep that are kept in the high pastures. The dwarven leather is noted for its durability and its resistance to the wear of the rocky terrain, and the dwarven shoemakers produce boots that are the most sought-after footwear in the mining regions.

The centaur kindreds of the plains produce leather from the hides of the cattle that they keep, and the centaur leather workers are skilled in the making of the saddles and the harness that the centaurs use for the carrying of goods. The salawa peoples of the desert produce leather from the hides of the goats that they keep, and the salawa leather is used for the water bags and the containers that the caravan trade requires.

The halfling communities of the valleys produce a soft and supple leather from the hides of the pigs that they keep in the woodlands, and the halfling leather is used for the making of the gloves and the fine garments that the halflings are known to produce. The leather of the halfling tanners is not as strong as the leather of the plains, but it is softer and more flexible, and it is valued by the craftsmen who require a supple material for the work that they do.
