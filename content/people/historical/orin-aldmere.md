## The Founder without a Personal Name

The scholar remembered as Orin Aldmere is held to have been the founder of the community from which the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading) descend, and the figure is the principal figure of the early history of the Unwritten Name. No personal name has been preserved; the doctrine has been content to honour the title without the name, holding that the work to which the figure gave the founding impulse is more worthy of remembrance than accidents of birth. The doctrine regards the absence of the name as a fitting first instance of the discipline of nouns, and students are taught to mark the absence as a sign of the master's own teaching.

The figure is placed by the doctrine in the period when older texts first began to circulate among the libraries of the river towns of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), active in a court of an early Aethelaran kingdom before withdrawing to found a small community of readers. The chronicles of the kingdom do not record the figure's name, and the chronicles of the cloister record only the title.

## Leaving the Royal Service for Solitude

Orin Aldmere is held to have served for some years in a court of an early Aethelaran kingdom, in an office whose duties included the comparison and copying of older texts that had begun to circulate among the libraries of the realm. The office required attendance at the reading of older texts at appointed hours, marking variations between copies, and reporting to the king on matters of doctrine and history. The office carried a small stipend and a degree of standing at the court; the holder was expected to maintain the books in good repair and to instruct junior scribes in the arts of reading and copying.

Orin Aldmere is held to have grown uneasy in the office as the comparison of older texts revealed contradictions in their accounts of the divine. The contradictions were not small, nor the sort that a careful copyist might resolve by collating manuscripts with diligence. They were of a kind that the figure came to regard as the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. After a season of further study, the figure resolved to withdraw from the court and devote the remainder of life to the patient examination of texts in the company of others who shared the same uneasiness.

The withdrawal is held to have been conducted without acrimony. The court permitted the figure to depart with the books copied during the years of service. The doctrine regards the manner of the withdrawal as a sign of the figure's own restraint: no public declaration of the reasons for departure was made, and offers extended by the court in hope of retaining the figure were refused. The court is said to have honoured the departure with a small pension and customary recognition, which the figure accepted with gratitude and without comment.

## A Small Estate and a Discipline of Reading

Orin Aldmere withdrew from the court to a small estate in the river country, joined by a handful of scholars who had served in the office of the court and who shared the figure's uneasiness about the contradictions of the older texts. The community was small, possessing no more than a single chamber set aside for the keeping of books and discussion of readings. The figure and the community devoted themselves to the comparison of older texts and to the long practice of reading that the figure had begun to formulate as a discipline, living upon the produce of the small estate and the labour of their own hands.

Orin Aldmere maintained, in the company of the community, that the contradictions of the older texts were not errors to be resolved by further study but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. The figure taught that every name for the divine is, in the moment of its utterance, a name for something less than the divine, and that the careful reader must therefore learn to use names without mistaking them for the thing named. The figure taught further that the discipline of reading was the chief instrument by which the reader could be brought to attend to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it, and counselled the community to undertake the long reading of a few chosen texts above the brief reading of many.

Orin Aldmere maintained the community for the remainder of the figure's life, instructing the most senior members in the practices of the long reading and the conference that were to become the central observances of the doctrine. In the final years of life, the figure composed a short treatise on the discipline of nouns that the doctrine has preserved as one of the foundational texts of the tradition, though the treatise is held to be only a fragment of the figure's teaching and not a complete account.

## One Figure or a Line of Masters?

The doctrine is content to leave open whether Orin Aldmere was a single figure or a succession of senior readers who held the title in turn, regarding the question as one of the lesser mysteries that the student will learn to leave aside. The doctrine's restraint upon the question is a deliberate part of its teaching: the title is a pointer toward a work and not a name for a person, and the doctrine is unwilling to confuse the work with the person. Students are taught that the truth of the doctrine does not depend upon the number of figures who have held the title and are counselled against seeking a definitive answer.

The accounts of the early cloisters preserve references to a single person called Orin Aldmere and references to a succession of senior readers, mingled without any attempt to reconcile them. The doctrine regards the mingling as a sign of fidelity to the figure's own teaching, for the figure is held to have taught that the names by which the tradition remembers its founders are themselves subject to the discipline of nouns and must be used with care. The doctrine has therefore preserved both kinds of reference and refrained from asserting either as the final account.

## How the Doctrine Spread Quietly

Orin Aldmere is held to have founded the small community that was the seed of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/religion/cloisters-of-reading), and the doctrine traces the customs of the cloisters — the library, the schedule of study, the long reading, and the conference of readers — to the practices established in the first community. The cloisters are modelled upon the first community in their plainness and devotion to the long reading, governed by the same discipline that the figure established.

The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of study in their home towns. The [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) tolerated the doctrine within its borders without ever adopting it, and the cloister readers came to be regarded by the courts as a valuable source of patient counsel in matters requiring study rather than action. Orin Aldmere set the example of the doctrine's manner of relation to the wider world: withdrawal from the court without public declaration and acceptance of customary recognition with gratitude and without comment.

## Honour in the Silence of the Cloisters

Orin Aldmere is remembered by the doctrine as the scholar who set the discipline of nouns at the centre of the long reading and who taught students to attend to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it. The doctrine regards the figure as the source of its patience, humility, and restraint. Masters of the cloisters teach the figure's treatise to novices as a foundation of the discipline. The treatise is preserved in the library of each cloister as one of the foundational texts of the tradition.

Public memory of the figure is preserved chiefly in the doctrine's own accounts; the figure is unknown to the wider world outside the cloisters. The doctrine regards this obscurity as a fitting consequence of the figure's own teaching, for the figure counselled the community against seeking the world's adherence and set the work of study above reputation. The figure's memory is honoured in the silence of the conference of readers and in the long reading of the chosen texts that the figure's instruction established.
