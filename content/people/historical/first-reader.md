## A Name That Is Not a Name

The scholar remembered only by the title of the First Reader is held to have been the founder of the community from which the [Reading Cloisters](/content/government/local/cloisters-of-reading) descend, and the figure is the principal figure of the early history of the [Unwritten Name](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/unwritten-name). No personal name has been preserved for the figure, and the doctrine has been content to honour the title without the name, holding that the work to which the figure gave the founding impulse is more worthy of remembrance than the figure's accidents of birth. The chronicler notes that the doctrine regards the absence of the name as a fitting first instance of the discipline of nouns, and the students of the doctrine are taught to mark the absence as a sign of the master's own teaching.

The figure is placed by the doctrine in the period in which the older texts first began to circulate among the libraries of the river towns of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the figure is held to have been active in a court of an early Aethelaran kingdom before withdrawing from the court to found the small community of readers. The chronicles of the kingdom do not record the figure's name, and the chronicles of the cloister record only the title, so the chronicler must rely upon the doctrine's own accounts and the inferences that may be drawn from the doctrine's later customs.

## The Withdrawal from the Court

The First Reader is held to have served for some years in a court of an early Aethelaran kingdom, in an office whose duties included the comparison and the copying of the older texts that had begun to circulate among the libraries of the realm. The office required its holder to attend the reading of the older texts at the appointed hours, to mark the variations between the copies, and to report to the king upon the matters of doctrine and of history that the texts contained. The chronicler records that the office carried with it a small stipend and a degree of standing at the court, and that the holder of the office was expected to maintain the books in good repair and to instruct the junior scribes in the arts of reading and of copying.

The First Reader is held to have grown uneasy in the office as the comparison of the older texts revealed to the figure the contradictions that the texts contained in their accounts of the divine. The contradictions were not small, and the contradictions were not the sort that a careful copyist might resolve by collating the manuscripts with diligence. The contradictions were of a kind that the figure came to regard as the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said, and the figure resolved, after a season of further study, to withdraw from the court and to devote the remainder of the figure's life to the patient examination of the texts in the company of others who shared the same uneasiness.

The withdrawal is held to have been conducted without acrimony, and the court is held to have permitted the figure to depart with the books that the figure had copied during the years of service. The doctrine regards the manner of the withdrawal as a sign of the figure's own restraint, for the figure is held to have made no public declaration of the reasons for the departure and to have refused the offers that the court extended in the hope of retaining the figure in the office. The chronicler notes that the court is said to have honoured the figure's departure with a small pension and a customary recognition, and that the figure accepted the recognition with gratitude and without comment.

## The First Community

The First Reader withdrew from the court to a small estate in the river country, and the figure was joined there by a handful of scholars who had served the figure in the office of the court and who shared the figure's uneasiness about the contradictions of the older texts. The community was small in number, and the community possessed no more than a single chamber that the figure set aside for the keeping of the books and the discussion of the readings. The figure and the community devoted themselves to the comparison of the older texts and to the long practice of reading that the figure had begun to formulate as a discipline, and the community lived upon the produce of the small estate and the labour of their own hands.

The First Reader is held to have maintained, in the company of the community, that the contradictions of the older texts were not errors to be resolved by further study but the necessary consequence of trying to say what cannot be said. The figure taught that every name for the divine is, in the moment of its utterance, a name for something less than the divine, and that the careful reader must therefore learn to use the names without mistaking them for the thing named. The figure taught further that the discipline of reading was the chief instrument by which the reader could be brought to attend to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it, and the figure counselled the community to undertake the long reading of a few chosen texts above the brief reading of many.

The First Reader is held to have maintained the community for the remainder of the figure's life, and the figure is held to have instructed the most senior of the community in the practices of the long reading and the conference that were to become the central observances of the doctrine. The figure is held to have composed, in the final years of the figure's life, a short treatise on the discipline of nouns that the doctrine has preserved as one of the foundational texts of the tradition, though the treatise is held to be only a fragment of the figure's teaching and not a complete account of the figure's thought.

## The Question of the Succession

The doctrine is content to leave open the question of whether the First Reader was a single figure or a succession of senior readers who held the title in turn, and the doctrine regards the question as one of the lesser mysteries that the student will learn to leave aside. The chronicler notes that the doctrine's restraint upon the question is a deliberate part of the doctrine's teaching, for the doctrine holds that the title is a pointer toward a work and not a name for a person, and the doctrine is unwilling to confuse the work with the person. The students of the doctrine are taught that the truth of the doctrine does not depend upon the number of figures who have held the title, and the students are counselled against the temptation to seek a definitive answer to the question.

The chronicler observes that the accounts of the early cloisters preserve references to a single First Reader and references to a succession of senior readers, and that the two kinds of references are mingled in the doctrine's records without any attempt to reconcile them. The doctrine regards the mingling as a sign of the doctrine's fidelity to the figure's own teaching, for the figure is held to have taught that the names by which the tradition remembers its founders are themselves subject to the discipline of nouns and must be used with care. The doctrine has therefore preserved both kinds of reference and has refrained from the assertion of either as the final account.

## The Cloister Tradition

The First Reader is held to have founded the small community that was the seed of the [Reading Cloisters](/content/government/local/cloisters-of-reading), and the doctrine traces the customs of the cloisters to the practices that the figure established in the first community. The cloister's library, the schedule of study, the long reading, and the conference of the readers are all held to descend from the figure's instruction, and the doctrine regards the figure as the source of the unity of the cloisters in their many regions. The chronicler notes that the cloisters are modelled upon the first community in their plainness and in their devotion to the long reading, and the cloisters are governed by the same discipline that the figure established in the founding community.

The doctrine spread quietly along the trade routes of the river country, carried by scholars who had been received into the reading houses and who kept the discipline of the study in their home towns. The [Vael](/content/government/national/vael) tolerated the doctrine in its borders without ever adopting it, and the cloister readers came to be regarded by the courts as a valuable source of patient counsel in matters that required study rather than action. The First Reader is held to have set the example of the doctrine's manner of relation to the wider world, for the figure withdrew from the court without making a public declaration of the reasons for the withdrawal and the figure accepted the court's customary recognition with gratitude and without comment.

## Legacy and Memory

The First Reader is remembered by the doctrine as the scholar who set the discipline of nouns at the centre of the long reading and who taught the students of the doctrine to attend to the difference between naming a thing and pointing at it. The doctrine regards the figure as the source of its patience, its humility, and its restraint, and the masters of the cloisters teach the figure's treatise to the novices as a foundation of the discipline. The figure is held to have composed the treatise in the final years of the figure's life, and the treatise is preserved in the library of each cloister as one of the foundational texts of the tradition.

The chronicler notes that the public memory of the figure is preserved chiefly in the doctrine's own accounts, and that the figure is unknown to the wider world outside the cloisters. The doctrine regards the obscurity of the figure as a fitting consequence of the figure's own teaching, for the figure is held to have counselled the community against the temptation to seek the world's adherence and to have set the work of the study above the reputation that the world might bestow. The chronicler finds in the obscurity a measure of the doctrine's fidelity to its own first teaching, and the chronicler records that the figure's memory is honoured in the silence of the conference of readers and in the long reading of the chosen texts that the figure's instruction established.
