## A Figure of the Hill Workshops

The personage remembered only as the First Smelter is held to have been the founder of the hill smiths' tradition from which the [Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/forged-flame) descends, and the figure is the principal figure of the early history of the faith. No name has been preserved for the figure, and the oral traditions of the smiths record only the title, for the smiths have always been more careful of their craft than of the fame of the individuals who have practised it. The chronicler notes that the tradition regards the absence of the name as a sign of the smiths' proper humility, and the elders of the tradition teach the apprentices to mark the absence as a sign of the figure's own devotion to the work of the forge.

The figure is placed by the oral traditions in the upper valleys of the northern reaches of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), though the precise location is disputed among the elders, and the figure's workshop is held to have been the first in which the fire was kindled with intention and with care. The chronicler records that the tradition is content to leave the question of the location open, and the chronicler notes that the figure's memory is honoured in the workshops of the hill country rather than in any single shrine or monument.

## The Work of the Forge

The First Smelter is held to have been a smith of unusual patience and unusual attentiveness, and the figure is said to have tended the fires of the workshop with a care that the other smiths of the time did not practise. The smiths of the hill country were accustomed to kindle their fires in the customary manner of the trade, and the smiths relied upon the prevailing winds and the readiness of the fuel to bring the fire to the heat required for the work. The First Smelter is held to have introduced the practice of kindling the fire with deliberate intention, and the figure is said to have spoken a brief acknowledgment to the flame as the figure set the kindling to the tinder.

The figure is held to have maintained the fire through every working day with a discipline that the other smiths of the hill country came to regard as a worthy example. The figure inspected the bellows, the anvil, and the quenching trough before the first piece was set upon the anvil, and the figure took care to bank the fire at the proper times of day and to renew the fuel at the proper intervals. The tradition teaches that the figure's discipline was not a matter of mere habit but a matter of the figure's conviction that the fire was itself a worthy object of attention, and the figure held that the smith who attended to the fire with care was attending to something greater than the convenience of the work.

The First Smelter is held to have instructed the apprentices of the workshop in the discipline of the fire, and the figure is held to have taught that the fire upon the forge was the same fire that moved in the heart of every craftsman and the same fire that held the world in being. The figure taught the apprentices to greet one another with a phrase that meant may the metal yield, and the figure taught the apprentices to reply may the hand be steady. The figure is held to have composed, in the later years of the figure's life, a short set of instructions for the kindling and the tending of the forge fire that the tradition has preserved as one of the foundational texts of the practice.

## The Hill Smiths

The First Smelter is held to have been the principal figure among a small community of hill smiths who worked the remote workshops of the upper valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), and the figure is held to have set the customs that the smiths of the community observed in common. The workshops of the hill country were small establishments, scattered across the upper valleys at distances that made a common counsel among the smiths a matter of considerable difficulty, and the smiths relied upon the visits of the elders of the community to maintain the unity of the practice. The figure is held to have been one of the elders of the community, and the figure is held to have travelled between the workshops to instruct the apprentices and to supervise the keeping of the customs.

The figure's reputation is held to have spread among the hill smiths of the surrounding valleys, and the apprentices of the neighbouring workshops are said to have sought out the figure's workshop to learn the discipline of the fire. The figure is held to have received the apprentices with patience and to have instructed them in the customs without fee, and the figure is held to have maintained the workshop's store of fuel and of iron against the needs of the apprentices who came from a distance. The tradition teaches that the figure's hospitality was a sign of the figure's conviction that the practice of the forge was a common inheritance and not a private possession, and the elders of the tradition teach the apprentices to mark the hospitality as a sign of the figure's own devotion to the work.

The oral traditions of the smiths preserve accounts of the figure's manner in the workshop, and the accounts describe the figure as a smith of few words and steady attention. The figure is held to have spoken seldom and to have listened often, and the figure is held to have corrected the apprentices' errors by the example of the figure's own work rather than by lengthy instruction. The chronicler notes that the tradition has preserved the figure's reputation for patience as one of the most valuable of the figure's legacies to the practice, and the elders teach the apprentices to mark the patience as the source of the figure's proficiency in the craft.

## The Question of the Succession

The tradition is content to leave open the question of whether the First Smelter was a single smith or a succession of master smiths who held the title in turn, and the tradition regards the question as one of the lesser matters that the apprentice will learn to leave aside. The chronicler notes that the elders of the tradition are divided upon the question, and the chronicler notes that the division is held among the elders as a sign of the tradition's proper humility before the figure's memory. The tradition teaches that the truth of the figure's teaching does not depend upon the number of smiths who have held the title, and the tradition counsels the apprentices against the temptation to seek a definitive answer to the question.

The chronicler observes that the oral traditions of the hill smiths preserve references to a single First Smelter and references to a succession of master smiths, and that the two kinds of references are mingled in the tradition's accounts without any attempt to reconcile them. The tradition regards the mingling as a sign of the tradition's fidelity to the figure's own teaching, for the figure is held to have taught that the names by which the smiths remember their forebears are themselves subject to the discipline of the craft and must be used with care. The tradition has therefore preserved both kinds of reference and has refrained from the assertion of either as the final account.

## The Spread of the Practice

The First Smelter is held to have set the customs that the [Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/forged-flame) has preserved in the workshops of the hill country and in the lowland forges to which the practice has spread. The oral traditions of the smiths preserve the figure's instructions for the kindling of the fire and the tending of the forge, and the elders of the tradition teach the apprentices to mark the instructions as the foundation of the unity of the practice across the scattered workshops. The figure's reputation spread from the hill country into the lowland towns and into the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), where the practice found a sympathetic hearing among the [Dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain valleys.

The tradition spread quietly along the trade routes of the hill country, carried by smiths who had been received into the workshops of the upper valleys and who kept the discipline of the fire in the forges to which they travelled. The elders of the tradition teach the apprentices to mark the manner of the spread as a sign of the practice's fidelity to the figure's own example, for the figure is held to have instructed the apprentices without fee and to have maintained the workshop's store against the needs of the apprentices who came from a distance. The chronicler notes that the [Dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) did not adopt the faith whole, for they hold to their own ancient traditions of hearth and stone, but they acknowledged a kinship with the Forged Flame and permitted the keeping of small forges dedicated to the Forger in certain workshops.

## Legacy and Memory

The First Smelter is remembered by the tradition as the smith who set the discipline of the fire at the centre of the practice of the forge, and the figure is honoured in the workshop of every smith who observes the customs that the figure established. The elders of the tradition teach the figure's instructions for the kindling of the fire to the apprentices as a foundation of the practice, and the elders hold the figure up as an example of the patience and the attentiveness that the practice requires. The chronicler notes that the figure's memory is preserved chiefly in the oral traditions of the smiths, and that the figure is unknown to the wider world outside the workshops.

The tradition regards the obscurity of the figure as a fitting consequence of the figure's own devotion to the work of the forge, for the figure is held to have counselled the apprentices against the temptation to seek the world's attention and to have set the work of the practice above the reputation that the world might bestow. The chronicler finds in the obscurity a measure of the tradition's fidelity to its own first teaching, and the chronicler records that the figure's memory is honoured in the steady return of the flame each working day and in the customary greeting that the smiths exchange as they take up the work of the forge.
