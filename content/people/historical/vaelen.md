## Overview

Vaelen is the most revered of the ancient scholars of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), the compiler of the first comprehensive natural history of the known world and the founder of the method of systematic observation that the natural sciences of the present age still employ. His great work, the [Known-World Codex](/content/magic/relic/codex-of-the-known-world), was completed after more than three decades of travel and study, and it remains the single most influential work of natural philosophy in the history of the continents. The chronicles of Vaelen's life are better preserved than those of most figures of his age, for his own writings contain many references to his travels and his methods, and the institutions that his work inspired have maintained his records with scrupulous care. He was born in the city of [Eldreth](/content/location/settlement/city/eldreth) in the latter half of the second age, and his lifespan of one hundred and four years was devoted with singular purpose to the observation and the classification of the natural world.

## The Great Chronicle

The Known-World Codex, the work upon which Vaelen's reputation rests, was begun in his thirty-fifth year and completed in his sixty-eighth, a span of thirty-three years during which he travelled to every continent and every region of the known world that was accessible to a solitary scholar of modest means. The Codex is divided into seven books, each treating a different aspect of the natural order: the first book describes the geography and the climate of the continents, the second the flora of the known world, the third the fauna, the fourth the sapient peoples, the fifth the minerals and the metals, the sixth the waters and the currents, and the seventh the heavens and the measurement of time.

The method that Vaelen employed in the compilation of the Codex was unprecedented in its rigour. He recorded only those observations that he had made himself or that had been reported to him by witnesses whose reliability he had assessed, and he distinguished clearly in his text between what he had seen with his own eyes and what he had been told by others. He preserved the local names for each species, each place, and each phenomenon in the languages of the peoples who used them, and he appended to his descriptions the measurements and the drawings that would allow a reader in a distant land to identify the subject of the passage.

The book on the sapient peoples of the known world is the most remarkable of the seven, for Vaelen approached the study of the thinking species with an impartiality that was rare in his age, describing the customs, the languages, and the physiologies of each people without the judgements of superiority that coloured the writings of his contemporaries. He recorded the physical characteristics of the [elves](/content/species/sapient/elves), the [dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves), the [orcs](/content/species/sapient/orcs), the [goblins](/content/species/sapient/goblins), the [halflings](/content/species/sapient/halflings), and the [gnomes](/content/species/sapient/gnomes) of the northern continents, and he drew comparisons between the northern peoples and the [tortolans](/content/species/sapient/tortolans), the [felinids](/content/species/sapient/felinids), the [lupines](/content/species/sapient/lupines), the [corvids](/content/species/sapient/corvids), and the [noctules](/content/species/sapient/noctules) of the southern land. The work is the first attempt in the known world to describe the full range of sapient life in a single systematic account, and it is the foundation upon which all later ethnological study has been built.

The book on the geography of the continents contains the earliest surviving description of the three great landmasses in their relation to one another, and Vaelen's account of the currents of the [Auric Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/auric-ocean) and the [Greyring Ocean](/content/location/natural/feature/greyring-ocean) was the first to recognise that the movement of water in the seas followed patterns that could be predicted and charted. The scholars of the later age have confirmed the accuracy of Vaelen's observations in nearly every particular, and the maps that were drawn from his descriptions remained the standard reference for more than two centuries after his death.

The greatest difficulty that Vaelen faced in the compilation of the Codex was the sheer scale of the task, for the known world of his age was far larger and more diverse than the chroniclers of the previous century had supposed, and every journey that he undertook revealed new species, new peoples, and new phenomena that demanded inclusion. The chronicles record that he revised the text of the Codex three times before he was satisfied with its completeness, and that the final revision required the copying of the entire work by a team of scribes who worked under his supervision for a period of five years.

## Scholarship

Vaelen was not merely a compiler of observations but a theorist of the natural order, and the Codex contains the first systematic attempt to classify the living things of the known world according to their shared characteristics. The system that he developed grouped organisms by the structure of their bodies, the manner of their reproduction, and the environments in which they were found, and the categories that he established have been refined by the naturalists of the later age but not fundamentally altered. The naturalists of the present age still use the Vaelenic classification, and the names that he assigned to the major groups of plants and animals are the standard terms of the natural sciences.

The High Chronicler was also the first scholar to recognise the relationship between the climate of a region and the forms of life that were found there, observing that the forests of the temperate latitudes of Aethelar supported a different assemblage of species from the arid interior of [Sahrana](/content/location/natural/continent/sahrana) or the mountainous highlands of [Morvind](/content/location/natural/continent/morvind). His observations on the distribution of species across the continents anticipated the later science of biogeography, and the principles that he laid down in the Codex are still taught in the academies of the present age.

Vaelen established the library of Eldreth as the principal repository of his work and the centre of the natural sciences in the known world. The library that he founded houses the original manuscript of the Codex, together with the journals, the drawings, and the specimens that he collected during his travels, and it has been maintained by a succession of scholars who have continued his work of observation and classification. The library is the most important institution of natural philosophy on the continent, and the scholars who work there are the custodians of the Vaelenic tradition.

The influence of Vaelen's scholarship extended beyond the natural sciences into the fields of history and geography, for the Codex contains the earliest systematic description of the political boundaries of the known world, including the [Aldreth](/content/government/national/aldreth), the [Vael](/content/government/national/vael), the [Amurra Republic](/content/government/national/amurra-republic), the [Auric Isles](/content/government/national/auric-isles), the [Kharesh Tribes](/content/government/national/kharesh-tribes), the [Plains Confederation](/content/government/national/plains-confederation), and the other realms and leagues that divided the continents among them. The account of the governments and the peoples of the known world that Vaelen included in his work is the most comprehensive that has survived from the age, and it is the primary source for the study of the political history of the period.

## Influence

The influence of Vaelen on the intellectual life of the known world is difficult to overstate. The Known-World Codex was copied and recopied in every centre of learning, and the copies that were carried to the libraries of the [Thessaran League](/content/government/national/thessaran-league), the [Vashari Pact](/content/government/national/vashari-pact), the [Karhold Realm](/content/government/national/karhold-realm), and the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) became the foundation of the natural sciences in those regions. The method of systematic observation that Vaelen established was adopted by the scholars of every continent, and the principles of careful recording and clear distinction between observation and inference that he laid down became the standard of the natural sciences.

The schools of natural philosophy that Vaelen inspired have produced generations of scholars who have extended and refined his work, and the discipline that he founded is still the most respected of the intellectual pursuits in the academies of the known world. The naturalists of the present age are trained in the Vaelenic method, and they hold the Codex as the foundational text of their science, a work of such completeness and accuracy that it commands the respect of every school.

The High Chronicler died in the library of Eldreth in the one hundred and fourth year of his life, and his body was interred in the courtyard of the library that he had founded, beneath a simple stone that bears only his name and the title of his great work. The library that he established has been maintained without interruption since his death, and the scholars who work there are the direct heirs of his tradition, carrying forward the work of observation, classification, and understanding that he began more than a millennium ago. The chronicler notes that the name of Vaelen is spoken with reverence in every academy of the known world, and the Known-World Codex is the book that every scholar of the natural sciences must know, for it is the foundation upon which the entire edifice of the natural philosophy of the age is built.
