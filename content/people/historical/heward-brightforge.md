## A Figure of the Hill Workshops

The personage remembered as Heward Brightforge is held to have been the founder of the hill smiths' tradition from which the [Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/forged-flame) descends. No personal name has been preserved; the oral traditions of the smiths record only the title, for the smiths have always been more careful of their craft than of the fame of individuals who have practised it. The tradition regards the absence of the name as a sign of the smiths' proper humility, and elders teach apprentices to mark the absence as a sign of the figure's own devotion to the work of the forge.

The figure is placed by the oral traditions in the upper valleys of the northern reaches of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), though the precise location is disputed among the elders. The figure's workshop is held to have been the first in which the fire was kindled with intention and with care. The tradition is content to leave the question of the location open; the figure's memory is honoured in the workshops of the hill country rather than in any single shrine or monument.

## The Work of the Forge

Heward Brightforge is held to have been a smith of unusual patience and attentiveness, said to have tended the fires of the workshop with a care that other smiths of the time did not practise. The smiths of the hill country were accustomed to kindle their fires in the customary manner, relying upon the prevailing winds and the readiness of the fuel to bring the fire to the required heat. Heward Brightforge is held to have introduced the practice of kindling the fire with deliberate intention, speaking a brief acknowledgment to the flame as the kindling was set to the tinder.

The figure is held to have maintained the fire through every working day with a discipline that other smiths came to regard as a worthy example. The bellows, the anvil, and the quenching trough were inspected before the first piece was set upon the anvil. The fire was banked at the proper times of day and fuel renewed at the proper intervals. The tradition teaches that this discipline was not mere habit but a conviction that the fire was itself a worthy object of attention; the smith who attended to the fire with care was attending to something greater than the convenience of the work.

Heward Brightforge is held to have instructed apprentices in the discipline of the fire, teaching that the fire upon the forge was the same fire that moved in the heart of every craftsman and the same fire that held the world in being. The figure taught the apprentices to greet one another with a phrase meaning may the metal yield, and to reply may the hand be steady. In later years, the figure composed a short set of instructions for the kindling and tending of the forge fire that the tradition has preserved as one of the foundational texts of the practice.

## The Hill Smiths

Heward Brightforge is held to have been the principal figure among a small community of hill smiths who worked the remote workshops of the upper valleys of [Aethelar](/content/location/natural/continent/aethelar), setting the customs that the smiths of the community observed in common. The workshops of the hill country were small establishments scattered across the upper valleys at distances that made common counsel a matter of considerable difficulty. The smiths relied upon visits of the elders to maintain the unity of the practice. The figure is held to have been one of these elders, travelling between workshops to instruct apprentices and supervise the keeping of the customs.

The figure's reputation spread among the hill smiths of the surrounding valleys, and apprentices from neighbouring workshops sought out the figure to learn the discipline of the fire. The figure is held to have received apprentices with patience, instructing them without fee, and to have maintained the workshop's store of fuel and iron against the needs of those who came from a distance. The tradition teaches that this hospitality was a sign of the conviction that the practice of the forge was a common inheritance and not a private possession.

The oral traditions of the smiths describe the figure as a smith of few words and steady attention, speaking seldom and listening often, correcting apprentices' errors by the example of the figure's own work rather than by lengthy instruction. The tradition has preserved the figure's reputation for patience as one of the most valuable legacies to the practice.

## The Question of the Succession

The tradition is content to leave open whether Heward Brightforge was a single smith or a succession of master smiths who held the title in turn, regarding the question as one of the lesser matters that the apprentice will learn to leave aside. The elders of the tradition are divided upon the question, and the division is held as a sign of the tradition's proper humility before the figure's memory. The truth of the figure's teaching does not depend upon the number of smiths who have held the title.

The oral traditions of the hill smiths preserve references to a single person called Heward Brightforge and references to a succession of master smiths, mingled without any attempt to reconcile them. The tradition regards this mingling as a sign of fidelity to the figure's own teaching, for the figure is held to have taught that names by which smiths remember their forebears are themselves subject to the discipline of the craft and must be used with care. The tradition has therefore preserved both kinds of reference and refrained from asserting either as the final account.

## The Spread of the Practice

Heward Brightforge is held to have set the customs that the [Forged Flame](/content/belief/religion/monotheist/forged-flame) has preserved in the workshops of the hill country and in the lowland forges to which the practice spread. The oral traditions preserve the figure's instructions for kindling the fire and tending the forge, and the elders teach apprentices to mark these instructions as the foundation of the unity of the practice across scattered workshops. The figure's reputation spread from the hill country into lowland towns and into the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds), where the practice found a sympathetic hearing among the [Dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the mountain valleys.

The tradition spread quietly along the trade routes of the hill country, carried by smiths who had been received into the workshops of the upper valleys and who kept the discipline of the fire in the forges to which they travelled. The elders teach apprentices to mark the manner of this spread as a sign of the practice's fidelity to the figure's own example, for the figure is held to have instructed apprentices without fee and to have maintained the workshop's store against the needs of those who came from a distance. The [Dwarves](/content/species/sapient/dwarves) of the [Stoneheim Holds](/content/government/national/stoneheim-holds) did not adopt the faith whole, for they hold to their own ancient traditions of hearth and stone, but they acknowledged a kinship with the Forged Flame and permitted the keeping of small forges dedicated to the Forger in certain workshops.

## Legacy and Memory

Heward Brightforge is remembered by the tradition as the smith who set the discipline of the fire at the centre of the practice of the forge, honoured in the workshop of every smith who observes the customs established. The elders teach the figure's instructions for kindling the fire to apprentices as a foundation of the practice, holding the figure up as an example of the patience and attentiveness that the practice requires. The figure's memory is preserved chiefly in the oral traditions of the smiths; the figure is unknown to the wider world outside the workshops.

The tradition regards the obscurity of the figure as a fitting consequence of devotion to the work of the forge, for the figure is held to have counselled apprentices against seeking the world's attention and to have set the work of the practice above reputation. The figure's memory is honoured in the steady return of the flame each working day and in the customary greeting that the smiths exchange as they take up the work of the forge.
